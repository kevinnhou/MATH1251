MATH1251 has two strands. Algebra develops linear structure: linear
combinations, linear maps, coordinates and eigenvalues. Calculus studies
change, approximation and accumulation. The two strands keep coming back to a
few shared ideas, which this page gathers in one place.

## The two strands

- [Algebra](/algebra): complex numbers, vector spaces, linear transformations
  and eigenvalues.
- [Calculus](/calculus): integration techniques, ordinary differential
  equations, Taylor series, functions of several variables and double
  integrals.

## Shared ideas

### Coordinates and linear structure

A [basis](/algebra/vector-spaces/basis-and-dimension) gives every vector a
unique list of coordinates, whether the vector is an arrow in space, a
polynomial or an element of another finite-dimensional function space. Once
bases are chosen, a [linear transformation has a
matrix](/algebra/linear-transformations/matrix-representations). If an
operator $V\to V$ has an [eigenbasis](/algebra/eigenvalues/diagonalisation),
its matrix in that basis is diagonal, so it scales each basis direction
independently.

Coordinates describe a vector; they are not the vector. Changing the basis
changes the coordinate column and the matrix, but not the underlying vector or
map.

The scalars are part of the structure. The notes work over $\mathbb R$ by
default and over $\mathbb C$ when complex roots or eigenvectors are needed, so
the same formula can describe different vector spaces.

### Kernels and solution sets

The [kernel](/algebra/linear-transformations/kernel-image-rank) of a linear
map $T$ contains the inputs that $T$ sends to zero. If $T(x)=b$ has one
particular solution $x_p$, then every solution has the form

$$
x=x_p+x_h,\qquad x_h\in\ker T.
$$

This one pattern describes the free directions in a [matrix
equation](/algebra/linear-transformations/rank-nullity) and the homogeneous
part of a [linear differential
equation](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/existence-uniqueness-and-linear-operators).

### Roots, modes and evolution

A mode is a simple solution whose shape the evolution preserves: $r^n$ in
discrete time and $e^{rt}$ in continuous time. The values of $r$ are
[characteristic roots](/algebra/complex-numbers/stability-of-systems) and
[polar form](/algebra/complex-numbers/polar-form) reads each one as a scaling
and a rotation.

- In discrete time, $|r|$ controls growth or decay.
- In continuous time, $\operatorname{Re}(r)$ controls growth or decay.
- A nonzero imaginary part produces oscillation.

[Scalar constant-coefficient
ODEs](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/second-order-constant-coefficient-equations)
build their solutions from these roots. In [coupled linear
ODEs](/algebra/eigenvalues/linear-ode-systems),
[eigenvectors](/algebra/eigenvalues/eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors) give the
directions that evolve as single exponential modes. [Matrix
powers](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications) and [Markov
systems](/algebra/eigenvalues/markov-systems) are the discrete-time versions
of the same calculation.

### Local approximation

An approximation replaces a hard object with a simpler one and keeps track of
the difference:

$$
\text{original object}=\text{simple model}+\text{error}.
$$

A [Taylor polynomial](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-polynomials) matches
derivatives at a centre and the
[remainder](/calculus/taylor-series/taylors-theorem) measures the error away
from that centre. An infinite Taylor series needs a separate convergence
argument: matching every derivative does not by itself show that the series
equals the function.

In several variables, the [tangent
plane](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/geometry-and-approximation) is
the first-order model. The [second-order
model](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/multivariable-approximation)
adds curvature through the Hessian, which can classify interior [stationary
points](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/local-extrema). These models
live in the polynomial spaces from Algebra, where
[interpolation](/algebra/vector-spaces/data-fitting) instead fixes a
polynomial by its values at distinct inputs.

### Accumulation and changes of variables

[Integration techniques](/calculus/integration-techniques) use the form of an
integrand to find an antiderivative and they return as tools for [solving
differential equations](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations).
[Double integrals](/calculus/double-integrals) accumulate a quantity over a
planar region. Slicing reduces them to iterated single integrals and a [change
of variables](/calculus/double-integrals/change-of-variables-and-polar-coordinates)
can simplify the region or the integrand, with the Jacobian determinant
accounting for local area scaling. It plays the same role as choosing a good
basis: a better description makes the calculation simpler.
