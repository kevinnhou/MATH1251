This page turns a double integral over a rectangle into two one-variable
integrals. The [Riemann-sum
definition](/calculus/double-integrals/double-integrals-and-riemann-sums) says
what the integral means but is not a practical way to evaluate it. Instead,
fix one coordinate, integrate across the resulting slice and then add the
slices with a second integral.

When slice endpoints depend on the outer variable, [regions bounded by
curves](/calculus/double-integrals/regions-and-order-of-integration) extend the
method. A [change of
variables](/calculus/double-integrals/change-of-variables-and-polar-coordinates)
reshapes slices that remain awkward.

<Recall of="double-integral-over-rectangle" />

## Add the slices

Let

$$
R=[a,b]\times[c,d].
$$

Fixing $x=x_0$ leaves a slice in the $y$-direction. Its signed cross-sectional
area is the one-variable integral

$$
C_{\mathrm{vert}}(x_0)=\int_c^d f(x_0,y)\,dy,
$$

provided this integral exists. Adding these areas as $x$ varies gives the
double integral. Fixing $y=y_0$ instead gives slices in the other direction.

<Definition slug="double-integral-cross-section">

### Cross-section integral

<Statement>

For a function $f$ on $R=[a,b]\times[c,d]$, when the one-variable integrals
exist, the vertical and horizontal cross-section integrals are

$$
C_{\mathrm{vert}}(x)=\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy,
\qquad
C_{\mathrm{horiz}}(y)=\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx.
$$

They measure the signed areas of slices obtained by fixing $x$ or $y$.

</Statement>

When $f\ge0$, these are ordinary cross-sectional areas. When $f$ takes both
signs, negative parts are subtracted.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="fubini-double-integral" of="double-integral-over-rectangle,double-integral-cross-section">

### Fubini's theorem on a rectangle

<Statement>

If $f$ is continuous on

$$
R=[a,b]\times[c,d],
$$

then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA
&=\int_a^b\left(\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx\\
&=\int_c^d\left(\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\right)dy.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Statement>

The inner integral computes one cross-section and the outer integral adds
the cross-sections. In

$$
\int_a^b\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx,
$$

the $dy$ says that $y$ is integrated first and the $dx$ says that $x$ is
integrated second. Limits must belong to the variable whose differential
they accompany.

The iterated formula remains valid for a bounded piecewise-continuous $f$
whose discontinuities lie on finitely many piecewise-smooth curves. This
includes zero extensions whose only discontinuities lie on the region
boundary.

Unbounded or improper integrals need stronger safeguards. If $f$ is
measurable and $\iint_R|f|\,dA<\infty$, Fubini's theorem gives the two
iterated integrals and permits their order to be exchanged. If $f\ge0$ is
measurable, Tonelli's theorem gives the same equality with values in
$[0,\infty]$, even when the integral is infinite. Similarly, if nonnegative
functions increase pointwise to a limit, monotone convergence justifies
passing the limit through the integral, as in the [Basel
sum](/calculus/double-integrals/basel-sum).

An improper calculation should therefore first use bounded truncations (or a
nonnegative convergence theorem). It must not subtract two separately
divergent integrals.

</Theorem>

<Method slug="choose-double-integral-order" of="fubini-double-integral">

### Choose an order of integration

<Statement>

To compute a double integral over a rectangle:

1. inspect the integrand for a direct antiderivative in $x$ or $y$;
2. write the corresponding inner integral first;
3. evaluate the inner integral while treating the other variable as constant;
4. evaluate the remaining one-variable integral;
5. use the other order as a check when it is comparably simple.

</Statement>

Under Fubini's hypotheses the two orders give the same value, but they need
not be equally convenient.

</Method>

<Example slug="double-integral-both-orders" of="choose-double-integral-order" difficulty="routine">

### Computing in both orders

Let

$$
R=[0,1]\times[0,2],
\qquad
f(x,y)=xy^2.
$$

Integrating with respect to $x$ first gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_R xy^2\,dA
&=\int_0^2\int_0^1xy^2\,dx\,dy\\
&=\int_0^2\left[\frac{x^2y^2}{2}\right]_{0}^{1}dy\\
&=\int_0^2\frac{y^2}{2}\,dy
=\frac43.
\end{aligned}
$$

Integrating with respect to $y$ first gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_R xy^2\,dA
&=\int_0^1\int_0^2xy^2\,dy\,dx\\
&=\int_0^1\left[\frac{xy^3}{3}\right]_{0}^{2}dx\\
&=\int_0^1\frac{8x}{3}\,dx
=\frac43.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="double-integral-useful-order" of="choose-double-integral-order" difficulty="exam">

### Choosing the useful order

Consider

$$
\iint_R xe^{xy}\,dA,
\qquad
R=[2,4]\times[1,3].
$$

Integrating in $y$ first uses the factor $x$ already present:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_R xe^{xy}\,dA
&=\int_2^4\int_1^3xe^{xy}\,dy\,dx\\
&=\int_2^4\left[e^{xy}\right]_{1}^{3}dx\\
&=\int_2^4(e^{3x}-e^x)\,dx\\
&=\left[\frac{e^{3x}}3-e^x\right]_2^4\\
&=\frac{e^{12}}3-e^4-\frac{e^6}{3}+e^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The reverse order leaves $xe^{xy}$ as the inner integrand in $x$, which
needs integration by parts.

</Example>

## Products separate over rectangles

When the integrand is a function of $x$ times a function of $y$, the double
integral over a rectangle is the product of two single integrals.

<Proposition slug="double-integral-product-separation" of="fubini-double-integral">

### Product separation

<Statement>

If $g$ is integrable on $[a,b]$ and $h$ is integrable on $[c,d]$, then
$f(x,y)=g(x)h(y)$ is integrable on $R=[a,b]\times[c,d]$ and

$$
\iint_R g(x)h(y)\,dA
=
\left(\int_a^b g(x)\,dx\right)
\left(\int_c^d h(y)\,dy\right).
$$

</Statement>

The factor that does not involve the inner variable pulls out of the inner
integral.

</Proposition>

<Example slug="double-integral-product-example" of="double-integral-product-separation" difficulty="routine">

### A separated product

For $R=[-1,1]\times[0,\pi/4]$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_R x^2\sin y\,dA
&=\left(\int_{-1}^{1}x^2\,dx\right)
\left(\int_0^{\pi/4}\sin y\,dy\right)\\
&=\frac23\left(1-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

</Example>

## Why the hypotheses matter

Fubini's theorem is about a genuine double integral. It does not say that two
improper iterated integrals must agree.

<Example slug="fubini-singular-counterexample" of="fubini-double-integral" difficulty="challenge">

### Different improper iterated integrals

Define on $[0,1]^2$

$$
f(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac1{x^2},&0<y\le x\le1,\\[6pt]
-\dfrac1{y^2},&0<x<y\le1,\\[6pt]
0,&x=0\text{ or }y=0.
\end{cases}
$$

The values on the axes remove the undefined endpoint without changing any
integral. For fixed $x>0$, split the improper $y$-integral at $y=x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 f(x,y)\,dy
&=\int_0^x\frac1{x^2}\,dy
\;+\int_x^1-\frac1{y^2}\,dy\\
&=\frac1x+\left(1-\frac1x\right)
=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus one iterated integral gives

$$
\int_0^1\int_0^1f(x,y)\,dy\,dx=1.
$$

For fixed $y>0$, split the improper $x$-integral at $x=y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1f(x,y)\,dx
&=\int_0^y-\frac1{y^2}\,dx
\;+\int_y^1\frac1{x^2}\,dx\\
&=-\frac1y+\left(\frac1y-1\right)
=-1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The other order gives

$$
\int_0^1\int_0^1f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=-1.
$$

There is no contradiction. The positive and negative parts each have
infinite integral near the origin, so $f$ is not integrable over the square
in the sense Fubini requires. The two calculations are different improper
cancellations, not two values of one double integral.

</Example>
