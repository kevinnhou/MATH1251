This page defines the double integral over a rectangle as a limit of Riemann
sums. The construction copies the one-variable integral: small intervals
become small rectangles and their lengths become areas.

The definition says what the integral means; later pages compute it.
[Fubini's theorem](/calculus/double-integrals/fubini-and-iterated-integrals)
replaces the sums by iterated integrals, [regions bounded by
curves](/calculus/double-integrals/regions-and-order-of-integration) extend the
construction beyond rectangles and a [change of
variables](/calculus/double-integrals/change-of-variables-and-polar-coordinates)
reshapes the iterated integrals.

## From one variable to two

For a one-variable partition

$$
a=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_m=b,
$$

the $i$th subinterval has length $\Delta x_i=x_i-x_{i-1}$. Choosing a tag
$x_i^*$ in each interval produces

$$
\sum_{i=1}^m f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x_i.
$$

The integral is the limit of these sums as the partition becomes fine,
provided the limit does not depend on the tags. Over a rectangle

$$
R=[a,b]\times[c,d],
$$

we partition both coordinates:

$$
a=x_0<\cdots<x_m=b,
\qquad
c=y_0<\cdots<y_n=d.
$$

The resulting small rectangles are

$$
R_{ij}=[x_{i-1},x_i]\times[y_{j-1},y_j],
\qquad
\Delta A_{ij}=\Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j.
$$

The mesh of this product partition is the largest side length among the small
rectangles:

$$
\lVert P\rVert=\max\left\{\max_i\Delta x_i,\ \max_j\Delta y_j\right\}.
$$

“The partition becomes fine” means that $\lVert P\rVert\to0$.

<Definition slug="double-riemann-sum">

### Double Riemann sum

<Statement>

For a partition of $R$ into rectangles $R_{ij}$ and tag points
$(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\in R_{ij}$, the corresponding double Riemann sum is

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A_{ij}.
$$

</Statement>

When $f\ge 0$, each term approximates the volume of a box above $R_{ij}$.
When $f$ takes both signs, the sum approximates a signed volume: contributions
below the $xy$-plane are subtracted.

</Definition>

<Definition slug="double-integral-over-rectangle" of="double-riemann-sum">

### Double integral over a rectangle

<Statement>

Let $f$ be bounded on $R=[a,b]\times[c,d]$. If there is a number $I$ such
that, for every $\varepsilon>0$, some $\delta>0$ makes every tagged
partition with $\lVert P\rVert<\delta$ satisfy

$$
\left|
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A_{ij}-I
\right|<\varepsilon,
$$

then $f$ is Riemann integrable over $R$ and

$$
\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA
 =
\lim
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A_{ij}.
$$

</Statement>

The notation

$$
dA=dx\,dy=dy\,dx
$$

records that area is being accumulated. It does not say which variable is
integrated first.

</Definition>

## Upper and lower sums

Upper and lower sums remove the arbitrary choice of tags. For each small
rectangle $R_{ij}$, set

$$
m_{ij}=\inf_{(x,y)\in R_{ij}}f(x,y),
\qquad
M_{ij}=\sup_{(x,y)\in R_{ij}}f(x,y).
$$

Then

$$
m_{ij}\le f(x,y)\le M_{ij}
\qquad ((x,y)\in R_{ij}),
$$

so multiplying by $\Delta A_{ij}$ and adding gives lower and upper bounds.

<Definition slug="double-upper-lower-sums" of="double-integral-over-rectangle">

### Upper and lower double Riemann sums

<Statement>

For a partition $P$ of $R$ into rectangles $R_{ij}$, the lower and upper
double Riemann sums are

$$
L(f,P)=\sum_{i,j}m_{ij}\,\Delta A_{ij},
\qquad
U(f,P)=\sum_{i,j}M_{ij}\,\Delta A_{ij},
$$

where $m_{ij}$ and $M_{ij}$ are the infimum and supremum of $f$ on
$R_{ij}$.

</Statement>

For a nonnegative $f$, $L(f,P)$ is the volume of the tallest boxes on the
partition that fit below the graph and $U(f,P)$ is the volume of the shortest
boxes that reach above it. For a signed $f$, the box heights are signed.
Whenever the integral exists,

$$
L(f,P)\le \iint_R f\,dA\le U(f,P).
$$

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="double-integrability-criterion" of="double-upper-lower-sums">

### Criterion for Riemann integrability

<Statement>

A bounded function $f$ is Riemann integrable over $R$ if and only if, for
every $\varepsilon>0$, there is a partition $P_\varepsilon$ such that

$$
0\le U(f,P_\varepsilon)-L(f,P_\varepsilon)<\varepsilon.
$$

In that case,

$$
\iint_R f\,dA
=\sup_P L(f,P)
=\inf_P U(f,P).
$$

</Statement>

This is equivalent to requiring that the tagged sums have a common limit. It
is usually the easier criterion for proving integrability or
non-integrability.

</Theorem>

<Theorem slug="continuous-double-integrability" of="double-integral-over-rectangle">

### Continuous functions are integrable

<Statement>

Every continuous function on a rectangle is Riemann integrable over that
rectangle.

</Statement>

On sufficiently small rectangles, continuity prevents large variation in
$f$, so the upper and lower sums can be made arbitrarily close.

Continuity is sufficient, not necessary: a discontinuous function may still
be integrable.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="double-midpoint-approximation" of="double-riemann-sum" difficulty="routine">

### A midpoint approximation

Estimate the signed volume under

$$
f(x,y)=x+y^2
$$

over

$$
R=[-2,2]\times[0,4]
$$

by dividing $R$ into $4\times2$ rectangles and using their midpoints.

Because $f$ is negative on part of this rectangle, the result estimates a
signed volume, not the geometric volume between the graph and the $xy$-plane.
For instance, $f(-\tfrac32,1)=-\tfrac12$, so that box contributes negatively.

The subinterval widths are $\Delta x=1$ and $\Delta y=2$, so
$\Delta A=2$. The midpoint $x$-values are

$$
-\frac32,\ -\frac12,\ \frac12,\ \frac32,
$$

and the midpoint $y$-values are $1$ and $3$. For $y=1$, the four function
values sum to $4$; for $y=3$, they sum to $36$. Therefore

$$
\iint_R f\,dA
\approx 2(4+36)=80.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="double-upper-lower-example" of="double-upper-lower-sums" difficulty="routine">

### Upper and lower sums on eight squares

Partition

$$
R=[0,1]\times[0,2]
$$

into eight squares of side length $\tfrac12$ and let

$$
f(x,y)=x^2y.
$$

The function is increasing in both variables on $R$, so on each small square
the minimum is at the lower-left corner and the maximum at the upper-right
corner. Every square has area $\tfrac14$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(f,P)
&=
\sum_{j=0}^{1}\sum_{k=0}^{3}
\left(\frac j2\right)^2
\left(\frac k2\right)\frac14
=\frac{3}{16},\\
U(f,P)
&=
\sum_{j=0}^{1}\sum_{k=0}^{3}
\left(\frac{j+1}{2}\right)^2
\left(\frac{k+1}{2}\right)\frac14
=\frac{25}{16}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
\frac{3}{16}\le\iint_R x^2y\,dA\le\frac{25}{16}.
$$

Refining the partition narrows the gap.

</Example>

<Example slug="nonintegrable-double-function" of="double-integrability-criterion" difficulty="challenge">

### A function that is not integrable

Define

$$
f(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
1,&\text{if $x$ and $y$ are both rational},\\
0,&\text{if $x$ or $y$ is irrational}.
\end{cases}
$$

Every rectangle contains points where both coordinates are rational and
points where at least one is irrational. Hence on every small rectangle,

$$
m_{ij}=0,\qquad M_{ij}=1.
$$

For every partition $P$,

$$
L(f,P)=0,
\qquad
U(f,P)=\sum_{i,j}\Delta A_{ij}=\operatorname{Area}(R).
$$

The upper and lower sums never approach one another, so $f$ is not Riemann
integrable.

</Example>
