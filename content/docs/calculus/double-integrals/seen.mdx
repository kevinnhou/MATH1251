### Riemann integrability and an upper sum

Let $f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ be a function defined on a rectangular
region $D\subset\mathbb R^2$. Let $P$ be any partition of $D$ into
subrectangles $\{R_{ij}\}$, let $(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)$ be points in $R_{ij}$,
and let $L(f,P)$ and $U(f,P)$ denote the lower and upper sums of $f$ over
$P$. Assume that $f$ is bounded on $D$.

#### (a)

Which of the following guarantees that $f$ is integrable over $D$? Select all
answers that apply.

1. $\displaystyle \inf_P L(f,P)=\sup_P U(f,P)$.
2. For each $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a partition $P$ of $D$ such that
   $\displaystyle L(f,P)-U(f,P)<\varepsilon$.
3. For each $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a partition $P$ of $D$ such that
   $\displaystyle U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$.
4. For every sequence of tagged rectangular partitions $(P_m)$ of $D$ whose
   mesh tends to zero, the corresponding tagged sums converge to a common
   limit independently of the choice of tags.
5. $f(x,y)\equiv0$ on $\mathbb R^2\setminus D$.

#### (b)

Kenneth is considering the problem of approximating the double integral of a
function using an upper Riemann sum. Let

$$
R_1=[3,9]\times[2,7],
\qquad
f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R,
\qquad
f(x,y)=7x^2y^2.
$$

Let $P_x$ be a partition of $[3,9]$ into $3$ equal parts and let $P_y$ be
a partition of $[2,7]$ into $2$ equal parts. Let $P$ be the partition
generated by the rectangular regions associated to $P_x\times P_y$.

Find the area $A$ of each subrectangle and compute the upper Riemann sum
$U(f,P)$. Identify the corresponding boxes relative to the graph of $f$.

#### Solution

Part (a) tests two equivalent forms of the definition of integrability: upper
and lower sums bound $f$ on each subrectangle, while tagged sums use
arbitrary sample points. Part (b) uses the fact that $f$ is increasing on the
rectangle, so its maximum on each subrectangle is at the upper-right corner.

#### (a) Selecting the integrability criteria

For a partition $P$, the lower and upper sums satisfy

$$
L(f,P)\le U(f,P).
$$

The upper/lower-sum criterion says that $f$ is integrable when, for every
$\varepsilon>0$, there is a partition for which

$$
0\le U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon.
$$

Therefore statement 3 is correct.

Statement 1 is a strong sufficient condition. If
$c=\inf_P L(f,P)=\sup_P U(f,P)$, then for every partition $P$,

$$
\inf_P L(f,P)\le L(f,P)\le U(f,P)\le\sup_P U(f,P),
$$

so $L(f,P)=U(f,P)=c$. The gap between upper and lower sums is zero, so $f$
is integrable. The condition is stronger than necessary, but it does
guarantee integrability.

Statement 2 has the wrong sign: since $L(f,P)-U(f,P)\le0$ always, the
inequality says nothing about the size of the gap. Statement 5 concerns
values outside $D$, whereas integrability over $D$ depends only on $f$ on $D$.

Statement 4 is the tagged-sum form of the definition: the partitions become
arbitrarily fine and the limit does not depend on the tags. Without that
independence, different choices of tags could give different limits. Hence
the correct selections are

$$
{\text{statements 1, 3 and 4}.}
$$

#### (b) Computing the upper sum

The width of $R_1$ is $9-3=6$. Dividing it into $3$ equal parts gives

$$
\Delta x=\frac63=2.
$$

The height is $7-2=5$. Dividing it into $2$ equal parts gives

$$
\Delta y=\frac52.
$$

Thus the area of every subrectangle is

$$
{A=\Delta x\,\Delta y
=2\cdot\frac52=5}.
$$

The partition points are

$$
P_x=\{3,5,7,9\},
\qquad
P_y=\left\{2,\frac92,7\right\}.
$$

On $R_1$, both $x$ and $y$ are positive and

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=14xy^2>0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=14x^2y>0.
$$

So $f$ is increasing in both variables and its maximum on each subrectangle
is at the upper-right corner. The upper-right $x$-values are $5,7,9$ and the
upper-right $y$-values are $\frac92,7$. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
U(f,P)
&=5\left[
7(5^2)\left(\frac92\right)^2
+7(5^2)(7^2)
+7(7^2)\left(\frac92\right)^2
+7(7^2)(7^2)\right.\\
&\qquad\left.
+7(9^2)\left(\frac92\right)^2
+7(9^2)(7^2)
\right]\\
&=5\cdot7
\left(5^2+7^2+9^2\right)
\left(\left(\frac92\right)^2+7^2\right)\\
&=35(155)\left(\frac{81}{4}+49\right)\\
&=35(155)\left(\frac{277}{4}\right)\\
&={\frac{1502725}{4}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This upper sum is the total volume of the boxes on the subrectangles of $P$
that reach above the graph. The lower sum would use the lower-left corners,
giving boxes that fit below the graph, as in the [double Riemann
sum](/calculus/double-integrals/double-integrals-and-riemann-sums) definition.

</Example>

### Changing variables in an elliptical integral

Your friend Brad is interested in calculating the following integral:

$$
J=\iint_{\Omega}(3x+2y)^2\,dx\,dy. \tag{1}
$$

Here $\Omega$ is the region bounded by

$$
y=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
y=\frac54\sqrt{4^2-x^2}.
$$

#### (a) Choosing the change of variables

Brad wants to find a change of variables to write this integral in terms of coordinates that are very similar to the standard polar coordinates. Choose the suitable change of variables for the region $\Omega$ from the following options:

1. $x=4r\cos\theta,\ y=5r\sin\theta$ where $0\le\theta\le2\pi$ and $-1\le r\le1$.
2. $x=4r\cos\theta,\ y=5r\sin\theta$ where $0\le\theta\le\pi$ and $-1\le r\le1$.
3. $x=4r\cos\theta,\ y=5r\sin\theta$ where $-\pi\le\theta\le0$ and $0\le r\le1$.
4. $x=4r\cos\theta,\ y=5r\sin\theta$ where $0\le\theta\le2\pi$ and $0\le r\le1$.
5. $x=4r\cos\theta,\ y=5r\sin\theta$ where $0\le\theta\le\pi$ and $0\le r\le1$.
6. $x=4r\cos\theta,\ y=5r\sin\theta$ where $-\pi\le\theta\le0$ and $-1\le r\le1$.

The suitable option is **5**:

$$
x=4r\cos\theta,\qquad y=5r\sin\theta,
\qquad 0\le\theta\le\pi,\qquad 0\le r\le1.
$$

To check the region, substitute the transformation into the equation of the
curved boundary:

$$
\frac{x^2}{16}+\frac{y^2}{25}
=r^2\cos^2\theta+r^2\sin^2\theta
=r^2.
$$

Thus $r=1$ gives the ellipse

$$
\frac{x^2}{16}+\frac{y^2}{25}=1,
$$

whose upper half is the given curve. The condition $0\le r\le1$ fills the
inside of the ellipse and $0\le\theta\le\pi$ gives $y=5r\sin\theta\ge0$.

The other angular ranges describe the lower half or the full ellipse.
Allowing negative $r$ gives some points a second representation.

#### (b) Finding $g(r,\theta)$

With the order of integration requested in the question, the transformed integral has the form

$$
J=\int_{a_1}^{a_2}\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}
g(r,\theta)\,d\theta\,dr. \tag{2}
$$

The Jacobian matrix is

$$
\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
4\cos\theta&-4r\sin\theta\\
5\sin\theta&5r\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}
&=
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
4\cos\theta&-4r\sin\theta\\
5\sin\theta&5r\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}\\
&=20r\cos^2\theta+20r\sin^2\theta\\
&=20r.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $0\le r\le1$, the absolute Jacobian factor is $20r$. The integrand becomes

$$
(3x+2y)^2
=\bigl(12r\cos\theta+10r\sin\theta\bigr)^2
=r^2\bigl(12\cos\theta+10\sin\theta\bigr)^2.
$$

Multiplying by the area factor gives

$$
{
g(r,\theta)
=20r^3\bigl(12\cos\theta+10\sin\theta\bigr)^2
}.
$$

Here $a_1=0$, $a_2=1$, $\theta_1=0$ and $\theta_2=\pi$.

#### (c) Calculating $J$

Substituting the transformed integrand and bounds into (2) gives

$$
J=\int_0^1\int_0^\pi
20r^3\bigl(12\cos\theta+10\sin\theta\bigr)^2
\,d\theta\,dr.
$$

The radial factor is

$$
\int_0^1 20r^3\,dr
=20\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^1
=5.
$$

For the angular factor, expand the square:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\pi
\bigl(12\cos\theta+10\sin\theta\bigr)^2\,d\theta
&=\int_0^\pi
\left(144\cos^2\theta
+240\sin\theta\cos\theta
+100\sin^2\theta\right)\,d\theta\\
&=144\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}
+240\left[\frac{\sin^2\theta}{2}\right]_0^\pi
+100\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\\
&=72\pi+0+50\pi\\
&=122\pi.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence

$$
{J=5(122\pi)=610\pi}.
$$

Scaled [polar
coordinates](/calculus/double-integrals/change-of-variables-and-polar-coordinates)
suit this problem because they turn the upper half ellipse into the rectangle
$0\le r\le1$, $0\le\theta\le\pi$, with the Jacobian supplying the area
scaling.

The vanishing mixed term reflects the symmetry of the upper half ellipse
under $x\mapsto-x$: the $\sin\theta\cos\theta$ term comes from the part of
the integrand that cancels between the left and right halves.

</Example>
