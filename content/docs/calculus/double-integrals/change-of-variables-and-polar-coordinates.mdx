This page changes the coordinates of a double integral to fit the region or
the integrand. When the Cartesian slices from [regions and order of
integration](/calculus/double-integrals/regions-and-order-of-integration) are
awkward, another coordinate system may be simpler. A disk, for example, has
square-root bounds in Cartesian coordinates but constant bounds in polar
coordinates.

The new coordinates simplify the description and the Jacobian corrects the
area element for how small pieces stretch or shrink. The same tools evaluate
weighted integrals in [mass, centroids and
probability](/calculus/double-integrals/mass-centroids-and-probability) and
the series integral in [Euler's Basel sum](/calculus/double-integrals/basel-sum).

## The Jacobian records area scaling

Suppose the old coordinates are given in terms of new ones by

$$
(x,y)=g(u,v)=\bigl(x(u,v),y(u,v)\bigr).
$$

<Definition slug="double-integral-jacobian-matrix">

### Jacobian matrix

<Statement>

The Jacobian matrix of $g$ is

$$
J_g(u,v)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u}&
\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v}\\[8pt]
\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u}&
\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Definition slug="double-integral-jacobian-determinant" of="double-integral-jacobian-matrix">

### Jacobian determinant

<Statement>

The Jacobian determinant is

$$
\det J_g(u,v)
=\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}.
$$

Its absolute value is the local area-scaling factor:

$$
dA=dx\,dy
=\left|\det J_g(u,v)\right|\,du\,dv.
$$

</Statement>

The determinant is negative when the map reverses orientation. Area is
non-negative, so the change-of-variables formula uses its absolute value.

To see why it measures area, take small increments $\Delta u,\Delta v$:

$$
g(u+\Delta u,v+\Delta v)-g(u,v)
\approx J_g(u,v)
\begin{pmatrix}\Delta u\\ \Delta v\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The linear map $J_g(u,v)$ sends a small coordinate rectangle of area
$\Delta u\,\Delta v$ to a parallelogram of area approximately
$|\det J_g(u,v)|\Delta u\,\Delta v$. Taking the limit gives the area element
$dA=|\det J_g(u,v)|\,du\,dv$.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="double-integral-change-of-variables" of="double-integral-jacobian-determinant">

### Change of variables

<Statement>

Let $\Omega$ be a region in $(x,y)$-coordinates and let $\Phi$ be a region
in $(u,v)$-coordinates. Assume that $f$ is integrable on $\Omega$, that $g$
is $C^1$ on $\Phi$, that $\Phi$ can be split into finitely many pieces on
which $g$ is one-to-one with non-overlapping interiors and that the Jacobian
is nonzero away from boundary or other exceptional sets of area zero. The
images of those pieces must cover $\Omega$ once, apart from boundaries of area
zero; otherwise the transformed integral can omit or count part of the region
more than once. If a map covers the target with multiplicity, split the
parameter region into one-to-one pieces (or account for that multiplicity)
before applying the formula.
Then

$$
\iint_\Omega f(x,y)\,dx\,dy
=
\iint_\Phi
f\bigl(x(u,v),y(u,v)\bigr)
\left|\det J_g(u,v)\right|\,du\,dv.
$$

</Statement>

Applying the theorem involves two separate tasks: rewriting the integrand in
terms of $u,v$ and describing the old region in the new coordinates. The
second task is geometric and cannot be skipped.

</Theorem>

<Method slug="choose-double-integral-change-of-variables" of="double-integral-change-of-variables">

### Choose and apply a coordinate change

<Statement>

To change variables in a double integral:

1. choose new coordinates suggested by the boundaries or by a repeated
   expression in the integrand;
2. solve for $x$ and $y$ in terms of the new coordinates when needed;
3. transform every boundary of the region;
4. rewrite the integrand;
5. calculate the absolute Jacobian factor;
6. integrate over the transformed region.

</Statement>

Good coordinates simplify the region, the integrand or both. A substitution
that simplifies the formula but complicates the region may not help.

</Method>

## Polar coordinates

<Theorem slug="polar-double-integral" of="double-integral-change-of-variables">

### Polar area element

<Statement>

For $r\ge0$, the polar-coordinate change of variables is

$$
x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,
$$

and its Jacobian satisfies

$$
\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}\right|
=r.
$$

Consequently,

$$
dA=r\,dr\,d\theta,
$$

and

$$
\iint_\Omega f(x,y)\,dA
=\iint_\Phi
f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta.
$$

</Statement>

The factor $r$ is also visible geometrically. A small polar rectangle has
radial side approximately $dr$ and angular side approximately $r\,d\theta$,
so its area is approximately $r\,dr\,d\theta$. The same $(r,\theta)$ pair
parametrises complex numbers in [polar form](/algebra/complex-numbers/polar-form),
with $x+iy=re^{i\theta}$.

A usual polar description takes $r\ge0$ and an angular interval of length at
most $2\pi$. Each point then has one representation, except on a boundary ray
and at the origin. Those exceptional sets have area zero, so the
change-of-variables theorem still applies.

The radial limits are read along each ray from the boundary of the region;
they need not be constant.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="quarter-disk-area" of="polar-double-integral" difficulty="routine">

### Area of a quarter disk

The first-quadrant portion of the unit disk is

$$
0\le r\le1,\qquad 0\le\theta\le\frac{\pi}{2}.
$$

Its area is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)
&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^1r\,dr\,d\theta\\
&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac12\,d\theta
=\frac{\pi}{4}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="polar-triangle-integral" of="polar-double-integral" difficulty="exam">

### A triangle in polar coordinates

Evaluate

$$
I=\int_0^1\int_0^x
\left(1+\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\right)\,dy\,dx.
$$

The region is the first-quadrant triangle bounded by $x=1$ and $y=x$. In
polar coordinates,

$$
0\le\theta\le\frac{\pi}{4},
\qquad
0\le r\le\sec\theta,
$$

because $x=r\cos\theta\le1$. Also,
$\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=r$. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
I
&=\int_0^{\pi/4}\int_0^{\sec\theta}(1+r)r\,dr\,d\theta\\
&=\int_0^{\pi/4}
\left[\frac{r^2}{2}+\frac{r^3}{3}\right]_{0}^{\sec\theta}
d\theta\\
&=\int_0^{\pi/4}
\left(\frac{\sec^2\theta}{2}
+\frac{\sec^3\theta}{3}\right)d\theta\\
&=\frac12+\frac{\sqrt2}{6}
+\frac16\ln(1+\sqrt2).
\end{aligned}
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="spiral-area" of="polar-double-integral" difficulty="routine">

### Area enclosed by a spiral

The region swept between

$$
r=\theta,\qquad0\le\theta\le2\pi,
$$

and the $x$-axis has

$$
0\le\theta\le2\pi,\qquad0\le r\le\theta.
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)
&=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\theta r\,dr\,d\theta\\
&=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\theta^2}{2}\,d\theta
=\frac{4\pi^3}{3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The familiar polar area formula

$$
\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)
=\frac12\int r(\theta)^2\,d\theta
$$

is the result of performing the radial integral first.

</Example>

<Example slug="gaussian-double-integral" of="polar-double-integral" difficulty="challenge">

### The Gaussian integral

Let $\Omega$ be the first quadrant and consider

$$
I=\iint_\Omega e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA.
$$

The region is unbounded, so apply the change of variables on bounded
quarter-disks and then let their radius tend to infinity. The integrand is
nonnegative, so monotone convergence justifies this limit. Polar coordinates
give

$$
\begin{aligned}
I
&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta\\
&=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac12\,d\theta
=\frac{\pi}{4}.
\end{aligned}
$$

In Cartesian coordinates the integrand is a product
$e^{-x^2}e^{-y^2}$. Because it is nonnegative, Tonelli's theorem (or monotone
limits over bounded rectangles) justifies separating the two improper
integrals:

$$
I
=\left(\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx\right)^2.
$$

Hence

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}.
$$

For

$$
G=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx,
$$

symmetry and $u=x/\sqrt2$ give

$$
\begin{aligned}
G
&=2\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2/2}\,dx\\
&=2\sqrt2\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}\,du
=\sqrt{2\pi}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Example>

## Other coordinate changes

<Example slug="linear-double-integral-change" of="choose-double-integral-change-of-variables" difficulty="exam">

### A linear change of variables

Evaluate

$$
I=\int_0^3\int_{-x}^{x}(2-x-y)^2\,dy\,dx
$$

using

$$
u=x+y,\qquad v=x-y.
$$

The inverse transformation is

$$
x=\frac{u+v}{2},
\qquad
y=\frac{u-v}{2},
$$

and

$$
\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|
=
\left|
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac12&\frac12\\[2pt]
\frac12&-\frac12
\end{pmatrix}
\right|
=\frac12.
$$

The original region is bounded by $y=-x$, $y=x$ and $x=3$. These become

$$
y=-x\Longrightarrow u=0,\qquad
y=x\Longrightarrow v=0,\qquad
x=3\Longrightarrow u+v=6.
$$

Thus

$$
0\le u\le6,\qquad0\le v\le6-u.
$$

Since $x+y=u$, the integrand is $(2-u)^2$. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
I
&=\int_0^6\int_0^{6-u}(2-u)^2\frac12\,dv\,du\\
&=\frac12\int_0^6(2-u)^2(6-u)\,du
=18.
\end{aligned}
$$

These coordinates suit the problem because $x+y$ occurs in the integrand and
the region becomes a triangle with two sides on the axes.

</Example>

<Example slug="nonlinear-double-integral-change" of="choose-double-integral-change-of-variables" difficulty="challenge">

### A nonlinear change of variables

Evaluate

$$
\iint_\Omega(4x^2-2y^2)\,dA,
$$

where

$$
\Omega=\{(x,y):1\le xy\le4,\ x\le y\le4x\}.
$$

Use

$$
u=xy,\qquad v=\frac yx.
$$

On $\Omega$, the inequalities force $x>0$: if $x<0$, then $4x<x$ and the
condition $x\le y\le4x$ is impossible. The map therefore has the positive
inverse branch
$$
x=\sqrt{\frac uv},\qquad y=\sqrt{uv},
$$
for $u,v>0$. The region becomes the rectangle

$$
1\le u\le4,\qquad1\le v\le4.
$$

The forward Jacobian is

$$
\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}
=\det
\begin{pmatrix}
y&x\\[2pt]
-y/x^2&1/x
\end{pmatrix}
=\frac yx+\frac yx=2v.
$$

Hence

$$
dA
=\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|\,du\,dv
=\frac1{2v}\,du\,dv.
$$

Since $x^2=u/v$ and $y^2=uv$,

$$
4x^2-2y^2=\frac{4u}{v}-2uv.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
I
&=\int_1^4\int_1^4
\left(\frac{4u}{v}-2uv\right)\frac1{2v}\,dv\,du\\
&=\int_1^4\int_1^4\left(\frac{2u}{v^2}-u\right)\,dv\,du\\
&=\int_1^4\left[-\frac{2u}{v}-uv\right]_{1}^{4}\,du\\
&=\int_1^4-\frac{3u}{2}\,du
=-\frac{45}{4}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The answer is negative because the integrand takes both signs; a double
integral is not automatically a geometric volume.

</Example>
