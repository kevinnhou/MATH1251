This page extends double integrals from rectangles to regions bounded by
curves and shows how to read the limits of integration from a sketch.

The idea is to place a bounded region inside a rectangle and extend the
function by zero outside the region. When that zero extension is bounded and
piecewise continuous, with discontinuities only along boundary curves,
[Fubini's theorem](/calculus/double-integrals/fubini-and-iterated-integrals)
applies to the enclosing rectangle because those curves have area zero.

Finding the limits is a separate geometric task: each slice must start and
stop on the boundary of the original region. When neither vertical nor
horizontal slices give workable bounds, reshape the region with a [change of
variables](/calculus/double-integrals/change-of-variables-and-polar-coordinates).

## Extending the integrand by zero

<Definition slug="double-integral-zero-extension" of="double-integral-over-rectangle">

### Zero extension to a rectangle

<Statement>

Let $\Omega$ be a bounded region contained in a rectangle $R$. Define

$$
f_\Omega(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
f(x,y),&(x,y)\in\Omega,\\
0,&(x,y)\in R\setminus\Omega.
\end{cases}
$$

If $f_\Omega$ is integrable on $R$ (for example, if it is bounded and
piecewise continuous with discontinuities only along finitely many
piecewise-smooth boundary curves), define the double integral over $\Omega$ by

$$
\iint_\Omega f(x,y)\,dA
:=\iint_R f_\Omega(x,y)\,dA.
$$

</Statement>

The zero extension contributes nothing outside $\Omega$, so the inner limits
describe only the part of each slice that lies inside $\Omega$.

</Definition>

## Vertical and horizontal slices

<Theorem slug="type-i-double-integral" of="double-integral-zero-extension">

### Vertical-slice regions

<Statement>

If $c(x)\le d(x)$ on $[a,b]$ and the boundary functions are sufficiently
regular for the integral to exist and if

$$
\Omega=\{(x,y):a\le x\le b,\ c(x)\le y\le d(x)\},
$$

then

$$
\iint_\Omega f(x,y)\,dA
=\int_a^b\int_{c(x)}^{d(x)}
f(x,y)\,dy\,dx.
$$

</Statement>

For each fixed $x$, the inner variable $y$ runs from the lower boundary
$c(x)$ to the upper boundary $d(x)$.

</Theorem>

<Theorem slug="type-ii-double-integral" of="double-integral-zero-extension">

### Horizontal-slice regions

<Statement>

If $a(y)\le b(y)$ on $[c,d]$ and the boundary functions are sufficiently
regular for the integral to exist and if

$$
\Omega=\{(x,y):c\le y\le d,\ a(y)\le x\le b(y)\},
$$

then

$$
\iint_\Omega f(x,y)\,dA
=\int_c^d\int_{a(y)}^{b(y)}
f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.
$$

</Statement>

For each fixed $y$, the inner variable $x$ runs from the left boundary
$a(y)$ to the right boundary $b(y)$.

</Theorem>

<Method slug="set-up-region-double-integral" of="type-i-double-integral,type-ii-double-integral">

### Set up an integral over a planar region

<Statement>

To set up a double integral over $\Omega$:

1. sketch the region and every boundary curve;
2. decide whether vertical or horizontal slices give simpler bounds;
3. find the range of the outside variable;
4. fix that variable and find the two inner bounds;
5. write the inner integral first, then integrate from the inside outward;
6. split the region if one pair of inner bounds does not describe every slice.

</Statement>

Changing the order describes the same region by different slices, so the new
limits must be read from the boundary, not by swapping the old ones.

</Method>

<Example slug="double-integral-triangle" of="set-up-region-double-integral" difficulty="routine">

### A triangular region

Let

$$
f(x,y)=xy^2
$$

and let $\Omega$ be the triangle with vertices

$$
(0,0),\qquad(1,1),\qquad(1,-1).
$$

Vertical slices give

$$
0\le x\le1,\qquad -x\le y\le x.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_\Omega xy^2\,dA
&=\int_0^1\int_{-x}^{x}xy^2\,dy\,dx\\
&=\int_0^1x\left[\frac{y^3}{3}\right]_{-x}^{x}dx\\
&=\int_0^1\frac{2x^4}{3}\,dx
=\frac{2}{15}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The same triangle can be described horizontally by

$$
-1\le y\le1,\qquad |y|\le x\le1.
$$

The reversed-order calculation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_\Omega xy^2\,dA
&=\int_{-1}^1\int_{|y|}^{1}xy^2\,dx\,dy\\
&=\int_{-1}^1\frac{y^2}{2}(1-y^2)\,dy
=\frac{2}{15}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="reverse-non-elementary-double-integral" of="set-up-region-double-integral" difficulty="exam">

### Reverse the order to find an antiderivative

Consider

$$
\int_0^1\int_{2x}^{2}\sin(y^2)\,dy\,dx.
$$

The inner integral has no elementary antiderivative. The region is

$$
0\le x\le1,\qquad 2x\le y\le2.
$$

Its $y$-values range from $0$ to $2$. For fixed $y$, the inequality
$2x\le y$ gives $0\le x\le y/2$. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1\int_{2x}^{2}\sin(y^2)\,dy\,dx
&=\int_0^2\int_0^{y/2}\sin(y^2)\,dx\,dy\\
&=\int_0^2\frac y2\sin(y^2)\,dy\\
&=\frac14\int_0^4\sin u\,du
\qquad(u=y^2)\\
&=\frac{1-\cos4}{4}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Reversing the order turns the difficult inner integral into a direct substitution.

</Example>

<Example slug="split-double-integral-region" of="set-up-region-double-integral" difficulty="challenge">

### A region that must be split

Consider

$$
\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{x^2}^{2-x^2}f(x,y)\,dy\,dx.
$$

The region lies between

$$
y=x^2,\qquad y=2-x^2.
$$

Its $y$-range is $0\le y\le2$. For $0\le y\le1$, the binding condition is
$x^2\le y$:

$$
-\sqrt y\le x\le\sqrt y.
$$

For $1\le y\le2$, the binding condition is $y\le2-x^2$:

$$
-\sqrt{2-y}\le x\le\sqrt{2-y}.
$$

The reversed order is therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{x^2}^{2-x^2}f(x,y)\,dy\,dx
&=\int_0^1\int_{-\sqrt y}^{\sqrt y}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\\
&\quad+\int_1^2\int_{-\sqrt{2-y}}^{\sqrt{2-y}}
f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.
\end{aligned}
$$

A single pair of horizontal bounds would either omit part of the region or
include points outside it.

</Example>

## Linearity, additivity and area

<Proposition slug="double-integral-properties" of="double-integral-zero-extension">

### Properties of double integrals

<Statement>

For integrable $f$ and $g$ over $\Omega$ and a constant $k$,

$$
\iint_\Omega(f+g)\,dA
=\iint_\Omega f\,dA+\iint_\Omega g\,dA,
$$

$$
\iint_\Omega kf\,dA
=k\iint_\Omega f\,dA.
$$

If $\Omega=\Omega_1\cup\Omega_2$ and the two regions are disjoint, then

$$
\iint_\Omega f\,dA
=\iint_{\Omega_1}f\,dA+\iint_{\Omega_2}f\,dA.
$$

</Statement>

Splitting the parabolic region above is an instance of additivity.

</Proposition>

<Corollary slug="double-integral-area" of="double-integral-properties">

### Area as a double integral

<Statement>

The area of a planar region $\Omega$ is

$$
\operatorname{Area}(\Omega)=\iint_\Omega 1\,dA.
$$

</Statement>

The integrand $1$ gives every point of the region unit weight. Replacing $1$
by a density gives [mass](/calculus/double-integrals/mass-centroids-and-probability)
instead of area.

</Corollary>
