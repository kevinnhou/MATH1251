This page uses the chapter's tools to prove Euler's result
$\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2=\pi^2/6$. The [p-series
test](/calculus/taylor-series/convergence-tests#p-series-test) with $p=2$
shows that the series converges; a double integral finds its value.

The [geometric-series
formula](/calculus/taylor-series/infinite-series#geometric-series-method) and
[Fubini's theorem](/calculus/double-integrals/fubini-and-iterated-integrals)
write the series as an integral over the unit square. A linear [change of
variables](/calculus/double-integrals/change-of-variables-and-polar-coordinates)
turns the square into a diamond, sliced as in [regions and order of
integration](/calculus/double-integrals/regions-and-order-of-integration).
Symmetry and one-variable substitutions then finish the evaluation.

## Turn the series into an integral

For $n\ge0$,

$$
\int_0^1x^n\,dx=\frac1{n+1}.
$$

Multiplying two copies of this identity and using Fubini on the unit square
gives

$$
\frac1{(n+1)^2}
=\int_0^1\int_0^1(xy)^n\,dx\,dy.
$$

To sum over $n$, use the geometric series: for $0\le x,y<1$,

$$
\sum_{n=0}^\infty(xy)^n=\frac1{1-xy}.
$$

The finite geometric sums are nonnegative and increase pointwise, so monotone
convergence justifies taking their limit under the integral.

The integrand $1/(1-xy)$ is singular only at the corner $(1,1)$, but it is
unbounded in every neighbourhood of that corner. The integral is therefore
improper, defined as the monotone limit over bounded truncations. The corner
has zero area, but the blow-up near it cannot be ignored.

Summing the identities over $n\ge0$ therefore gives

$$
S:=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^2}
=\iint_{[0,1]^2}\frac1{1-xy}\,dx\,dy.
$$

For the same reason, the proof applies the change-of-variables theorem on
compact subregions that stay away from $(1,1)$ and then removes the
truncation by monotone convergence. The transformed integrals are understood
as the corresponding improper limits.

<Theorem slug="basel-sum">

### Euler's evaluation of the Basel sum

<Statement>

The reciprocal-square series satisfies

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}.
$$

</Statement>

</Theorem>

<Proof of="basel-sum">

Use the linear substitution

$$
x=u-v,\qquad y=u+v.
$$

The inverse transformation is

$$
u=\frac{x+y}{2},
\qquad
v=\frac{y-x}{2},
$$

and

$$
\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|
=
\left|
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix}
\right|
=2.
$$

The square $0\le x,y\le1$ becomes a diamond with vertices $(0,0)$,
$(\tfrac12,\pm\tfrac12)$ and $(1,0)$ in the $uv$-plane. Vertical slices
describe it as

$$
\begin{cases}
0\le u\le\frac12,\quad -u\le v\le u,\\[4pt]
\frac12\le u\le1,\quad u-1\le v\le1-u.
\end{cases}
$$

In the new coordinates,

$$
xy=(u-v)(u+v)=u^2-v^2,
\qquad
1-xy=1-u^2+v^2.
$$

The integrand is even in $v$, so we integrate over the upper half and
multiply by two. The Jacobian supplies another factor of two, giving

$$
\begin{aligned}
S
&=
4\int_0^{1/2}\int_0^u
\frac{1}{1-u^2+v^2}\,dv\,du\\
&\quad+
4\int_{1/2}^{1}\int_0^{1-u}
\frac{1}{1-u^2+v^2}\,dv\,du.
\end{aligned}
$$

For fixed $u$,

$$
\int_0^a\frac{dv}{1-u^2+v^2}
=
\frac1{\sqrt{1-u^2}}
\arctan\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\right).
$$

Each piece now needs one trigonometric substitution. The first uses
$u=\sin\theta$; the second uses $u=\cos\theta$, so that the inverse tangent
simplifies by the half-angle identity.

For the first piece, let $u=\sin\theta$, so
$0\le\theta\le\pi/6$. Then

$$
\frac{u}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}=\tan\theta,
$$

and hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_1
&=
4\int_0^{1/2}
\frac1{\sqrt{1-u^2}}
\arctan\left(\frac u{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\right)\,du\\
&=4\int_0^{\pi/6}\theta\,d\theta
=\frac{\pi^2}{18}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For the second piece, let $u=\cos\theta$, so
$0\le\theta\le\pi/3$. As $u$ runs from $1/2$ to $1$, $\theta$ runs from
$\pi/3$ to $0$; flipping the limits removes the minus sign from
$du=-\sin\theta\,d\theta$. The ratio in the inverse tangent is

$$
\frac{1-u}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}
=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}
=\tan\frac\theta2.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_2
&=4\int_0^{\pi/3}\frac\theta2\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{\pi^2}{9}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding the two pieces,

$$
S=S_1+S_2
=\frac{\pi^2}{18}+\frac{\pi^2}{9}
=\frac{\pi^2}{6}.
$$

</Proof>
