This page integrates a density over a region to find total mass, centres of
mass and expected values. The [area
formula](/calculus/double-integrals/regions-and-order-of-integration) uses
unit density, so every point has the same weight. A varying density weights
some parts of the region more than others.

The same weighted integrals describe a thin plate, a population spread over a
region and a continuous probability model. They are evaluated by slicing, as
in [Fubini's theorem](/calculus/double-integrals/fubini-and-iterated-integrals),
often after a [change of
variables](/calculus/double-integrals/change-of-variables-and-polar-coordinates).

## Density on a lamina

A **lamina** is a flat sheet of material with negligible thickness. If
$\rho(x,y)$ is its mass density, then a small area $\Delta A$ near $(x,y)$
has approximate mass $\rho(x,y)\Delta A$.

<Definition slug="lamina-mass-density">

### Mass density of a lamina

<Statement>

A mass density $\rho(x,y)$ assigns mass per unit area to a lamina occupying a
region $\Omega$. For a physical lamina, $\rho\ge0$ and $\rho$ is integrable.
The total mass is

$$
M=\iint_\Omega\rho(x,y)\,dA.
$$

</Statement>

When $\rho=1$, the mass is the area of $\Omega$.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="lamina-centre-of-mass" of="lamina-mass-density">

### Centre of mass and moments

<Statement>

For a lamina with mass density $\rho$ and total mass $M>0$, the centre of
mass is

$$
{
\bar x=\frac1M\iint_\Omega x\rho(x,y)\,dA,
\qquad
\bar y=\frac1M\iint_\Omega y\rho(x,y)\,dA
}.
$$

The density-weighted first moments are

$$
M_y=\iint_\Omega x\rho(x,y)\,dA,
\qquad
M_x=\iint_\Omega y\rho(x,y)\,dA.
$$

</Statement>

The factor $x$ weights mass by horizontal position and the factor $y$
weights it by vertical position. For uniform density, the centre of mass is
the centroid, the geometric centre of the region.

Units give a check: if $x$ and $y$ are measured in units of length
and $\rho$ in mass per unit area, then $M$ has units of mass, each first
moment has units of mass\,$\times$\,length and $(\bar x,\bar y)$ has units
of length.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="variable-density-triangular-lamina" of="lamina-centre-of-mass" difficulty="exam">

### A symmetric region with nonconstant density

Let

$$
\Omega=\{(x,y):|x|\le y\le1\},
\qquad
\rho(x,y)=x^2+y^2.
$$

Horizontal slices give

$$
0\le y\le1,\qquad-y\le x\le y.
$$

The mass is

$$
\begin{aligned}
M
&=\int_0^1\int_{-y}^{y}(x^2+y^2)\,dx\,dy\\
&=\int_0^1\left[\frac{x^3}{3}+xy^2\right]_{-y}^{y}dy\\
&=\int_0^1\frac{8y^3}{3}\,dy
=\frac23.
\end{aligned}
$$

The region and density are symmetric under $x\mapsto-x$, so

$$
\bar x=0.
$$

The vertical moment is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_\Omega y\rho\,dA
&=\int_0^1\int_{-y}^{y}y(x^2+y^2)\,dx\,dy\\
&=\int_0^1\frac{8y^4}{3}\,dy
=\frac{8}{15}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
\bar y=\frac{8/15}{2/3}=\frac45,
$$

and the centre of mass is

$$
{\left(0,\frac45\right)}.
$$

For uniform density, this triangle's centroid has $y$-coordinate $2/3$.
Here the density increases with $y$, so the centre of mass lies higher.

</Example>

## A semicircular lamina in three densities

Let $\Omega$ be the upper half of the unit disk:

$$
0\le r\le1,\qquad0\le\theta\le\pi.
$$

The example compares three densities on this region. Each is symmetric under
$x\mapsto-x$, so $\bar x=0$ and only $\bar y$ needs computing. Coordinates
are treated as pure numbers; in a physical model, the coefficients
multiplying $y$ or $r$ would carry the units needed to make $\rho$ a mass per
unit area.

<Example slug="semicircular-lamina-densities" of="lamina-centre-of-mass,polar-double-integral" difficulty="exam">

### Uniform, vertical and radial density

For uniform density $\rho=1$, the mass is the area:

$$
M=\frac{\pi}{2}.
$$

The vertical moment is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_\Omega y\,dA
&=\int_0^\pi\int_0^1
(r\sin\theta)r\,dr\,d\theta\\
&=\left(\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\right)
\left(\int_0^1r^2\,dr\right)
=\frac23.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
\bar y=\frac{4}{3\pi}.
$$

For $\rho=y$, the mass is

$$
\begin{aligned}
M
&=\iint_\Omega y\,dA\\
&=\int_0^\pi\int_0^1r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta
=\frac23.
\end{aligned}
$$

The vertical moment becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_\Omega y\rho\,dA
&=\iint_\Omega y^2\,dA\\
&=\int_0^\pi\int_0^1
r^3\sin^2\theta\,dr\,d\theta
=\frac{\pi}{8}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence

$$
\bar y=\frac{\pi/8}{2/3}=\frac{3\pi}{16}.
$$

For radial density

$$
\rho=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=r,
$$

the mass is

$$
\begin{aligned}
M
&=\int_0^\pi\int_0^1r\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta
=\frac{\pi}{3},
\end{aligned}
$$

and the vertical moment is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_\Omega y\rho\,dA
&=\int_0^\pi\int_0^1
(r\sin\theta)\,r\,r\,dr\,d\theta
=\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
\bar y=\frac{1/2}{\pi/3}=\frac{3}{2\pi}.
$$

The three heights are approximately $0.424$, $0.589$ and $0.477$. Radial
density moves weight away from the origin and density proportional to $y$
moves it more strongly towards the top.

</Example>

## Population and probability

<Proposition slug="density-to-probability" of="lamina-mass-density">

### Normalising a density

<Statement>

If $\rho\ge0$ on $\Omega$ and

$$
0<M=\iint_\Omega\rho(x,y)\,dA<\infty,
$$

then

$$
p(x,y)=\frac{\rho(x,y)}{M}
$$

is a two-dimensional probability density on $\Omega$, because

$$
\iint_\Omega p(x,y)\,dA=1.
$$

</Statement>

If $\rho$ is a population density, $\iint_R\rho\,dA$ is the population in a
subregion $R\subseteq\Omega$. The normalised density $p$ has units of
inverse area, so $p\,dA$ is dimensionless.

After normalisation, the centre-of-mass coordinates become expected values:

$$
\mathbb E[X]=\iint_\Omega xp(x,y)\,dA,
\qquad
\mathbb E[Y]=\iint_\Omega yp(x,y)\,dA.
$$

For an event represented by a subregion $E\subseteq\Omega$, the probability
is

$$
\mathbb P((X,Y)\in E)=\iint_E p(x,y)\,dA.
$$

This is the population formula with the normalisation making the whole
region have probability $1$. Mass, a population centre and an expected value
are all the same weighted-average construction.

</Proposition>
