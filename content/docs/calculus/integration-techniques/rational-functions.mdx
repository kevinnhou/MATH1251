This page integrates rational functions, which are ratios of polynomials. If
the fraction is improper, divide first; the polynomial part integrates
immediately and the proper remainder decomposes uniquely into partial
fractions. Linear factors lead to logarithms and negative powers, while
non-repeated irreducible quadratics lead to logarithms and inverse tangents.
Repeated quadratics may also need a reduction step or a trigonometric
substitution.

Other integrands belong elsewhere: trigonometric powers in [trigonometric
integrals](/calculus/integration-techniques/trigonometric-integrals), quadratic
surds in [trigonometric and hyperbolic
substitutions](/calculus/integration-techniques/trigonometric-and-hyperbolic-substitutions),
and other algebraic surds in [rationalising
substitutions](/calculus/integration-techniques/rationalising-substitutions).

## Classifying and dividing rational functions

<Definition slug="rational-function">

### Rational function

<Statement>

A function $f$ is a *rational function* if it is of the form
$f(x)=\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$, where $P$ and $Q$ are polynomials and $Q$ is not the
zero polynomial. Its real domain excludes the zeros of $Q$.

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Definition slug="proper-rational" of="rational-function">

### Proper rational function

<Statement>

A rational function $f$ is *proper* if $\deg P<\deg Q$. If $f$ is not proper, long division writes it as a polynomial plus a proper rational function,
$$
\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}=p(x)+\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)},
$$
with $\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)}$ proper.

</Statement>

The polynomial $p$ integrates termwise. Only the proper remainder needs a further decomposition.

</Definition>

<Example slug="classify-rational" of="rational-function" difficulty="routine">

### Which of these are rational?

- $\frac{2x+1}{3x^2-4}$ is rational and proper: $\deg(2x+1)=1<2=\deg(3x^2-4)$.
- $\frac{\cos x}{x^2+1}$ is not rational: $\cos x$ is not a polynomial.
- $\frac{x^2}{x-1}$ is rational and improper: $\deg(x^2)=2>1=\deg(x-1)$.
- $\frac{\sqrt{x}}{x^2+3}$ is not rational: $\sqrt{x}$ is not a polynomial.

</Example>

<Example slug="divide-rational" of="proper-rational" difficulty="exam">

### Polynomial plus proper remainder

Write each rational function as a polynomial plus a proper rational function.

For $\frac{4x-5}{2x+3}$, the leading-term ratio is $2$ and $2(2x+3)=4x+6$, so
$$
(4x-5)-(4x+6)=-11.
$$
Hence
$$
\frac{4x-5}{2x+3}=2-\frac{11}{2x+3}.
$$

For $\frac{x^3-x+1}{x^2+3x-1}$, divide. The first quotient term is $x$ and
$$
x(x^2+3x-1)=x^3+3x^2-x,
$$
so
$$
(x^3-x+1)-(x^3+3x^2-x)=-3x^2+1.
$$
The next quotient term is $-3$ and
$$
-3(x^2+3x-1)=-3x^2-9x+3,
$$
so
$$
(-3x^2+1)-(-3x^2-9x+3)=9x-2.
$$
Hence
$$
\frac{x^3-x+1}{x^2+3x-1}=x-3+\frac{9x-2}{x^2+3x-1}.
$$

</Example>

## Decomposing into partial fractions

Once the remainder is proper, cancel any common factors and factor its denominator over $\mathbb{R}$. Cancellation simplifies the formula only on the original domain: any zero of the original denominator remains an excluded point when choosing an integration interval. A separate partial-fraction term is needed for every power of every factor; omitting a power can make the coefficient equations impossible to solve.

<Theorem slug="partial-fractions" of="proper-rational">

### Partial fractions decomposition

<Statement>

For a fixed denominator after cancellation and factorisation, every *proper* rational function $f$ has a unique partial-fraction expansion with terms of the form
$$
\frac{A}{(x-a)^k},\qquad \frac{Bx+C}{(x^2+bx+c)^k},
$$
where the quadratic $x^2+bx+c$ is irreducible over the real numbers (i.e. $b^2-4c<0$).

</Statement>

The integers $k$ run up to the multiplicity of each factor. Uniqueness means the linear equations for the undetermined coefficients have exactly one solution.
The real irreducible quadratics arise by pairing a non-real complex root with
its conjugate, as in [real linear and quadratic
factorisation](/algebra/complex-numbers/complex-polynomials#real-linear-quadratic-factorisation).

</Theorem>

For $q(x)=ax^2+bx+c$, the quantity $\Delta=b^2-4ac$ is its **discriminant**.
If $\Delta<0$, the quadratic has no real roots and is irreducible over
$\mathbb R$; this is the quadratic case in the partial-fraction forms above.

<Method slug="partial-fraction-terms" of="partial-fractions" see="integrate-rational">

### Partial fraction terms

<Statement>

Given a proper rational function, each irreducible factor of the denominator contributes terms as follows.

- Linear factor $ax+b$: $\frac{A}{ax+b}$.
- Repeated linear factor $(ax+b)^k$: $\frac{A_1}{ax+b}+\frac{A_2}{(ax+b)^2}+\cdots+\frac{A_k}{(ax+b)^k}$.
- Irreducible quadratic factor $ax^2+bx+c$ with $a\ne0$: $\frac{Ax+B}{ax^2+bx+c}$.
- Repeated irreducible quadratic factor $(ax^2+bx+c)^k$: $\frac{A_1x+B_1}{ax^2+bx+c}+\frac{A_2x+B_2}{(ax^2+bx+c)^2}+\cdots+\frac{A_kx+B_k}{(ax^2+bx+c)^k}$.

</Statement>

Here each factor has a general leading coefficient $a$, whereas the decomposition theorem used the monic quadratic $x^2+bx+c$, irreducible when $b^2-4c<0$.

</Method>

<Method slug="integrate-rational" of="partial-fractions,proper-rational" see="partial-fraction-terms">

### Integrate a rational function

<Statement>

To integrate a rational function on an interval containing no zero of its
denominator:

1. If the function is improper, use polynomial division to write it as a polynomial plus a proper rational function and integrate the polynomial.
2. Decompose the proper part into partial fractions.
3. Integrate each linear-factor term using $u=ax+b$: its first power gives $\frac{A}{a}\ln|ax+b|$, while a power $k>1$ gives $\frac{A(ax+b)^{1-k}}{a(1-k)}$.
4. For $q(x)=ax^2+bx+c$ and a term
$\int\frac{Bx+C}{q(x)^k}\,dx$, split the numerator into a multiple of
$q'(x)=2ax+b$ and a constant remainder:
$$
\frac{Bx+C}{q(x)^k}
=\frac{B}{2a}\frac{q'(x)}{q(x)^k}+\frac{D}{q(x)^k},
\qquad D=C-\frac{Bb}{2a}.
$$
Then integrate the derivative term directly and complete the square in the
constant remainder.

</Statement>

The derivative term is immediate:
$$
\int\frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\, dx=\ln|g(x)|+K,
$$
and, for $k>1$,
$$
\int\frac{g'(x)}{g(x)^k}\,dx
=\frac{g(x)^{1-k}}{1-k}+K.
$$
For the constant term, complete the square. When $k=1$, this produces the
standard inverse-tangent integral
$$
\int\frac{1}{h^2+x^2}\, dx=\frac{1}{h}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{h}\right)+K,
\qquad h>0.
$$
For $k>1$, reduce the remaining power or use a trigonometric substitution, as
in the repeated-quadratic example below.

</Method>

## Integrating linear factors

<Example slug="int-distinct-linear" of="partial-fractions" difficulty="exam">

### Distinct linear factors

Find $\int\frac{2x^2+2x-6}{(x-1)(x+1)(x-2)}\, dx$.

The denominator is a product of distinct linear factors, so
$$
\frac{2x^2+2x-6}{(x-1)(x+1)(x-2)}=\frac{A}{x-1}+\frac{B}{x+1}+\frac{C}{x-2}.
$$
Call this identity $(*)$. Heaviside's cover-up method finds $A$, $B$ and $C$. Multiplying $(*)$ by $x-1$ yields
$$
\frac{2x^2+2x-6}{(x+1)(x-2)}=A+\frac{B(x-1)}{x+1}+\frac{C(x-1)}{x-2}.
$$
Identity $(*)$ holds for $x\neq 1,-1,2$, but after multiplying through by $x-1$ both sides are defined at $x=1$, so setting $x=1$ is legitimate:
$$
\frac{2(1)^2+2(1)-6}{(1+1)(1-2)}=\frac{-2}{2\cdot(-1)}=1,
$$
hence $A=1$.

Similarly, multiplying $(*)$ by $x+1$ and setting $x=-1$ gives
$$
B=\left.\frac{2x^2+2x-6}{(x-1)(x-2)}\right|_{x=-1}=-1,
$$
and doing the same with $x-2$ and $x=2$ gives
$$
C=\left.\frac{2x^2+2x-6}{(x-1)(x+1)}\right|_{x=2}=2.
$$
Hence
$$
\int\frac{2x^2+2x-6}{(x-1)(x+1)(x-2)}\, dx=\int\frac{dx}{x-1}-\int\frac{dx}{x+1}+\int\frac{2}{x-2}\, dx
$$
$$
=\ln|x-1|-\ln|x+1|+2\ln|x-2|+C.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="int-repeated-linear" of="partial-fractions" difficulty="exam">

### Repeated linear factors

Find $I=\int\frac{8x+9}{(x-2)(x+3)^2}\, dx$.

The factor $x+3$ is repeated, so
$$
\frac{8x+9}{(x-2)(x+3)^2}=\frac{A}{x-2}+\frac{B}{x+3}+\frac{C}{(x+3)^2}
$$
(or, equivalently, $\frac{A}{x-2}+\frac{Bx+C}{(x+3)^2}$). With the three-term form, the cover-up method from the [distinct linear factors](/calculus/integration-techniques/rational-functions) example gives $A$ by multiplying by $x-2$ and setting $x=2$:
$$
A=\left.\frac{8x+9}{(x+3)^2}\right|_{x=2}=\frac{16+9}{25}=1.
$$
Multiplying by $(x+3)^2$ and setting $x=-3$ gives
$$
C=\left.\frac{8x+9}{x-2}\right|_{x=-3}=\frac{-24+9}{-5}=3.
$$
Cover-up does not give $B$: multiplying by $x+3$ leaves a term $\frac{C}{x+3}$, so $x=-3$ cannot be substituted. Instead, insert the known $A$ and $C$,
$$
\frac{8x+9}{(x-2)(x+3)^2}=\frac{1}{x-2}+\frac{B}{x+3}+\frac{3}{(x+3)^2}
$$
and set $x=0$:
$$
\frac{9}{-18}=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{B}{3}+\frac{1}{3}\implies B=-1.
$$
Hence
$$
I=\int\frac{dx}{x-2}-\int\frac{dx}{x+3}+\int\frac{3}{(x+3)^2}\, dx=\ln|x-2|-\ln|x+3|-\frac{3}{x+3}+C.
$$

This formula is valid on any interval that contains neither pole, $x=2$ nor
$x=-3$; the constant need not be the same on different such intervals.

</Example>

## Integrating irreducible quadratics

For an irreducible quadratic denominator, rewrite the numerator around the derivative of the quadratic. This splits off a logarithm and the constant remainder becomes an inverse tangent after completing the square.

<Example slug="int-linear-over-quadratic" of="integrate-rational" difficulty="exam">

### Linear numerator over a quadratic

Find $I=\int\frac{2x+1}{x^2+4x+5}\, dx$.

The quadratic $x^2+4x+5$ is irreducible ($4^2-4\cdot5=-4<0$), so write the numerator in terms of the derivative $2x+4$ of the denominator:
$$
2x+1=(2x+4)-3.
$$
Thus
$$
I=\int\frac{2x+4}{x^2+4x+5}\, dx-\int\frac{3}{x^2+4x+5}\, dx.
$$
The first integrand is $g'/g$. Completing the square in the second, $x^2+4x+5=(x+2)^2+1$, gives
$$
I=\ln|x^2+4x+5|-\int\frac{3}{(x+2)^2+1}\, dx=\ln|x^2+4x+5|-3\tan^{-1}(x+2)+C.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="int-repeated-quadratic" of="partial-fractions" see="int-cos-sq-sin-sq,int-a2-plus-x2-three-halves" difficulty="exam">

### A repeated irreducible quadratic

Find $I=\int\frac{2x^2-x}{(x^2+1)^2}\, dx$.

Decompose as
$$
\frac{2x^2-x}{(x^2+1)^2}=\frac{Ax+B}{x^2+1}+\frac{Cx+D}{(x^2+1)^2}.
$$
Multiplying by $(x^2+1)^2$,
$$
2x^2-x=(Ax+B)(x^2+1)+Cx+D=Ax^3+Bx^2+(A+C)x+(B+D).
$$
Comparing coefficients, $A=0$, $B=2$, $A+C=-1$ so $C=-1$ and $B+D=0$ so $D=-2$. Hence
$$
I=\int\frac{2}{x^2+1}\, dx-\int\frac{x+2}{(x^2+1)^2}\, dx=2\tan^{-1}(x)-\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{2x}{(x^2+1)^2}\, dx-2\int\frac{1}{(x^2+1)^2}\, dx.
$$
The middle integral is $\int g'/g^2$ with $g(x)=x^2+1$, so
$$
I=2\tan^{-1}(x)+\frac{1}{2}(x^2+1)^{-1}-2\int\frac{1}{(x^2+1)^2}\, dx.
$$

For the remaining integral, let $x=\tan\theta$, so $dx=\sec^2\theta\, d\theta$ (the $x=a\tan\theta$ row of the [trigonometric and hyperbolic substitutions](/calculus/integration-techniques/trigonometric-and-hyperbolic-substitutions), with $a=1$). Then $\tan^2\theta+1=\sec^2\theta$ and
$$
\int\frac{1}{(x^2+1)^2}\, dx=\int\frac{\sec^2\theta}{(\sec^2\theta)^2}\, d\theta=\int\cos^2\theta\, d\theta.
$$
Using $\cos^2\theta=\frac{1+\cos 2\theta}{2}$, as in [trigonometric integrals](/calculus/integration-techniques/trigonometric-integrals),
$$
\int\cos^2\theta\, d\theta=\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\sin(2\theta)}{4}+C=\frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}(x)+\frac{1}{4}\sin(2\tan^{-1}(x))+C.
$$
Altogether,
$$
I=\tan^{-1}(x)+\frac{1}{2}(x^2+1)^{-1}-\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\tan^{-1}(x))+C.
$$

To rewrite the sine, let $\alpha=\tan^{-1}(x)$, so $\tan\alpha=x$ and $\cos\alpha>0$. From $1+\tan^2\alpha=\sec^2\alpha$ and the sign of $\cos\alpha$,
$$
\sin\alpha=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}},\qquad \cos\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}.
$$
The double-angle formula $\sin(2\alpha)=2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha$ then gives
$$
\sin(2\tan^{-1}(x))=2\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}\right)=\frac{2x}{x^2+1}.
$$
Thus
$$
I=\tan^{-1}(x)+\frac{1-2x}{2(x^2+1)}+C.
$$

</Example>
