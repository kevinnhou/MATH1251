This page gives an exact formula for the error of a [Taylor polynomial](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-polynomials) $P_n$, called the remainder $R_{n+1}$:

$$
f(x)=P_n(x)+R_{n+1}(x).
$$

Bounding the remainder turns an approximation into a guaranteed one and its leading term classifies stationary points.

## The remainder measures what the polynomial misses

<Recall of="taylor-polynomial" />

<Theorem slug="taylors-theorem" of="taylor-polynomial">

### Taylor's theorem with remainder

<Statement>

Suppose $f$ has $n+1$ continuous derivatives on an interval containing $a$ and $x$. If

$$
P_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k,
$$

then

$$
f(x)=P_n(x)+R_{n+1}(x),
$$

where the integral remainder is

$$
R_{n+1}(x)
=\frac1{n!}\int_a^x f^{(n+1)}(t)(x-t)^n\,dt.
$$

For $x\ne a$, there is also a point $c$ between $a$ and $x$ for which the Lagrange remainder is

$$
R_{n+1}(x)
=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}.
$$

</Statement>

The integral form accumulates the first omitted derivative. The Lagrange form replaces the
integral by one unknown derivative value. If

$$
|f^{(n+1)}(t)|\le M
$$

throughout the interval between $a$ and $x$, then

$$
|f(x)-P_n(x)|
=|R_{n+1}(x)|
\le \frac{M|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.
$$

The bound depends on the distance $|x-a|$ from the centre and the size of the first omitted
derivative. The factorial often dominates the numerator for large $n$, but this must be
checked for the interval and degree in use.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="taylors-theorem">

We prove the integral formula by induction. When $n=0$, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
gives

$$
R_1(x)=\int_a^x f'(t)\,dt=f(x)-f(a).
$$

Assume that

$$
R_k(x)
=\frac1{(k-1)!}\int_a^x f^{(k)}(t)(x-t)^{k-1}\,dt.
$$

For the next remainder, take $u=(x-t)^k$ and
$dv=f^{(k+1)}(t)\,dt$, so $du=-k(x-t)^{k-1}\,dt$ and $v=f^{(k)}(t)$. We obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{k+1}(x)
&=\frac1{k!}\int_a^x f^{(k+1)}(t)(x-t)^k\,dt\\
&=\left[\frac{f^{(k)}(t)}{k!}(x-t)^k\right]_{t=a}^{t=x}
+\frac1{(k-1)!}\int_a^x f^{(k)}(t)(x-t)^{k-1}\,dt\\
&=-\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+R_k(x)\\
&=f(x)-P_{k-1}(x)-\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k\\
&=f(x)-P_k(x),
\end{aligned}
$$

The $t=x$ endpoint vanishes because $(x-x)^k=0$ and the penultimate line
uses the induction hypothesis $R_k(x)=f(x)-P_{k-1}(x)$. Each induction step
removes one Taylor term, which proves the integral form for every $n$.

To obtain the Lagrange form, apply the mean value theorem for integrals to the continuous
function $f^{(n+1)}$ with the non-negative weight $|x-t|^n$ between $a$ and $x$.
Accounting for the orientation when $x<a$ gives
$\int_a^x f^{(n+1)}(t)(x-t)^n\,dt
=f^{(n+1)}(c)(x-a)^{n+1}/(n+1)$ for some intermediate $c$.

</Proof>

## Taylor's theorem extends the Mean Value Theorem

When $n=0$, Taylor's theorem reads

$$
f(x)=f(a)+f'(c)(x-a).
$$

<Corollary slug="mean-value-theorem-from-taylor" of="taylors-theorem">

### The Mean Value Theorem as the first case

<Statement>

For some $c$ between $a$ and $x$,

$$
\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}=f'(c)
$$

whenever $x\ne a$ and the hypotheses of the Mean Value Theorem hold.

</Statement>

</Corollary>

## Turn the remainder into an error bound

<Method slug="taylor-error-bound" of="taylors-theorem">

### Bound a Taylor approximation error

<Statement>

To prove that $P_n(x)$ approximates $f(x)$ within a tolerance $\varepsilon$:

1. identify $f^{(n+1)}$;
2. find a bound $M$ for $|f^{(n+1)}(t)|$ between $a$ and the relevant values of $x$;
3. use
   $$
   |f(x)-P_n(x)|
   \le \frac{M|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!};
   $$
4. choose $n$ or the interval so that the right-hand side is at most $\varepsilon$.

</Statement>

The estimate is uniform on an interval when one $M$ and one distance bound work throughout
that interval. It bounds the error from above; it is not the actual error.

</Method>

<Example slug="exponential-error-bound" of="taylor-error-bound" difficulty="routine">

### Approximating $e^x$ by a quadratic

Take

$$
P_2(x)=1+x+\frac{x^2}{2}
$$

for $f(x)=e^x$ about zero. The third derivative is $e^x$. On
$[-1/2,1/2]$, an intermediate point satisfies $e^c\le e^{1/2}$, so

$$
|R_3(x)|
\le \frac{e^{1/2}|x|^3}{3!}
\le \frac{e^{1/2}}{48}
\approx0.03435.
$$

The bound holds for every $x$ in the interval.

</Example>

<Example slug="compute-e-with-taylor-remainder" of="taylor-error-bound" difficulty="exam">

### Computing $e$ to within $10^{-6}$

At $x=1$, the Maclaurin polynomial gives

$$
P_n(1)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac1{k!}.
$$

For $0<c<1$, the Lagrange remainder satisfies $e^c<3$, hence

$$
|R_{n+1}(1)|<\frac3{(n+1)!}.
$$

With $n=9$,

$$
\frac3{10!}<10^{-6}.
$$

Therefore

$$
e\approx P_9(1)
=\sum_{k=0}^{9}\frac1{k!}
=2.7182815256
$$

with the requested accuracy. The bound guarantees the accuracy; the actual error is about
$3.03\times10^{-7}$.

</Example>

<Example slug="sine-taylor-error" of="taylor-error-bound" difficulty="routine">

### Error for a sine approximation

Every derivative of $\sin x$ is one of $\pm\sin x$ or $\pm\cos x$, so its absolute value is
at most $1$. For a degree-$n$ Taylor polynomial about $a$,

$$
|R_{n+1}(x)|
\le \frac{|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.
$$

Near the expansion centre, the factorial makes the bound decrease rapidly.

</Example>

## The first nonzero term controls local behaviour

At a stationary point the linear term vanishes, so the first nonzero higher derivative
decides whether values rise, fall or change sign on each side. The remainder is of strictly
higher order, so close to the centre it is too small to reverse that sign.

<Theorem slug="stationary-point-test" of="taylors-theorem">

### Classify a stationary point with the first nonzero derivative

<Statement>

Suppose $k$ is an integer with $k\ge2$ and $f$ has $k+1$ continuous
derivatives on a neighbourhood of $a$. If

$$
f^{(j)}(a)=0\quad(1\le j<k),
\qquad f^{(k)}(a)\ne0.
$$

then Taylor's theorem gives

$$
f(x)-f(a)
=\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+R_{k+1}(x),
\qquad
R_{k+1}(x)=O\bigl(|x-a|^{k+1}\bigr),\qquad
R_{k+1}(x)=o\bigl(|x-a|^k\bigr).
$$

Thus, locally:

- if $k$ is even and $f^{(k)}(a)>0$, $a$ is a local minimum;
- if $k$ is even and $f^{(k)}(a)<0$, $a$ is a local maximum;
- if $k$ is odd, the leading term changes sign across $a$, giving a horizontal point of
  inflection.

</Statement>

Here $O(|x-a|^{k+1})$ means an error bounded by a constant times
$|x-a|^{k+1}$ near $a$ and $o(|x-a|^k)$ means an error whose ratio to $|x-a|^k$
tends to zero.

The test requires $k\ge2$, a genuinely first nonzero derivative and enough continuity
to ensure that the remainder is $o(|x-a|^k)$. If all derivatives examined vanish,
those terms give no classification.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="stationary-point-test">

Taylor's theorem gives

$$
f(x)-f(a)
=\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k+R_{k+1}(x),
$$

because the lower-order terms vanish. Since $f^{(k+1)}$ is continuous near $a$, it is
bounded on a smaller neighbourhood, so the remainder estimate gives
$R_{k+1}(x)=O(|x-a|^{k+1})$ and hence
$R_{k+1}(x)=o(|x-a|^k)$. Therefore the first nonzero term determines
the sign of $f(x)-f(a)$ sufficiently close to $a$.

When $k$ is even, $(x-a)^k$ is positive on both sides of $a$, so the sign of
$f^{(k)}(a)$ determines a minimum or maximum. When $k$ is odd, then $k\ge3$ and the
same expansion applied to $f''$ gives

$$
f''(x)=\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{(k-2)!}(x-a)^{k-2}+o\bigl(|x-a|^{k-2}\bigr).
$$

Since $k-2$ is odd, $f''$ changes sign across $a$; together with $f'(a)=0$, this gives
a horizontal point of inflection.

</Proof>

<Example slug="fifth-order-stationary-point" of="stationary-point-test" difficulty="challenge">

### A fifth-order stationary point

Let

$$
f(x)=x^7-10x^6+40x^5-80x^4+80x^3-32x^2-10.
$$

At $x=2$,

$$
f'(2)=f''(2)=f^{(3)}(2)=f^{(4)}(2)=0,
\qquad
f^{(5)}(2)=480>0.
$$

The first nonzero derivative has odd order, so $x=2$ is a horizontal point of inflection.
Since $f(2)=-10$, the point is $(2,-10)$.

</Example>

<Proposition slug="leading-taylor-coefficients-limit" of="taylors-theorem">

### Comparing the first nonzero Taylor terms

<Statement>

Suppose $m\ge1$, $f$ and $g$ are $m$ times differentiable near $a$,
$f^{(j)}(a)=g^{(j)}(a)=0$ for $0\le j<m$ and
$f^{(m)}(a),g^{(m)}(a)\ne0$. Then

$$
\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}
=\frac{f^{(m)}(a)}{g^{(m)}(a)}.
$$

</Statement>

The leading expansions are

$$
f(x)=\frac{f^{(m)}(a)}{m!}(x-a)^m+o((x-a)^m),
$$

and

$$
g(x)=\frac{g^{(m)}(a)}{m!}(x-a)^m+o((x-a)^m).
$$

The common power $(x-a)^m$ cancels in the quotient. This explains why repeated L'Hôpital
differentiation can work, but the hypotheses of that rule must still be checked.

</Proposition>

## From one polynomial to a sequence

For a fixed $x$, the [Taylor polynomials](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-polynomials) of degree $0,1,2,\ldots$ form a [sequence](/calculus/taylor-series/sequences-and-limits) of approximations. Two questions decide its limit: the [convergence tests](/calculus/taylor-series/convergence-tests) settle whether the series converges and the remainder decides whether the sum is $f(x)$. The [Taylor series](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-series) page combines the two and [power series](/calculus/taylor-series/power-series) adds termwise calculus inside the radius.
