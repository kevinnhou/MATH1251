Most series have no closed-form sum, so this page collects tests that decide whether the [partial sums](/calculus/taylor-series/infinite-series) converge without finding their limit. Begin with the $n$-th term test, then look for a special shape:

1. geometric or telescoping;
2. positive terms suited to an integral or comparison;
3. factorials or exponentials suited to the ratio test;
4. large powers suited to the root test;
5. alternating signs suited first to absolute convergence and then to the alternating-series
   test.

Each test has its own hypotheses and conclusions. An inconclusive limit proves neither convergence nor divergence.

## Benchmark positive series with integrals

When the terms are positive and come from a decreasing function, $a_n=f(n)$, an improper
integral measures the same tail behaviour. The resulting $p$-series test is the benchmark
for algebraic decay.

<Recall of="infinite-series" />

<Theorem slug="integral-test" of="infinite-series">

### Integral test

<Statement>

Suppose $f$ is positive, continuous and decreasing for $x\ge N$, where $N$ is a
positive integer and $a_n=f(n)$. Then

$$
\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}a_n
$$

and

$$
\int_N^\infty f(x)\,dx
$$

either both converge or both diverge.

</Statement>

Positivity and monotonicity give, for $m\ge N$,

$$
\int_N^{m+1}f(x)\,dx
\le\sum_{n=N}^{m}f(n)
\le f(N)+\int_N^{m}f(x)\,dx.
$$

The integral and partial sums therefore have the same convergence behaviour.

</Theorem>

<Theorem slug="p-series-test" of="integral-test">

### The $p$-series test

<Statement>

For real $p$,

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^p}
\begin{cases}
\text{converges},&p>1,\\
\text{diverges},&p\le1.
\end{cases}
$$

</Statement>

When $p\le0$, the terms do not tend to zero. When $p>0$, the integral test uses

$$
\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^p},
$$

which is finite exactly when $p>1$.
The special case $p=2$ is evaluated exactly in [Euler's Basel
sum](/calculus/double-integrals/basel-sum).

</Theorem>

<Example slug="logarithmic-integral-test" of="integral-test" difficulty="exam">

### A logarithmic borderline

For

$$
\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac1{n\ln n},
$$

the corresponding integral is

$$
\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x\ln x}
=\lim_{b\to\infty}\bigl(\ln(\ln b)-\ln(\ln2)\bigr)
=\infty.
$$

Therefore the series diverges.

</Example>

## Compare with a known tail

Comparison tests suit terms that can be bounded by, or shown to have the same dominant
behaviour as, the terms of a familiar series.

<Theorem slug="direct-comparison-test" of="infinite-series">

### Direct comparison test

<Statement>

Suppose $0\le a_n\le b_n$ for all sufficiently large $n$. Then:

- if $\sum b_n$ converges, $\sum a_n$ converges;
- if $\sum a_n$ diverges, $\sum b_n$ diverges.

</Statement>

The inequality must point towards the conclusion. A smaller positive series is controlled by
a convergent larger series; a larger positive series inherits divergence from a divergent
smaller series.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="direct-comparison-examples" of="direct-comparison-test" difficulty="exam">

### Direct comparisons

For

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac n{n^3+2},
$$

we have

$$
0<\frac n{n^3+2}\le\frac n{n^3}=\frac1{n^2}.
$$

The $p$-series test proves convergence.

For

$$
\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac1{\sqrt{n^2-2}},
$$

we have $\sqrt{n^2-2}<n$, hence

$$
\frac1{\sqrt{n^2-2}}>\frac1n.
$$

The given series dominates the divergent harmonic series and therefore diverges.

For

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\sqrt{n^2+3}},
$$

the direct comparison with $1/n$ points in the wrong direction. Limit comparison gives the
right conclusion.

</Example>

Direct comparison needs an inequality with the useful direction. Limit comparison replaces
that inequality with a finite positive ratio, which is often easier to calculate.

<Theorem slug="limit-comparison-test" of="infinite-series">

### Limit comparison test

<Statement>

If $a_n>0$ and $b_n>0$ for all sufficiently large $n$ and

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n}=K
$$

with $0<K<\infty$, then

$$
\sum a_n\text{ converges}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sum b_n\text{ converges}.
$$

</Statement>

The finite positive ratio means that $a_n$ and $b_n$ are eventually bounded above and below
by positive multiples of one another. If the ratio is $0$, only one-way conclusions are
available: convergence of $\sum b_n$ implies convergence of $\sum a_n$ and divergence of
$\sum a_n$ implies divergence of $\sum b_n$.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="limit-comparison-examples" of="limit-comparison-test" difficulty="exam">

### Dominant-term comparisons

For

$$
\frac{(2n+1)/(n^3-2)}{1/n^2}\longrightarrow2,
$$

the series

$$
\sum\frac{2n+1}{n^3-2}
$$

converges like $\sum1/n^2$.

For

$$
\frac{(n-1)/(n^4+1)^{1/3}}{1/n^{1/3}}
\longrightarrow1,
$$

the series

$$
\sum\frac{n-1}{(n^4+1)^{1/3}}
$$

diverges like $\sum1/n^{1/3}$.

Also,

$$
\frac{\dfrac{3n^2+5n}{2^n(n^2+1)}}{3/2^n}
\longrightarrow1,
$$

so the series has the same convergence behaviour as a geometric series.

Finally, for

$$
\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\ln n}{\sqrt{n^3+1}},
$$

use $\ln n\le n^{1/4}$ for sufficiently large $n$:

$$
\frac{\ln n}{\sqrt{n^3+1}}
\le\frac{n^{1/4}}{n^{3/2}}
=\frac1{n^{5/4}}.
$$

The series converges by direct comparison.

</Example>

## Choose ratio or root for factorials and powers

The ratio test is designed for factorials and exponentials because consecutive terms cancel
their repeated factors. The root test is designed for large $n$-th powers because taking an
$n$-th root exposes the base. Both tests reduce the question to whether a number is below or
above $1$.

<Theorem slug="ratio-test" of="infinite-series">

### Ratio test

<Statement>

For a series of positive terms, suppose

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=L.
$$

Then:

- $L<1$ implies convergence;
- $L>1$ implies divergence;
- $L=1$ gives no conclusion.

For sign-changing terms, apply the test to $|a_n|$ when testing absolute convergence.

</Statement>

</Theorem>

<Example slug="ratio-test-examples" of="ratio-test" difficulty="routine">

### Factorials and exponentials

For $\sum n^2/2^n$,

$$
\frac{(n+1)^2/2^{n+1}}{n^2/2^n}
=\frac12\left(1+\frac1n\right)^2
\longrightarrow\frac12.
$$

The series converges.

For $\sum1/n!$,

$$
\frac{1/(n+1)!}{1/n!}=\frac1{n+1}\longrightarrow0,
$$

so the series converges.

For $\sum n!/2^n$,

$$
\frac{(n+1)!/2^{n+1}}{n!/2^n}
=\frac{n+1}{2}\longrightarrow\infty.
$$

The terms do not approach zero, so the series diverges.

</Example>

<Theorem slug="root-test" of="infinite-series">

### Root test

<Statement>

If

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}=L,
$$

then:

- $L<1$ implies absolute convergence;
- $L>1$ implies divergence;
- $L=1$ gives no conclusion.

</Statement>

If the ratio test gives $1$, switching to the root test does not automatically help: the
root test may also be inconclusive.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="root-test-examples" of="root-test" difficulty="exam">

### Large powers

For

$$
a_n=\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^{n^2},
$$

we have

$$
\sqrt[n]{a_n}
=\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^n
=\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{-n}
\longrightarrow e^{-1}<1.
$$

Therefore $\sum a_n$ converges.

For

$$
a_n=(\arctan n)^n,
$$

$$
\sqrt[n]{a_n}=\arctan n\longrightarrow\frac\pi2>1.
$$

Therefore the series diverges.

</Example>

## Use cancellation carefully

Positive-term tests cannot see cancellation between positive and negative terms, so first
test the series of absolute values. If that fails, the alternating-series test may still
prove convergence. Such conditional convergence is fragile: rearranging the terms can
change the sum.

<Definition slug="absolute-convergence">

### Absolute and conditional convergence

<Statement>

A series $\sum a_n$ is absolutely convergent if $\sum|a_n|$ converges. It is
conditionally convergent if $\sum a_n$ converges but $\sum|a_n|$ diverges.

</Statement>

Absolute convergence controls the series without relying on cancellation. For example,
$\sum(-1)^n/n^2$ is absolutely convergent because $\sum1/n^2$ converges, while
$\sum(-1)^n/n$ is a candidate for conditional convergence.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="absolute-convergence-implies-convergence" of="absolute-convergence">

### Absolute convergence implies convergence

<Statement>

If $\sum|a_n|$ converges, then $\sum a_n$ converges.

</Statement>

If $S_n$ are the partial sums, then for $m>n$
$$
|S_m-S_n|
=\left|\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}a_k\right|
\le\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}|a_k|.
$$
The tails of the convergent series $\sum|a_k|$ become arbitrarily small, so any two
sufficiently late partial sums of $\sum a_k$ are arbitrarily close. Completeness of $\mathbb R$
then guarantees that these partial sums approach a finite limit, even when the signs of the
terms vary.

</Theorem>

<Theorem slug="alternating-series-test" of="infinite-series">

### Alternating-series test

<Statement>

If $b_n\ge0$, $b_{n+1}\le b_n$ and $b_n\to0$, then

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^n b_n
$$

converges.

</Statement>

The successive partial sums lie on opposite sides of the limiting value and close in on it.
The magnitudes need only be eventually decreasing, since finitely many initial terms do not
affect convergence.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="alternating-harmonic-series" of="alternating-series-test,absolute-convergence" difficulty="exam">

### Conditional convergence of the alternating harmonic series

The positive magnitudes $1/n$ decrease to zero, so

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n
$$

converges by the alternating-series test. But its absolute series is the harmonic series,
which diverges. The alternating harmonic series is therefore conditionally convergent.

</Example>

<Theorem slug="series-rearrangement-theorem" of="absolute-convergence">

### Rearranging a series

<Statement>

Every rearrangement of an absolutely convergent series has the same sum. A conditionally
convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any prescribed real number, or to diverge
to $+\infty$ or $-\infty$.

</Statement>

The second statement is the Riemann rearrangement theorem.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="rearranged-alternating-harmonic" of="series-rearrangement-theorem" difficulty="challenge">

### Rearranging the alternating harmonic series

Let

$$
S=1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\frac15-\cdots.
$$

Take one positive term followed by two negative terms:

$$
1-\frac12-\frac14+\frac13-\frac16-\frac18
+\frac15-\frac1{10}-\frac1{12}+\cdots.
$$

Grouping in blocks gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\left(1-\frac12-\frac14\right)
+\left(\frac13-\frac16-\frac18\right)+\cdots\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}
\left(\frac1{2k-1}-\frac1{4k-2}-\frac1{4k}\right)
=\frac S2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The changed sum is a consequence of conditional convergence, not an algebraic contradiction.

</Example>
