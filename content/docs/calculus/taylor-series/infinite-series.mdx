This page gives meaning to an infinite sum

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n=a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots
$$

by taking the limit of its finite totals and evaluates the series whose totals have an exact formula. A [sequence](/calculus/taylor-series/sequences-and-limits) records individual terms; a series asks whether their accumulated totals settle.

## Define an infinite sum through partial sums

The partial sums are finite, so whether they converge is a question about sequences.

<Recall of="sequence-convergence" />

<Definition slug="partial-sums">

### Partial sums

<Statement>

For a sequence $\{a_n\}$, the $k$-th partial sum is

$$
s_k=\sum_{n=1}^{k}a_n.
$$

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Definition slug="infinite-series" of="partial-sums">

### Infinite series

<Statement>

The series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n$ converges to $L$ precisely when its partial sums satisfy

$$
\lim_{k\to\infty}s_k=L,
\qquad
s_k=\sum_{n=1}^{k}a_n.
$$

In that case,

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n=L.
$$

</Statement>

The terms and the series answer different questions:

- $a_n$ asks what happens to one term far out in the sequence;
- $\sum a_n$ asks what happens to the accumulated totals.

A convergent series must have $a_n\to0$, but this is not sufficient: the harmonic series
below is the standard counterexample.

</Definition>

<Proposition slug="finite-series-changes" of="infinite-series">

### Finite changes do not affect convergence

<Statement>

Adding, removing or changing finitely many terms does not affect whether a series converges.
If two series converge, their termwise sum and scalar multiples also converge.

</Statement>

Changing finitely many terms changes the partial sums by a fixed amount after the changed
terms have been passed. A fixed amount can change the sum, but not whether the partial-sum
sequence has a finite limit.

</Proposition>

## Series with exact algebraic structure

Some series can be handled without a comparison theorem because their finite partial sums
have a clear pattern. Geometric series use a closed formula; telescoping series use
cancellation. In both cases, write the finite sum first and take its limit afterwards.

<Method slug="geometric-series-method" of="infinite-series">

### Evaluate a geometric series

<Statement>

For $r\ne1$, first compute the finite partial sum

$$
1+r+\cdots+r^N=\frac{1-r^{N+1}}{1-r}.
$$

Then let $N\to\infty$. The geometric series converges exactly when $|r|<1$ and in that
case

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^n=\frac1{1-r}.
$$

</Statement>

If $|r|>1$, the terms do not approach zero. If $r=1$, the partial sums grow without bound,
and if $r=-1$, they oscillate.

</Method>

<Example slug="geometric-series-example" of="geometric-series-method" difficulty="routine">

### A geometric sum

For $r=1/2$,

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{2^n}
=\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots
=\frac{1/2}{1-1/2}=1.
$$

</Example>

The geometric formula works because every term is a fixed multiple of the preceding one.
Telescoping uses a different kind of repetition: neighbouring terms cancel, leaving only a
small boundary contribution.

<Method slug="telescoping-series-method" of="infinite-series">

### Evaluate a telescoping series

<Statement>

If a term can be written as

$$
a_n=b_n-b_{n+1},
$$

then the finite partial sum is

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_n
=\sum_{n=0}^{N}(b_n-b_{n+1})
=b_0-b_{N+1}.
$$

Take the limit of the single uncancelled tail to decide convergence and find the sum.

</Statement>

Cancelling inside an infinite expression, without first writing the finite sum, can hide a
divergent tail.

</Method>

<Example slug="factorial-telescoping-series" of="telescoping-series-method" difficulty="exam">

### A factorial telescoping series

Since

$$
\frac{k}{(k+1)!}
=\frac1{k!}-\frac1{(k+1)!},
$$

we have

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{k}{(k+1)!}
=1-\frac1{(N+1)!}
\longrightarrow1.
$$

Therefore

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k}{(k+1)!}=1.
$$

</Example>

## Why vanishing terms are not enough

For a general series there is no exact partial-sum formula, so the first question is whether
the partial sums converge at all. The terms must vanish, but small terms can still
accumulate without bound.

<Theorem slug="nth-term-series-test" of="infinite-series">

### The $n$-th term test

<Statement>

If

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n\ne0
$$

or the limit does not exist, then

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n
$$

diverges. Equivalently,

$$
\sum a_n\text{ convergent}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_n\to0.
$$

</Statement>

This is a divergence test, not a convergence test: when $a_n\to0$, more information is
needed. Apply it first, because a nonzero term limit settles the question at once.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="nth-term-divergence" of="nth-term-series-test" difficulty="routine">

### When the terms do not vanish

Because

$$
\frac{2n}{3n+1}\longrightarrow\frac23\ne0,
$$

the series

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2n}{3n+1}
$$

diverges.

</Example>

<Example slug="harmonic-series-divergence" of="nth-term-series-test" difficulty="exam">

### The harmonic series

Although

$$
\frac1n\longrightarrow0,
$$

the harmonic series diverges. Group its terms:

$$
1+\frac12
+\left(\frac13+\frac14\right)
+\left(\frac15+\cdots+\frac18\right)+\cdots.
$$

Every block after the first has total at least $1/2$: the block from
$2^{j-1}+1$ to $2^j$ contains $2^{j-1}$ terms, each at least $1/2^j$. Hence the partial
sums increase by at least $1/2$ from one block to the next and cannot converge.

</Example>

The contrasting series

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}
$$

does converge; for $n\ge2$,

$$
\frac1{n^2}<\frac1{n(n-1)}
=\frac1{n-1}-\frac1n,
$$

so comparison with a telescoping series proves convergence. The exact value
$\pi^2/6$ is not needed for this convergence argument.

Only special patterns give exact sums. For most series the task is to decide convergence without finding the sum, which is the job of the [convergence tests](/calculus/taylor-series/convergence-tests). The same tests later find the radius and endpoints of each [power series](/calculus/taylor-series/power-series).
