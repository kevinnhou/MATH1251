### Sequences and series

#### (a)

Let $\{a_n\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$ be a sequence of non-negative real numbers. Which of the following ensures that $\{a_n\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$ converges? Select all that apply.

- $\{a_n\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$ is bounded below and non-decreasing.
- There exists $L\in\mathbb R$ such that for each $\varepsilon>0$ there corresponds $K\in\mathbb N$ such that $|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$ whenever $n>K$.
- $\sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$ exists.
- $\{a_n\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$ is bounded above and non-increasing.
- $\inf\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$ exists.

#### (b)

Consider the sequence $\{a_n\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$ given by

$$
a_0=7,\qquad
a_{n+1}=8-\sqrt{38-a_n}
\qquad(n\ge0).
$$

Given that this sequence is decreasing and converges, find

$$
L=\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n.
$$

#### (c)

Let $\{c_n\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$ and $\{d_n\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$ be sequences of strictly positive real numbers satisfying

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}c_n=L_1<1,
\qquad
\lim_{n\to\infty}d_n=L_2>1.
$$

For $n\ge1$, define

$$
b_n=\left(\frac{c_n}{d_n}\right)^n.
$$

Determine which statements are true:

- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n$ is convergent by the integral test.
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n$ is divergent by the ratio test.
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n$ is divergent by the root test.
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n$ is convergent by the ratio test.
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n$ is convergent by the root test.
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n$ is divergent by the integral test.

#### (d)

Let $\{a_n\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$ and $\{b_n\}_{n=0}^{\infty}$ be sequences such that

$$
a_n=\frac{2}{n^\alpha}+O(n^{-\alpha-1})
\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,
$$

and

$$
b_n=\frac{9}{n^\alpha}+O(n^{-\alpha-1})
\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,
$$

where $\alpha$ is a positive integer. Since these formulas describe only
the tail, the associated series may be started at $n=1$; finitely many initial
terms do not affect convergence.

Determine which of the following statements hold:

- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n$ converges if and only if, $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n$ diverges.
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n$ converges or diverges independently of whether $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n$ converges or not.
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n$ converges if and only if, $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n$ converges.
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n$ diverges if and only if, $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n$ converges.
- $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n$ diverges if and only if, $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n$ diverges.

#### Solution

##### (a)

The second statement is exactly the $\varepsilon$-$N$ definition of convergence, so it is sufficient.

The fourth statement is also sufficient. Being bounded above gives no lower bound, but the terms are non-negative, so $a_n\ge0$ supplies one and the Monotone Convergence Theorem gives convergence.

The other three statements are not sufficient:

- a non-decreasing sequence that is only bounded below can be $a_n=n$;
- a finite supremum does not prevent oscillation, as in $a_n=0,1,0,1,\ldots$;
- a finite infimum also does not prevent the same oscillation.

Therefore the correct selections are

$$
{\text{the $\varepsilon$-$N$ statement and the bounded-above, non-increasing statement}.}
$$

##### (b)

Because the sequence converges and the square-root function is continuous on its domain, taking limits in the recurrence gives

$$
L=8-\sqrt{38-L}.
$$

Before squaring, observe that

$$
\sqrt{38-L}=8-L\ge0,
$$

so any admissible limit must satisfy $L\le8$. Squaring now gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
38-L&=(8-L)^2\\
&=64-16L+L^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

and hence

$$
L^2-15L+26=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
(L-2)(L-13)=0.
$$

The algebraic candidates are $L=2$ and $L=13$, but $L=13$ violates the restriction $L\le8$ introduced before squaring. The remaining value checks in the original recurrence:

$$
8-\sqrt{38-2}=8-6=2.
$$

Therefore

$$
{L=2}.
$$

##### (c)

For $n\ge1$, the $n$th root of $b_n$ is

$$
\sqrt[n]{b_n}=\frac{c_n}{d_n},
$$

because $c_n,d_n>0$. Therefore

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{b_n}
=\frac{L_1}{L_2}<1.
$$

The root test proves convergence. The $n=0$ term, if included, is just one
finite initial term and has no effect on convergence. The given information
does not determine the ratio-test limit or provide the smoothness and
monotonicity needed for the integral test. Thus the only correct statement is

$$
{\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n
\text{ is convergent by the root test}.}
$$

##### (d)

The error terms are smaller than the displayed leading terms:

$$
O(n^{-\alpha-1})=o(n^{-\alpha}).
$$

Consequently,

$$
a_n\sim\frac{2}{n^\alpha},
\qquad
b_n\sim\frac{9}{n^\alpha},
$$

so the terms are eventually positive. Their ratio satisfies

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{b_n}{a_n}
=\lim_{n\to\infty}
\frac{9n^{-\alpha}+O(n^{-\alpha-1})}
{2n^{-\alpha}+O(n^{-\alpha-1})}
=\frac92.
$$

Since this limit is finite and strictly positive, the Limit Comparison Test gives

$$
\sum b_n\text{ converges}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sum a_n\text{ converges}.
$$

If $\alpha=1$, both tails behave like the harmonic series and diverge; if $\alpha>1$, both behave like a convergent $p$-series. The correct selections are the third and fifth listed statements. The third is

$$
{\displaystyle
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n\text{ converges if and only if }
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n\text{ converges}.}
$$

Taking logical complements gives the fifth:

$$
{\displaystyle
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n\text{ diverges if and only if }
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n\text{ diverges}.}
$$

The sequence parts separate the definition of convergence from the sufficient conditions of monotonicity and boundedness. The series parts use the root test for exponential-type terms and limit comparison for matching power laws. In both, only the tail matters.

</Example>

### Taylor polynomials and a Lagrange remainder

Aaron and Cindy are investigating a function $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ and its first eight Taylor polynomials about $x_0=0.3$:

$$
p_0(x),p_1(x),\ldots,p_7(x).
$$

Aaron compares the first six polynomials and records these features near $x_0$:
$p_1$ is decreasing at $x_0$; $p_2=p_1$; and, just to the right of $x_0$,
$p_3-p_2$ and $p_4-p_3$ are positive, while $p_5-p_4$ is negative.
These observations replace the plots, which are not included here.

#### (a)

##### (i)

Select all derivatives of $f$ at $0.3$ that are zero:

$$
f'(0.3),\quad f''(0.3),\quad f^{(3)}(0.3),\quad
f^{(4)}(0.3),\quad f^{(5)}(0.3).
$$

##### (ii)

Select all derivatives of $f$ at $0.3$ that are positive.

##### (iii)

Select all derivatives of $f$ at $0.3$ that are negative.

#### (b)

Aaron tells Cindy that the coefficient of $(x-0.3)^6$ in $p_6(x)$ is

$$
a_6=-6.8,
$$

and the coefficient of $(x-0.3)^7$ in $p_7(x)$ is

$$
a_7=5.
$$

They both agree that the graph of $f^{(6)}$ looks like a straight line and decide to assume this is true.

They consider $x_1=0.5$ and want to use Lagrange's form of the remainder to find a bound on

$$
\left|f(0.5)-p_5(0.5)\right|.
$$

##### (i)

Is knowing the value of $a_7$ necessary to calculate the error bound found using Lagrange's form of the remainder? Select all true statements:

- The value of $a_7$ is necessary for these particular values of $a_6$ and $x_1$.
- The value of $a_7$ is not necessary for these particular values of $a_6$ and $x_1$.
- The value of $a_7$ would be necessary, regardless of the values of $a_6$ and $x_1$.
- The value of $a_7$ would be necessary for some values of $a_6$ and $x_1$ and unnecessary for others.

##### (ii)

Find the smallest error bound guaranteed by Lagrange's form of the remainder:

$$
\left|f(0.5)-p_5(0.5)\right|\le \ ? 
$$

#### (c)

The fact that the graph in part (b) looks like a straight line with non-zero slope suggests that $f$ is a polynomial of degree $7$. Discuss this conclusion, making clear what follows from an exact global assumption and what a graph alone can establish.

#### Solution

For a Taylor polynomial about $x_0=0.3$,

$$
p_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(0.3)}{k!}(x-0.3)^k.
$$

The coefficient of $(x-0.3)^k$ is therefore $f^{(k)}(0.3)/k!$. Since $k!>0$, the sign of that coefficient is the sign of the corresponding derivative.

##### (a)

Consecutive Taylor polynomials differ by a single term:

$$
p_k(x)-p_{k-1}(x)
=\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k.
$$

The polynomial $p_1$ is decreasing at $x_0$, so

$$
f'(0.3)<0.
$$

Because $p_2=p_1$, its quadratic coefficient vanishes, so

$$
f''(0.3)=0.
$$

For $x>x_0$, the factor $(x-x_0)^k$ is positive. The recorded signs of
$p_3-p_2$, $p_4-p_3$ and $p_5-p_4$ therefore give

$$
f^{(3)}(0.3)>0,\qquad
f^{(4)}(0.3)>0,\qquad
f^{(5)}(0.3)<0.
$$

Thus the selections are:

- zero: ${f''(0.3)}$;
- positive: ${f^{(3)}(0.3),f^{(4)}(0.3)}$;
- negative: ${f'(0.3),f^{(5)}(0.3)}$.

##### (b)(i)

The Lagrange remainder for $p_5$ involves $f^{(6)}(c)$ for some $c$ between $0.3$ and $0.5$. The value $a_6$ gives $f^{(6)}(0.3)$, but the assumed straight-line graph of $f^{(6)}$ also needs its slope to determine the largest absolute value on the interval. That slope is determined by $a_7$.

For these particular values, the first statement is true:

$$
{\text{$a_7$ is necessary for these particular values of $a_6$ and $x_1$.}}
$$

The fourth statement is also true in general: if $x_1=x_0$, the error is zero
without knowing $a_7$, whereas over a nontrivial interval the slope can change the
maximum of $|f^{(6)}|$. The second and third statements are false.

##### (b)(ii)

The Taylor coefficients give

$$
f^{(6)}(0.3)=6!a_6=720(-6.8)=-4896
$$

and

$$
f^{(7)}(0.3)=7!a_7=5040(5)=25200.
$$

Because $f^{(6)}$ is assumed to be a straight line,

$$
f^{(6)}(x)
=-4896+25200(x-0.3).
$$

On the interval $[0.3,0.5]$,

$$
f^{(6)}(0.3)=-4896,\qquad
f^{(6)}(0.5)=-4896+25200(0.2)=144.
$$

A linear function takes its extreme values on a closed interval at the endpoints. Therefore

$$
\max_{0.3\le c\le0.5}|f^{(6)}(c)|
=\max\{|-4896|,|144|\}
=4896.
$$

Lagrange's form of the remainder for the degree-five polynomial is

$$
f(0.5)-p_5(0.5)
=\frac{f^{(6)}(c)}{6!}(0.5-0.3)^6
$$

for some $c\in(0.3,0.5)$. Consequently,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|f(0.5)-p_5(0.5)\right|
&\le \frac{4896}{6!}(0.2)^6\\
&=6.8(0.2)^6\\
&={0.0004352}.
\end{aligned}
$$

##### (c)

If $f^{(6)}$ is assumed to be exactly a straight line with non-zero slope on all of $\mathbb R$, then $f^{(7)}$ is a non-zero constant and $f^{(8)}=0$. Integrating this identity repeatedly shows that $f$ is a polynomial of degree exactly seven:

$$
{f\text{ is a polynomial of degree }7}.
$$

A graph that merely looks linear, or is known to be linear only on the interval used for the
error bound, does not establish this global conclusion; it supports the assumption locally
but cannot determine $f$ outside that interval.

The plots encode derivative signs through Taylor coefficients. The sixth and seventh coefficients turn the straight-line assumption for $f^{(6)}$ into a bound on its maximum, which is the quantity Lagrange's remainder needs.

</Example>

### A power series and its endpoints

It is “play with series” night and Chelsea and Finn ask you to consider the
power series

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt n}+\frac{(-1)^n}{n}\right)
\frac{(x-7)^n}{6^n}. \tag{1}
$$

#### (a)

Let

$$
a_n=\frac{1}{\sqrt n}+\frac{(-1)^n}{n}.
$$

Which of the following statements is true?

- $a_n>0$ for all $n\ge1$;
- $a_n$ takes both positive and negative values at an infinite number of positive integers $n$;
- $a_n>0$ for all $n>1$;
- $a_n>0$ if $n$ is odd and $a_n<0$ if $n$ is even.

#### (b)

Chelsea asks you to use an appropriate criterion to find the open interval of
convergence $I=(a,b)$ for the power series (1) and to enter the values of $a$
and $b$.

#### (c)

Determine what happens at the endpoints $x=a$ and $x=b$. Select the true
statements:

- The power series (1) at $x=b$ is convergent.
- The power series (1) at $x=a$ is not convergent.
- The power series (1) at $x=b$ is not convergent.
- The power series (1) at $x=a$ is convergent.

In a few sentences, justify your answers, explaining your reasoning.

#### Solution

For $n=1$,

$$
a_1=1-1=0.
$$

If $n$ is even, then

$$
a_n=\frac1{\sqrt n}+\frac1n>0.
$$

If $n$ is odd and $n>1$, then

$$
a_n=\frac1{\sqrt n}-\frac1n
=\frac{\sqrt n-1}{n}>0.
$$

Hence the correct statement in part (a) is

$$
{a_n>0\text{ for all }n>1.}
$$

For $n>1$,

$$
\frac1{\sqrt n}-\frac1n
\le a_n
\le\frac1{\sqrt n}+\frac1n.
$$

Both bounds have $n$th root tending to $1$, so

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}=1.
$$

Apply the root test to the general term of (1):

$$
\sqrt[n]{
\left|a_n\frac{(x-7)^n}{6^n}\right|}
=\sqrt[n]{|a_n|}\frac{|x-7|}{6}
\longrightarrow\frac{|x-7|}{6}.
$$

The series converges absolutely when

$$
\frac{|x-7|}{6}<1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
|x-7|<6.
$$

Thus the open interval is

$$
{I=(1,13),\qquad a=1,\quad b=13.}
$$

The root test does not decide the endpoints, so they must be substituted into
the original series.

At $x=b=13$, the factor $(x-7)/6$ is $1$ and the series becomes

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n.
$$

For $n\ge4$,

$$
a_n\ge\frac1{\sqrt n}-\frac1n
\ge\frac1{2\sqrt n}.
$$

The comparison series $\sum 1/\sqrt n$ diverges, so the series at $x=b$ is
not convergent.

At $x=a=1$, the factor $(x-7)/6$ is $-1$ and the series becomes

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^na_n
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n}
+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1n.
$$

The first series converges by the alternating-series test, while the second
is the divergent harmonic series. Therefore the series at $x=a$ is also not
convergent.

Thus the endpoint statements are

$$
{
\text{the series at }x=a\text{ is not convergent and the series at }x=b
\text{ is not convergent}.
}
$$

The radius depends only on the $n$th-root limit of the slowly varying
coefficients, which is $1$. The endpoints need separate arguments: at the left
endpoint the alternating $1/\sqrt n$ part converges but the harmonic part does
not and at the right endpoint the positive $1/\sqrt n$ behaviour forces
divergence.

</Example>
