A power series starts with coefficients and asks where their infinite polynomial
converges. This reverses the [Taylor-series](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-series)
viewpoint: a Taylor series starts with a function, derives coefficients and asks
where the series equals that function.

## From coefficients to a function

A power series defines a function only where it converges, so find that set before
studying its algebra or calculus. Convergence has its [partial-sum meaning](/calculus/taylor-series/infinite-series) and the [convergence tests](/calculus/taylor-series/convergence-tests) find where it holds.

<Recall of="infinite-series" />

<Definition slug="power-series">

### Power series

<Statement>

A power series centred at $a$ is a series of the form

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_k(x-a)^k.
$$

</Statement>

The series itself may define a new function at every value of $x$ where it converges.

</Definition>

<Definition slug="radius-of-convergence" of="power-series">

### Radius and interval of convergence

<Statement>

The radius of convergence $R$ of

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_k(x-a)^k
$$

is the number such that the series converges absolutely when $|x-a|<R$ and diverges when
$|x-a|>R$.

The interval of convergence is the open interval $(a-R,a+R)$ together with whichever
endpoints converge.

</Statement>

With $R=0$ the series converges only at its centre, while $R=\infty$ gives
convergence for every real $x$. Here $R$ denotes a radius; the [Taylor remainder](/calculus/taylor-series/taylors-theorem)
is written separately as $R_{n+1}(x)$.

</Definition>

## Find the radius and test the endpoints

The radius gives the open interval where the power series converges, but it does not settle
the boundary. At each endpoint the power series becomes an ordinary series, to be tested
with the [convergence tests](/calculus/taylor-series/convergence-tests).

<Theorem slug="power-series-convergence-region" of="radius-of-convergence">

### Convergence has a central interval

<Statement>

Every real power series has a radius $R\in[0,\infty]$ with the following behaviour:

- absolute convergence for $|x-a|<R$;
- divergence for $|x-a|>R$;
- separate endpoint behaviour when $0<R<\infty$.

</Statement>

To see why the region has this shape, suppose the series converges at some $x_0\ne a$.
Then its terms satisfy $|c_k(x_0-a)^k|\le M$ for some $M>0$. At any point with
$|x-a|<|x_0-a|$,

$$
|c_k(x-a)^k|
\le M\left|\frac{x-a}{x_0-a}\right|^k.
$$

The right-hand side is geometric with ratio below $1$, so convergence at one point
forces absolute convergence at every point closer to the centre.

</Theorem>

<Method slug="find-power-series-interval" of="power-series-convergence-region">

### Find the interval of convergence

<Statement>

For a power series centred at $a$:

1. apply the ratio or root test when its limit exists and is decisive; otherwise estimate
   the coefficients by another convergence test;
2. rewrite that condition as $|x-a|<R$;
3. record the open interval $(a-R,a+R)$;
4. if $0<R<\infty$, substitute $x=a-R$ and $x=a+R$ into the original series;
5. apply the appropriate ordinary series test at each finite endpoint.

</Statement>

The endpoint series may be geometric, harmonic, alternating or something else and the two
endpoints need not behave alike. If $R=0$ there are no endpoints to test;
if $R=\infty$ every real $x$ is already inside the radius.

</Method>

<Example slug="power-series-open-interval" of="find-power-series-interval" difficulty="exam">

### A power series with two divergent endpoints

Consider

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(x-3)^k}{3^k+2}.
$$

Writing its term as $a_k$, the ratio test gives

$$
\left|\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}\right|
=|x-3|\frac{3^k+2}{3^{k+1}+2}
\longrightarrow\frac{|x-3|}{3}.
$$

Therefore the series converges when $|x-3|<3$, giving the possible interval $(0,6)$.

At $x=0$,

$$
a_k=\frac{(-3)^k}{3^k+2},
\qquad |a_k|\longrightarrow1,
$$

so the terms do not tend to zero. At $x=6$,

$$
a_k=\frac{3^k}{3^k+2}\longrightarrow1,
$$

so the terms again fail the $n$-th term test. The interval of convergence is

$$
{(0,6)}.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="power-series-half-open-interval" of="find-power-series-interval" difficulty="exam">

### A power series with one convergent endpoint

Consider

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{4^k}{k}(x-2)^k.
$$

The ratio test gives

$$
\left|\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}\right|
=4|x-2|\frac{k}{k+1}
\longrightarrow4|x-2|.
$$

Thus $R=1/4$, with possible interval $(7/4,9/4)$.

At $x=7/4$,

$$
\frac{4^k}{k}\left(-\frac14\right)^k
=\frac{(-1)^k}{k},
$$

which converges by the alternating-series test. At $x=9/4$,

$$
\frac{4^k}{k}\left(\frac14\right)^k
=\frac1k,
$$

which diverges. Hence

$$
{\left[\frac74,\frac94\right)}.
$$

</Example>

## Algebra inside a common radius

Inside a common interval of absolute convergence, power series can be added and multiplied
like polynomials.

<Theorem slug="power-series-algebra" of="power-series">

### Algebra of power series

<Statement>

Inside a common interval where the power series converge absolutely, if

$$
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_k(x-a)^k,
\qquad
g(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_k(x-a)^k,
$$

then

$$
f(x)\pm g(x)
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(a_k\pm b_k)(x-a)^k,
$$

and

$$
f(x)g(x)
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_jb_{k-j}\right)(x-a)^k.
$$

</Statement>

The second formula is the Cauchy product. The coefficient of $(x-a)^k$ collects every
pair of terms whose powers add to $k$. Products at endpoints require separate justification
when endpoint convergence is only conditional.

</Theorem>

## Calculus inside the radius

Inside the open interval of convergence, a power series can be differentiated and integrated
term by term.

<Theorem slug="termwise-power-series-calculus" of="power-series">

### Differentiate and integrate term by term

<Statement>

Inside the radius of convergence,

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_k(x-a)^k
=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}ka_k(x-a)^{k-1},
$$

and

$$
\int\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_k(x-a)^k\,dx
=C+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_k}{k+1}(x-a)^{k+1}.
$$

Both resulting power series have the same open radius of convergence as the original one.

</Statement>

On every closed interval $[a-r,a+r]$ with $0<r<R$, the original series and its
differentiated series converge uniformly. This local uniform control justifies the
termwise operations; pointwise convergence alone would not.

Uniform convergence on a set means that for every tolerance $\varepsilon>0$, there
is one index $N$ such that $|S_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ for every $n>N$ and every
input $x$ in the set. Pointwise convergence may require a different $N$ at each
input.

The theorem concerns only the open interval. Uniform convergence at either endpoint is
a separate question and endpoint behaviour may change after differentiating or
integrating.

If

$$
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_k(x-a)^k,
$$

then evaluating derivatives at the centre gives

$$
f^{(j)}(a)=j!a_j.
$$

Consequently, the power series is the Taylor series of the function it defines.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="recover-cosine-from-sine-series" of="termwise-power-series-calculus" difficulty="routine">

### Recovering $\cos x$ from $\sin x$

Termwise differentiation of

$$
\sin x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}
$$

gives

$$
\cos x
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="sum-k-squared-x-k" of="termwise-power-series-calculus" difficulty="exam">

### Find $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k^2x^k$

Start with the geometric series:

$$
\frac1{1-x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^k,
\qquad |x|<1.
$$

Differentiate and multiply by $x$:

$$
\frac{x}{(1-x)^2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}kx^k.
$$

Differentiate once more and multiply by $x$:

$$
\frac{x(1+x)}{(1-x)^3}
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k^2x^k,
\qquad |x|<1.
$$

Therefore

$$
{
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k^2x^k
=\frac{x(1+x)}{(1-x)^3},
\qquad |x|<1.
}
$$

</Example>

## Why pointwise convergence is not enough

The termwise calculus theorem is special to power series. For a general sequence of
functions, a limit at each fixed input does not control the derivatives.

<Proposition slug="pointwise-convergence-not-termwise-calculus" of="termwise-power-series-calculus">

### Pointwise convergence is not enough

<Statement>

Convergence at each fixed $x$ (called pointwise convergence) does not, by itself,
justify differentiating the limit term by term.

</Statement>

Consider

$$
f_n(x)=\frac{\sin(n^2x)}n.
$$

For every fixed $x$,

$$
|f_n(x)|\le\frac1n\longrightarrow0,
$$

so $f_n(x)\to f(x)=0$. However,

$$
f_n'(x)=n\cos(n^2x),
\qquad
f_n'(0)=n,
$$

which does not converge to $f'(0)=0$. In general,

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{f_n(x+h)-f_n(x)}h
\quad\text{and}\quad
\lim_{h\to0}\lim_{n\to\infty}
\frac{f_n(x+h)-f_n(x)}h
$$

need not agree. Power-series theorems supply the extra control inside the radius of
convergence.

</Proposition>

Because a power series is the Taylor series of its sum, its partial sums are the [Taylor polynomials](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-polynomials) of that sum. The [remainder in Taylor's theorem](/calculus/taylor-series/taylors-theorem) controls their error and the [Taylor series](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-series) page decides when a Taylor series built from a given function equals it.
