This page lets the degree of a Taylor polynomial grow without bound and asks when the resulting series equals the function. For a fixed $x$, the [Taylor polynomials](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-polynomials) form a [sequence](/calculus/taylor-series/sequences-and-limits):

$$
P_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.
$$

Letting $n\to\infty$ raises two separate questions:

1. does the infinite series converge?
2. if it converges, does its sum equal $f(x)$?

## Separate convergence from representation

The first question belongs to series theory and is settled by applying the [convergence tests](/calculus/taylor-series/convergence-tests) to the [partial sums](/calculus/taylor-series/infinite-series). The second is specific to Taylor series and is answered by the [remainder in Taylor's theorem](/calculus/taylor-series/taylors-theorem). Keep them separate: matching every derivative does not by itself make the series equal the function.

<Recall of="taylors-theorem" />

<Definition slug="taylor-series" of="taylor-polynomial">

### Taylor series

<Statement>

If $f$ has derivatives of every order at $a$, its Taylor series about $a$ is

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.
$$

The case $a=0$ is the Maclaurin series:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}x^k.
$$

</Statement>

Defining the series does not make it equal to $f$. At a given point it must converge and
the remainder must tend to zero there.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="taylor-series-representation" of="taylor-series,taylors-theorem">

### When a Taylor series represents its function

<Statement>

Let $I$ be an open interval containing $a$ and the fixed point $x$ and suppose
$f\in C^\infty(I)$. Taylor's theorem then gives, for every $n$,

$$
f(x)=P_n(x)+R_{n+1}(x).
$$

For a fixed $x$ in the domain of $f$, if

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}R_{n+1}(x)=0,
$$

then

$$
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.
$$

</Statement>

The conclusion is stronger than convergence of the series. A convergent series can define a
function different from the function whose derivatives supplied its coefficients; the
remainder argument is what proves equality.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="taylor-series-representation">

For fixed $x$, Taylor's theorem gives

$$
f(x)=P_n(x)+R_{n+1}(x).
$$

If $R_{n+1}(x)\to0$, then taking $n\to\infty$ gives

$$
f(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}P_n(x)
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.
$$

</Proof>

<Example slug="exponential-series-remainder" of="taylor-series-representation" difficulty="routine">

### The remainder estimate for $e^x$

The degree-$n$ Maclaurin polynomial for $e^x$ has Lagrange remainder

$$
R_{n+1}(x)=\frac{e^c x^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}
$$

for some $c$ between $0$ and $x$. For fixed $x$, take $M=e^{|x|}$. Then

$$
|R_{n+1}(x)|
\le e^{|x|}\frac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}
\longrightarrow0.
$$

Therefore

$$
e^x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^k}{k!}
$$

for every real $x$.

</Example>

## Standard series and their domains

Each formula below holds only on its stated domain and the endpoints are often where two
otherwise similar series differ.

<Proposition slug="standard-maclaurin-series" of="taylor-series">

### Standard Taylor and Maclaurin series

<Statement>

The following expansions hold on the stated domains:

$$
\frac1{1-x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^k,
\qquad |x|<1,
$$

$$
e^x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^k}{k!},
\qquad x\in\mathbb R,
$$

$$
\sin x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!},
\qquad x\in\mathbb R,
$$

$$
\cos x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!},
\qquad x\in\mathbb R,
$$

$$
\ln(1+x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{x^k}{k},
\qquad -1<x\le1,
$$

$$
\arctan x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k+1}}{2k+1},
\qquad -1\le x\le1,
$$

$$
\sinh x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!},
\qquad x\in\mathbb R,
$$

$$
\cosh x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!},
\qquad x\in\mathbb R.
$$

</Statement>

For example, the geometric series diverges at $x=1$, while the logarithm series converges there by the [alternating-series test](/calculus/taylor-series/convergence-tests) and gives $\ln2$. At $x=-1$, the logarithm series becomes $-\sum1/k$ and diverges.

Convergence at an endpoint does not by itself prove that the sum is the stated function value.
For the logarithm at $x=1$, integrate the *finite* geometric identity
$1/(1+t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(-t)^k+(-t)^N/(1+t)$ from $0$ to $1$.
The omitted integral has absolute value at most $1/(N+1)$, so its limit is zero
and the sum is $\ln2$. Likewise, integrating the finite identity for
$1/(1+t^2)$ gives the $\arctan(1)=\pi/4$ series with an omitted integral
bounded by $1/(2N+1)$; oddness gives the value at $-1$.

</Proposition>

## Build new series from known ones

New expansions are built from the standard series by polynomial-like operations. The
formal algebra does not decide the domain, so carry the convergence restriction through
every substitution, rescaling and multiplication.

<Method slug="construct-taylor-series" of="standard-maclaurin-series,taylor-series-representation">

### Construct a new Taylor or Maclaurin series

<Statement>

Starting from a known series, construct another one by:

1. substituting an expression for the variable;
2. rescaling the variable;
3. multiplying by a power of $x-a$;
4. differentiating or integrating term by term where the convergence theorem permits;
5. multiplying two series by collecting all pairs of terms whose powers have the same total.

At every step, carry the convergence restrictions into the new expression.

</Statement>

</Method>

<Example slug="quadratic-denominator-series" of="construct-taylor-series" difficulty="routine">

### A quadratic denominator

Starting from

$$
\frac1{1-u}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}u^k,
\qquad |u|<1,
$$

put $u=2x^2$:

$$
\frac1{1-2x^2}
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2x^2)^k
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^k x^{2k}.
$$

The restriction is

$$
|2x^2|<1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
|x|<\frac1{\sqrt2}.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="rational-function-taylor-series" of="construct-taylor-series" difficulty="exam">

### A rational function

Rewrite the denominator in geometric form:

$$
\frac{x^3}{1+3x}
=x^3\frac1{1-(-3x)}
=x^3\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-3x)^k
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-3)^k x^{k+3}.
$$

This is valid when $|-3x|<1$, that is, when $|x|<1/3$.

</Example>

<Example slug="rescaled-exponential-series" of="construct-taylor-series" difficulty="routine">

### Rescaling the exponential

Substituting $-x$ into the exponential series gives

$$
e^{-x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k x^k}{k!},
\qquad x\in\mathbb R.
$$

For the separate function $x^4e^{5x}$,

$$
e^{5x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{5^k x^k}{k!},
$$

so multiplication by $x^4$ gives

$$
x^4e^{5x}
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{5^k}{k!}x^{k+4}.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="product-of-exponential-and-geometric-series" of="construct-taylor-series" difficulty="exam">

### The first terms of $\frac{e^x}{1-x}$

For $|x|<1$,

$$
\frac{e^x}{1-x}
=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^j}{j!}\right)
\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^k\right).
$$

Collect equal powers:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{e^x}{1-x}
&=1+\left(1+1\right)x
+\left(\frac12+1+1\right)x^2\\
&\quad+\left(\frac16+\frac12+1+1\right)x^3+\cdots\\
&=1+2x+\frac52x^2+\frac83x^3+\cdots.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Example>

## Applications: cancellation and termwise integration

Taylor series expose the first term that survives cancellation in a limit and they turn
some integrals without an elementary antiderivative into convergent numerical series.

<Example slug="cosine-series-limit" of="taylor-series-representation" difficulty="routine">

### A limit from the cosine series

Using

$$
\cos x=1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}-\cdots,
$$

we obtain

$$
\frac{\cos x-1+x^2/2}{x^4}
=\frac1{4!}-\frac{x^2}{6!}+\cdots
\longrightarrow\frac1{24}.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="integrating-sine-square-series" of="construct-taylor-series" difficulty="challenge">

### Approximating $\int_0^1\sin(x^2)\,dx$

Substituting $x^2$ into the sine series gives

$$
\sin(x^2)=x^2-\frac{x^6}{3!}+\frac{x^{10}}{5!}-\cdots.
$$

Termwise integration produces

$$
\int_0^1\sin(x^2)\,dx
=\frac13-\frac1{7\cdot3!}
+\frac1{11\cdot5!}-\frac1{15\cdot7!}+\cdots.
$$

The first two terms give

$$
\frac13-\frac1{42}=\frac{13}{42}\approx0.30952.
$$

Adding the next term gives approximately $0.31028$, while the numerical value is
$0.3102683\ldots$. The resulting series alternates with decreasing terms, so the first
omitted term gives an error bound.

</Example>

Termwise integration and differentiation are justified by [power-series calculus](/calculus/taylor-series/power-series) inside the radius of convergence and the [remainder in Taylor's theorem](/calculus/taylor-series/taylors-theorem) controls the accuracy of each truncation.
