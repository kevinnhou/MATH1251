This page approximates $f$ near a point $a$ by the polynomial in powers of $x-a$ that matches its value, slope, curvature and higher derivatives up to a chosen degree. [Interpolation](/algebra/vector-spaces/data-fitting) is the other polynomial construction in the course: it matches values at distinct nodes rather than derivatives at one centre.

The degree-one Taylor model is the tangent-line step used by
[Euler's method](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/numerical-solutions).
The degree-two term, built from $f''(a)$, is the one-variable version of
the quadratic [Hessian model](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/multivariable-approximation)
used for functions of several variables.

## Build a local model from derivative data

The centre $a$ is the point where the approximation is anchored. Matching only the value
gives a constant model; matching the first derivative as well gives the tangent line.
Each further matched derivative makes the polynomial share more of the local behaviour of $f$.

<Definition slug="taylor-polynomial">

### Taylor polynomial

<Statement>

If $f$ has derivatives through order $n$ at $a$, its Taylor polynomial of degree at most
$n$ about $a$ is

$$
P_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.
$$

</Statement>

The polynomial is centred at $a$: its powers measure displacement from $a$, not from the
origin. The factorial occurs because the $k$-th derivative of $(x-a)^k$ at $a$ is $k!$.
In this chapter $P_n(x)$ names the Taylor polynomial; the similar notation $P_n(F)$ in
[vector spaces](/algebra/vector-spaces/vector-spaces) denotes the whole space of polynomials
of degree at most $n$ over a field $F$.

</Definition>

<Definition slug="maclaurin-polynomial" of="taylor-polynomial">

### Maclaurin polynomial

<Statement>

The Maclaurin polynomial of degree at most $n$ is the Taylor polynomial about $a=0$:

$$
P_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}x^k.
$$

</Statement>

Maclaurin polynomials are convenient when the derivatives at zero have a simple pattern.

</Definition>

## Why the coefficients are forced

<Theorem slug="taylor-polynomial-uniqueness" of="taylor-polynomial">

### Matching derivatives determines the coefficients

<Statement>

Suppose $Q$ is a polynomial of degree at most $n$ satisfying

$$
Q^{(j)}(a)=f^{(j)}(a),\qquad 0\le j\le n.
$$

Then $Q=P_n$, where $P_n$ is the Taylor polynomial of $f$ about $a$.

</Statement>

How accurate this model is away from the centre is measured by the [remainder in Taylor's theorem](/calculus/taylor-series/taylors-theorem).

</Theorem>

<Proof of="taylor-polynomial-uniqueness">

Write $Q$ in powers of $x-a$:

$$
Q(x)=c_0+c_1(x-a)+\cdots+c_n(x-a)^n.
$$

Evaluating at $a$ gives $Q(a)=c_0$. After differentiating once, evaluating at $a$ gives
$Q'(a)=c_1$ and after differentiating $j$ times it gives

$$
Q^{(j)}(a)=j!c_j.
$$

The matching condition therefore forces

$$
c_j=\frac{f^{(j)}(a)}{j!}
$$

for every $0\le j\le n$. These are exactly the coefficients of $P_n$, so $Q=P_n$.

</Proof>

## Construct the polynomial in practice

Choose a centre close to the values of $x$ you care about, so that the powers
$(x-a)^k$ stay small.

<Method slug="construct-taylor-polynomial" of="taylor-polynomial">

### Construct a Taylor polynomial

<Statement>

To construct the degree-$n$ Taylor polynomial of $f$ about $a$:

1. choose the centre $a$ and degree $n$;
2. calculate $f(a),f'(a),\ldots,f^{(n)}(a)$;
3. substitute those values into
   $$
   P_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k;
   $$
4. use Taylor's theorem to quantify the error of the approximation.

</Statement>

</Method>

## Read the pattern in standard functions

At zero, parity and repeating derivative cycles often make the coefficients
visible at once. At a nonzero centre the same rule applies, but every power is
a power of the displacement $x-a$.

<Example slug="sine-maclaurin-polynomial" of="construct-taylor-polynomial" difficulty="routine">

### A Maclaurin polynomial for $\sin x$

The derivatives at zero alternate between $0,1,0,-1$. The odd-degree polynomial is

$$
P_{2m+1}(x)
=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}
=x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots
+(-1)^m\frac{x^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!}.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="cosine-maclaurin-polynomial" of="construct-taylor-polynomial" difficulty="routine">

### A Maclaurin polynomial for $\cos x$

The derivatives cycle as

$$
\cos x,\quad-\sin x,\quad-\cos x,\quad\sin x,\quad\cos x,\ldots
$$

At zero, the odd derivatives vanish. Therefore

$$
P_6(x)=1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}-\frac{x^6}{6!}.
$$

Only even powers occur, as expected for the even function $\cos x$.

</Example>

<Example slug="cosine-taylor-at-pi-over-four" of="construct-taylor-polynomial" difficulty="exam">

### Moving the centre to $\pi/4$

Let $h=x-\pi/4$. Since

$$
\cos\frac{\pi}{4}=\sin\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2},
$$

the degree-three Taylor polynomial is

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_3(x)
&=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}
-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}h
-\frac{\sqrt2}{4}h^2
+\frac{\sqrt2}{12}h^3\\
&=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}
\left(1-h-\frac{h^2}{2}+\frac{h^3}{6}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The coefficient rule is unchanged from the Maclaurin case; only the displacement changes
from $x$ to $x-\pi/4$.

</Example>

When derivatives repeat, record the pattern instead of differentiating each order from
scratch.

<Example slug="standard-finite-taylor-polynomials" of="construct-taylor-polynomial" difficulty="routine">

### Standard finite expansions

For $e^x$, every derivative is $e^x$, so every derivative at zero is $1$:

$$
P_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{x^k}{k!}
=1+x+\frac{x^2}{2!}+\cdots+\frac{x^n}{n!}.
$$

For $\ln(1+x)$ and $k\ge1$,

$$
\left.\frac{d^k}{dx^k}\ln(1+x)\right|_{x=0}
=(-1)^{k+1}(k-1)!,
$$

so

$$
P_n(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{x^k}{k}
=x-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^3}{3}-\cdots
+(-1)^{n+1}\frac{x^n}{n}.
$$

Replacing $x$ by $-x$ gives the finite expansion

$$
-x-\frac{x^2}{2}-\frac{x^3}{3}-\cdots-\frac{x^n}{n}
$$

for $\ln(1-x)$.

</Example>

All of these are finite approximations. For a fixed $x$, increasing $n$ produces a [sequence](/calculus/taylor-series/sequences-and-limits) of approximations whose error is the [remainder in Taylor's theorem](/calculus/taylor-series/taylors-theorem). Passing to the infinite [Taylor series](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-series) needs a [convergence](/calculus/taylor-series/convergence-tests) argument and a proof that this remainder tends to zero.
