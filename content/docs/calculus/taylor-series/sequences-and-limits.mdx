This page makes precise what it means for a sequence to converge and collects the tools for proving that it does. For a fixed $x$, the [Taylor polynomials](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-polynomials) form a sequence and an infinite Taylor expression means something at that $x$ only if this sequence settles towards a finite number. The same ideas then apply to the [partial sums](/calculus/taylor-series/infinite-series) that define an infinite series.

## From increasing degree to sequences

A sequence is indexed by integers, so its limit is taken as $n\to\infty$ through the
integers, not as a real variable approaches a point.

<Recall of="taylor-polynomial" />

<Definition slug="real-sequence">

### Real sequence

<Statement>

A real sequence is a function

$$
a:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R.
$$

Its value at $n$ is written $a_n$ and the sequence is written $\{a_n\}$.

</Statement>

The starting index may be $0$ or $1$; changing finitely many initial terms does not change
the limit. We will use the index that makes a given recurrence or series easiest to state.

Examples include

$$
a_n=n^2,\qquad b_n=(-1)^n,\qquad
c_n=\frac1{n+1},\qquad d_n=e^{-n}.
$$

A function of a real variable and a sequence are different objects: a sequence records only
integer-indexed values, although a function $f$ can generate the sequence $a_n=f(n)$.
Sequences may also be recursive, as in

$$
a_1=a_2=1,\qquad a_{n+1}=a_n+a_{n-1}\quad(n\ge2).
$$

</Definition>

## Convergence means controlling the tail

The first terms of a sequence may be irregular, so convergence is a statement about the
tail: for every tolerance, all terms after some index lie within that tolerance of one
number.

<Definition slug="sequence-convergence">

### Convergence of a sequence

<Statement>

The sequence $\{a_n\}$ converges to $L$, written

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=L,
$$

if

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists N\in\mathbb N
\text{ such that }n>N\implies |a_n-L|<\varepsilon.
$$

</Statement>

The index $N$ may depend on $\varepsilon$. The condition says that, for each
$\varepsilon$, the tail eventually lies inside $(L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon)$.
If no finite $L$ has this property, the sequence diverges.

Divergence includes several behaviours:

- $a_n\to+\infty$, as for $n^2$;
- $a_n\to-\infty$, as for $-n$;
- bounded oscillation, as for $\sin n$;
- unbounded oscillation, as for $(-1)^n n$.

</Definition>

<Example slug="epsilon-proof-sequence-limit" of="sequence-convergence" difficulty="routine">

### Proving $2+\frac1{n+1}\to2$

Let $a_n=2+\frac1{n+1}$. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose an integer $N$ with
$N>1/\varepsilon$. If $n>N$, then

$$
|a_n-2|
=\frac1{n+1}
<\frac1N
<\varepsilon.
$$

</Example>

The limit laws save repeating an $\varepsilon$ estimate for every algebraic expression.

<Theorem slug="sequence-limit-laws" of="sequence-convergence">

### Algebra and continuity of sequence limits

<Statement>

If $a_n\to K$ and $b_n\to L$, with $K$ and $L$ finite, then

$$
a_n\pm b_n\to K\pm L,\qquad
a_nb_n\to KL,\qquad
\frac{a_n}{b_n}\to\frac KL\quad(L\ne0).
$$

If $h$ is continuous at $K$, then

$$
a_n\to K\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h(a_n)\to h(K).
$$

</Statement>

For example,

$$
\frac{4n^2-3n+2}{2n^2+6n+1}
=\frac{4-3/n+2/n^2}{2+6/n+1/n^2}
\longrightarrow2.
$$

If a real function has a limit as $x\to\infty$, its sampled sequence $f(n)$ has the same
limit. The converse is false: $f(x)=\sin(\pi x)$ gives $f(n)=0$ for every integer $n$,
although $f(x)$ has no limit as $x\to\infty$.

</Theorem>

## Practical limit tools: algebra, growth and squeeze

When the limit laws do not apply directly, three tools help. Rationalisation exposes a
hidden common scale, logarithms turn powers into products and squeezing replaces an
unavailable exact formula with two manageable bounds.

Growth rates are compared with asymptotic notation. For sequences, $a_n=O(b_n)$ means
that $|a_n|\le C|b_n|$ for some constant $C$ and all sufficiently large $n$. When
$b_n\ne0$ eventually, the stronger statement $a_n=o(b_n)$ means $a_n/b_n\to0$, while
$a_n\sim b_n$ means $a_n/b_n\to1$.

Thus $O(b_n)$ gives an upper scale, $o(b_n)$ is negligible relative to it and
$a_n\sim b_n$ records the same leading behaviour. These are comparisons, not exact
equalities. The [limit comparison test](/calculus/taylor-series/convergence-tests) uses
the idea behind $\sim$ to transfer convergence between positive-term series.

<Method slug="sequence-limit-techniques" of="sequence-limit-laws">

### Simplify a sequence limit

<Statement>

For a sequence limit:

1. simplify algebraically, dividing by the dominant power of $n$ when appropriate;
2. rationalise when a square root creates a difference of large, nearly equal quantities;
3. rewrite positive powers using logarithms and exponentials;
4. use the squeeze theorem when direct limit laws do not apply;
5. for a recursively defined sequence, prove convergence before solving the equation
   obtained by taking its limit.

</Statement>

The order matters for recursive sequences. Solving a fixed-point equation first only finds
possible limits; it does not prove that the sequence converges to one of them.

</Method>

<Example slug="rationalised-sequence-limit" of="sequence-limit-techniques" difficulty="routine">

### Rationalising a square root

For

$$
\sqrt{n^2+3n}-n,
$$

multiply by the conjugate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{n^2+3n}-n
&=\frac{(n^2+3n)-n^2}{\sqrt{n^2+3n}+n}\\
&=\frac{3}{\sqrt{1+3/n}+1}
\longrightarrow\frac32.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="standard-sequence-limits" of="sequence-limit-techniques" difficulty="routine">

### Logarithmic, root and geometric limits

For every $\alpha>0$,

$$
\frac{\ln n}{n^\alpha}\longrightarrow0.
$$

L'Hôpital's rule applied to $\ln x/x^\alpha$ gives

$$
\frac{(1/x)}{\alpha x^{\alpha-1}}
=\frac1{\alpha x^\alpha}\longrightarrow0.
$$

For fixed $x>0$,

$$
x^{1/n}=e^{(\ln x)/n}\longrightarrow1,
\qquad
n^{1/n}=e^{(\ln n)/n}\longrightarrow1.
$$

For $0<x<1$, geometric decay gives $x^n\to0$. Finally,

$$
\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n\longrightarrow e.
$$

Taking logarithms,

$$
n\ln\left(1+\frac1n\right)
=\frac{\ln(1+1/n)}{1/n}
\longrightarrow1,
$$

and exponentiating gives the result.

</Example>

<Theorem slug="sequence-squeeze-theorem" of="sequence-convergence">

### Squeeze theorem for sequences

<Statement>

If, for all sufficiently large $n$,

$$
a_n\le b_n\le c_n,
$$

and $a_n\to L$ and $c_n\to L$, then $b_n\to L$.

</Statement>

Only the tail needs to satisfy the inequalities. This helps with growth estimates when an
exact formula for the middle sequence is unavailable.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="factorial-growth-squeeze" of="sequence-squeeze-theorem" difficulty="routine">

### A factorial sequence

For

$$
0<\frac{n!}{n^n}
=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{k}{n},
$$

at least $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ factors are at most $1/2$. Therefore

$$
0<\frac{n!}{n^n}
\le\left(\frac12\right)^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
\longrightarrow0.
$$

The rough hierarchy

$$
1,\quad \ln n,\quad n^p,\quad c^n,\quad n!,\quad n^n
$$

for fixed $p>0$ and $c>1$ helps choose comparisons, but it is not itself a proof of a
limit.

</Example>

## Completeness supplies limits

Some sequences can be shown to converge without any formula for the limit. The
completeness of the real numbers is what supplies the limit in those cases.

<Definition slug="supremum-and-infimum">

### Supremum and infimum

<Statement>

An upper bound of $S\subseteq\mathbb R$ is a number $M$ satisfying $x\le M$ for every
$x\in S$. The supremum $\sup S$ is the least upper bound. Dually, a lower bound
satisfies $m\le x$ for every $x\in S$ and the infimum $\inf S$ is the greatest lower
bound.

</Statement>

The supremum or infimum need not belong to the set. For $S=(0,1)$, $\sup S=1$ and
$\inf S=0$, although neither endpoint is in $S$. This distinction will matter when the
endpoints of a power-series interval are tested.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="real-number-completeness" of="supremum-and-infimum">

### Completeness of the real numbers

<Statement>

Every non-empty subset of $\mathbb R$ that is bounded above has a supremum and every
non-empty subset bounded below has an infimum.

</Statement>

The characterisation

$$
M=\sup S
$$

is equivalent to:

1. $x\le M$ for every $x\in S$;
2. for every $\varepsilon>0$, some $x\in S$ satisfies $M-\varepsilon<x$.

The second condition says that no smaller number remains an upper bound. Reversing the
inequalities gives the corresponding characterisation of an infimum.

</Theorem>

## Bounded monotone and recursive sequences

Monotonicity gives a direction, while a bound prevents the sequence from escaping. Together
they prove convergence even when no closed formula for $a_n$ is available.

<Definition slug="monotone-sequence">

### Monotone sequence

<Statement>

A sequence is non-decreasing if $a_{n+1}\ge a_n$ for every $n$ and non-increasing
if $a_{n+1}\le a_n$ for every $n$.

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="monotone-convergence-theorem" of="monotone-sequence">

### Monotone convergence theorem

<Statement>

A non-decreasing sequence bounded above converges. A non-increasing sequence bounded below
converges.

</Statement>

The theorem guarantees a finite limit without giving a formula for it. Completeness
supplies the least upper bound or greatest lower bound that becomes the limit.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="monotone-convergence-theorem">

For a non-decreasing sequence bounded above, let $L=\sup\{a_n:n\in\mathbb N\}$.
Given $\varepsilon>0$, some term $a_N$ is greater than $L-\varepsilon$; otherwise
$L-\varepsilon$ would be a smaller upper bound. Monotonicity gives
$L-\varepsilon<a_N\le a_n\le L$ for all $n\ge N$, so $a_n\to L$.
The non-increasing case uses the infimum in the same way.

</Proof>

<Example slug="monotone-sequence-examples" of="monotone-convergence-theorem" difficulty="routine">

### Two monotone sequences

The sequence

$$
a_n=\frac n{n+1}
$$

is increasing and bounded above by $1$, so it converges. Its limit is $1$.

For

$$
b_n=\frac{2^n}{n!},
$$

we have

$$
\frac{b_{n+1}}{b_n}=\frac2{n+1}<1
$$

once $n\ge2$. It is eventually decreasing and bounded below by $0$, so it converges.
Its limit is $0$, because the ratio is at most $1/2$ for $n\ge3$, so
$b_n\le b_3(1/2)^{n-3}$ for all later terms.

</Example>

<Method slug="recursive-sequence-convergence" of="monotone-convergence-theorem">

### Analyse a recursive sequence

<Statement>

For a recursive sequence:

1. find candidate limits by solving the fixed-point equation;
2. prove a bound that is preserved by the recursion;
3. prove monotonicity on that bounded region;
4. invoke the Monotone Convergence Theorem;
5. use continuity to substitute the now-established limit into the recursion;
6. select the algebraic root compatible with the bound.

</Statement>

</Method>

<Example slug="recursive-square-root-sequence" of="recursive-sequence-convergence" difficulty="challenge">

### A recursively defined sequence

Let

$$
a_1=4,\qquad a_{n+1}=\sqrt{1+3a_n}.
$$

The positive solution of the fixed-point equation $L^2=1+3L$ is

$$
\alpha=\frac{3+\sqrt{13}}2.
$$

Since $a_1=4\ge\alpha$ and since
$$
a_n\ge\alpha\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
1+3a_n\ge1+3\alpha=\alpha^2,
$$
the recursion gives $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{1+3a_n}\ge\alpha$. Induction therefore
gives $a_n\ge\alpha$ for every $n$. For $a_n\ge\alpha$,

$$
a_{n+1}\le a_n
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
1+3a_n\le a_n^2,
$$

and the latter holds because $a_n^2-3a_n-1\ge0$ for $a_n\ge\alpha$. Thus $\{a_n\}$ is
decreasing and bounded below by $\alpha$, so it converges.

If $a_n\to L$, continuity of the square-root function gives

$$
L=\sqrt{1+3L}.
$$

The sequence is positive, so the compatible root is

$$
{\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=\frac{3+\sqrt{13}}2.}
$$

</Example>

Partial sums are themselves sequences, so the tools above decide their convergence. [Infinite series](/calculus/taylor-series/infinite-series) defines convergence through partial sums and [convergence tests](/calculus/taylor-series/convergence-tests) turns that definition into usable tests.
