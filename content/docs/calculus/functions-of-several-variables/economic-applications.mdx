This page applies [constrained
optimisation](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/constrained-extrema) to
production: a firm chooses inputs to maximise output within a budget. Each
problem starts by identifying the output, the feasible input choices and
whether the budget constraint binds.

## Production and budget

<Definition slug="production-function">

### Production function

<Statement>

A production function describes output $Q$ as a function of input quantities:

$$
Q=f(E,L),
$$

where $E,L\ge0$, $E$ denotes capital equipment or investment and $L$ denotes
labour. The formula is understood on the economically feasible part of this
non-negative quadrant.

</Statement>

The gradient of $Q$ records how output changes when either input changes. An optimal
allocation compares those changes with input prices through the [multiplier condition](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/constrained-extrema), which requires $\nabla Q$ to be parallel to the budget-line normal.

</Definition>

<Definition slug="budget-constraint">

### Budget constraint

<Statement>

If $q>0$ is the price per unit of capital, $p>0$ is the price per unit of
labour and $B>0$ is the available budget, the feasible input choices satisfy

$$
qE+pL\le B,
\qquad
E\ge0,\quad L\ge0.
$$

The products $qE$ and $pL$ are both measured in currency units, as is $B$;
this keeps the budget equation dimensionally meaningful.

</Statement>

The inequality includes every affordable input bundle. Its boundary
$qE+pL=B$ is the budget line: the bundles that spend the entire budget.

</Definition>

<Method slug="budget-constrained-production" of="production-function,budget-constraint">

### Formulate a production problem

<Statement>

To maximise production under a budget:

1. identify the production function $Q=f(E,L)$;
2. write the feasible set
   $$
   qE+pL\le B,
   \qquad
   E,L\ge0;
   $$
3. if increasing either input increases output, replace the inequality by the binding budget equation $qE+pL=B$;
4. apply the [constrained-extrema method](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/constrained-extrema) and interpret the resulting inputs.

</Statement>

If output increases with either input, an allocation leaving budget unused
cannot be optimal, because some additional productive input could be
purchased. Without this monotonicity
assumption (or if inputs are indivisible), keep the inequality and compare
interior, axis and slack candidates instead.

</Method>

## Cobb-Douglas allocation

<Definition slug="cobb-douglas-production" of="production-function">

### Cobb-Douglas production function

<Statement>

A Cobb-Douglas production function has the form

$$
Q(E,L)=AE^\varepsilon L^{1-\varepsilon},
\qquad
A>0,
\qquad
0<\varepsilon<1.
$$

</Statement>

The exponent $\varepsilon$ controls the relative contribution of capital and
$1-\varepsilon$ controls the relative contribution of labour. The positive
scale parameter $A$ changes output levels but, under the linear budget
constraint, does not change the optimal input shares.

</Definition>

<Example slug="cobb-douglas-optimal-allocation" of="cobb-douglas-production,budget-constrained-production,lagrange-multiplier-method" difficulty="exam">

### Optimal Cobb-Douglas allocation

Maximise

$$
Q(E,L)=AE^\varepsilon L^{1-\varepsilon}
$$

subject to

$$
qE+pL=B.
$$

Assume $p,q,B>0$. A feasible bundle with $E,L>0$ produces positive output,
whereas a bundle with $E=0$ or $L=0$ produces zero output. Hence an optimum
has $E,L>0$, so the following first-order equations and division are valid.

The multiplier equations are

$$
\varepsilon AE^{\varepsilon-1}L^{1-\varepsilon}
=\lambda q,
$$

$$
(1-\varepsilon)AE^\varepsilon L^{-\varepsilon}
=\lambda p,
$$

together with $qE+pL=B$.

Divide the first equation by the second. The common factors simplify to

$$
\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\frac{L}{E}
=\frac{q}{p}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
p\varepsilon L=q(1-\varepsilon)E.
$$

Combining this relation with the budget equation gives

$$
qE=\varepsilon B,
\qquad
pL=(1-\varepsilon)B.
$$

Thus the optimal input quantities are

$$
{
E=\frac{\varepsilon B}{q},
\qquad
L=\frac{(1-\varepsilon)B}{p}
}.
$$

The firm spends the fraction $\varepsilon$ of its budget on capital and the
fraction $1-\varepsilon$ on labour:

$$
\frac{qE}{B}=\varepsilon,
\qquad
\frac{pL}{B}=1-\varepsilon.
$$

The scale parameter $A$ cancels from the allocation because it multiplies
both multiplier equations in the same way.

Here $\lambda$ has units of output per currency unit and measures the
marginal value of relaxing the budget. Then $\lambda q$ and $\lambda p$ have
the units of the marginal products $Q_E$ and $Q_L$: output per unit of capital
and output per unit of labour.

This interpretation can be checked directly. The first
multiplier equation gives
$\lambda=\varepsilon Q/(qE)=Q/B$ at the optimum. Since both optimal inputs
are proportional to $B$, the optimal output is also proportional to $B$;
thus $dQ_{\max}/dB=Q_{\max}/B=\lambda$.

The multiplier equations locate a candidate, not by themselves a global
maximum. Along the budget line,
$L=(B-qE)/p$ for $0<E<B/q$ and

$$
\frac{d}{dE}\log Q\left(E,\frac{B-qE}{p}\right)
=\frac{\varepsilon}{E}
-\frac{(1-\varepsilon)q}{B-qE}.
$$

This derivative is positive before $E=\varepsilon B/q$ and negative after it;
output is zero at both endpoints. The candidate is therefore the global
maximum on the feasible budget segment.

</Example>
