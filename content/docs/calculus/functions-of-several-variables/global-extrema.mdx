This page finds the absolute maximum and minimum of a function on a region.
Unlike a local test, an absolute extremum compares every point of the domain,
so the boundary of the region enters the calculation. On a closed and bounded
region the extreme-value theorem guarantees that both extrema exist; interior
candidates come from the [stationary
points](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/local-extrema) and each
boundary piece gives a lower-dimensional optimisation problem.

## Closed and bounded regions

<Definition slug="closed-bounded-region">

### Closed and bounded region

<Statement>

A region $D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2$ is **closed** if it contains its boundary
$\partial D$ and **bounded** if it fits inside a sufficiently large disk:
there is an $R>0$ such that $\|\mathbf{x}\|\le R$ for every
$\mathbf{x}\in D$.

</Statement>

An **interior point** has a neighbourhood contained entirely in $D$. A
**boundary point** has every neighbourhood meeting both $D$ and its
complement; the set of boundary points is written $\partial D$.

The closed unit disk

$$
\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le1\}
$$

contains its boundary, the unit circle $x^2+y^2=1$. An open disk omits that
circle and is not closed.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="multivariable-extreme-value-theorem" of="closed-bounded-region">

### The multivariable extreme-value theorem

<Statement>

If $f$ is continuous on a nonempty closed and bounded region
$D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2$, then $f$ attains an absolute maximum and an absolute
minimum on $D$.

</Statement>

An absolute extremum can occur at an interior critical point or anywhere on
the boundary $\partial D$. The theorem guarantees existence, but it does not
identify the candidates. The boundary is usually an infinite set, so it must
be treated as a lower-dimensional optimisation problem.

If either closedness or boundedness fails, an extremum need not be attained;
for example, $f(x,y)=x$ on the open unit disk has supremum $1$ but no maximum.

</Theorem>

## The candidate workflow

<Method slug="absolute-extrema-workflow" of="multivariable-extreme-value-theorem">

### Find absolute extrema on a closed bounded region

<Statement>

For a continuous $f$ on a nonempty closed and bounded region $D$, use the
following workflow, checking differentiability wherever a derivative is used:

1. find all stationary points in the differentiable interior of $D$ and
   separately record any interior points where $f$ is not differentiable;
2. restrict $f$ to each boundary piece;
3. find critical points of every restricted function, including endpoints and
   corners;
4. evaluate $f$ at every candidate;
5. compare the resulting values.

</Statement>

Neither part of the search can be skipped: a boundary point can have a larger
or smaller value than every interior critical point and a corner may not be
detected by a one-variable derivative.

</Method>

<Method slug="boundary-parametrisation" of="absolute-extrema-workflow">

### Analyse a boundary by parameterisation

<Statement>

To analyse a differentiable boundary curve, parameterise it as

$$
\mathbf{r}(t)=(x(t),y(t))
$$

and optimise the one-variable function

$$
\phi(t)=f(x(t),y(t)).
$$

Include all parameter endpoints when the boundary is a segment or a
piecewise-smooth curve and include corners separately.

</Statement>

Parameterisation converts the boundary part of a two-variable problem into
one-variable calculus. When the boundary is naturally a level curve $g=0$,
[Lagrange
multipliers](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/constrained-extrema)
solve the boundary problem without a parameterisation.

</Method>

<Example slug="rectangle-absolute-extrema" of="absolute-extrema-workflow" difficulty="exam">

### Extrema on a rectangle

Find the maximum and minimum of

$$
f(x,y)=x^2-x^2y+3y^2
$$

on

$$
D=\{(x,y):-1\le x\le1,\ -1\le y\le1\}.
$$

For interior candidates,

$$
f_x=2x(1-y),
\qquad
f_y=-x^2+6y.
$$

The equations $f_x=f_y=0$ give either $x=0$, hence $y=0$, or $y=1$, hence
$x^2=6$, which is outside the rectangle. The only interior candidate is
$(0,0)$, with

$$
f(0,0)=0.
$$

On the vertical edges $x=\pm1$,

$$
f(\pm1,y)=1-y+3y^2.
$$

The derivative is $-1+6y$, so the edge critical point is $y=1/6$, where

$$
f\left(\pm1,\frac16\right)
=1-\frac16+3\left(\frac16\right)^2
=\frac{11}{12}.
$$

The edge endpoints give

$$
f(\pm1,-1)=5,
\qquad
f(\pm1,1)=3.
$$

On the horizontal edges,

$$
f(x,1)=3,
\qquad
f(x,-1)=2x^2+3.
$$

The latter has minimum $3$ at $x=0$ and maximum $5$ at $x=\pm1$. Comparing
all candidates gives

$$
{\min_D f=0\text{ at }(0,0)}
$$

and

$$
{\max_D f=5\text{ at }(1,-1)\text{ and }(-1,-1)}.
$$

The minimum is interior, while the maximum occurs at corners.

</Example>

<Example slug="disk-absolute-extrema" of="absolute-extrema-workflow,boundary-parametrisation" difficulty="exam">

### Extrema on the closed unit disk

Let

$$
f(x,y)=x^2+4xy+y^2
$$

on

$$
D=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\le1\}.
$$

The interior gradient is

$$
\nabla f
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2x+4y\\
4x+2y
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Solving $\nabla f=\mathbf{0}$ gives $(0,0)$, with $f(0,0)=0$.

The boundary is the unit circle. Parameterise it by

$$
x=\cos t,
\qquad
y=\sin t.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(\cos t,\sin t)
&=\cos^2t+4\cos t\sin t+\sin^2t\\
&=1+2\sin(2t).
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $-1\le\sin(2t)\le1$,

$$
-1\le f(\cos t,\sin t)\le3.
$$

The maximum $3$ occurs at

$$
\left(\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac1{\sqrt2}\right),
\qquad
\left(-\frac1{\sqrt2},-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right),
$$

and the minimum $-1$ occurs at

$$
\left(\frac1{\sqrt2},-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right),
\qquad
\left(-\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac1{\sqrt2}\right).
$$

Comparing with the interior value gives

$$
{\max_D f=3}
$$

at the two same-sign boundary points and

$$
{\min_D f=-1}
$$

at the two opposite-sign boundary points.

</Example>

The boundary calculation can also be done without a parameter by treating
$x^2+y^2=1$ as a constraint. The [constrained
extrema](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/constrained-extrema) page
develops this method, in which a gradient-parallel condition replaces the
parameter derivative.
