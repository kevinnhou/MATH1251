This page locates and classifies the local extrema of a function of several
variables. At a stationary point the [tangent
plane](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/geometry-and-approximation) is
horizontal, which does not say whether the graph is a hill, a valley or a
saddle. First-order calculus locates the candidates; the quadratic part of the
[multivariable Taylor
model](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/multivariable-approximation)
classifies them.

## Local and absolute extrema

<Definition slug="local-and-absolute-extrema">

### Local and absolute extrema

<Statement>

Let $f:D\to\mathbb{R}$ and let $\mathbf{x}_0\in D$.

- $f$ has a **local minimum** at $\mathbf{x}_0$ if
  $f(\mathbf{x})\ge f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ for every $\mathbf{x}$ in $D$ sufficiently
  close to $\mathbf{x}_0$.
- $f$ has a **local maximum** at $\mathbf{x}_0$ if
  $f(\mathbf{x})\le f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ for every $\mathbf{x}$ in $D$ sufficiently
  close to $\mathbf{x}_0$.
- $f$ has an **absolute**, or **global**, minimum at $\mathbf{x}_0$ if
  $f(\mathbf{x})\ge f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ for every $\mathbf{x}\in D$.
- $f$ has an absolute maximum at $\mathbf{x}_0$ if
  $f(\mathbf{x})\le f(\mathbf{x}_0)$ for every $\mathbf{x}\in D$.

</Statement>

A local classification cannot settle a global optimisation problem.

</Definition>

## Critical and stationary points

<Definition slug="multivariable-critical-point">

### Critical and stationary points

<Statement>

For $f:D\to\mathbb{R}$, an interior point
$\mathbf{x}_0\in\operatorname{int}(D)$ is a **critical point** if $f$ is not
differentiable at $\mathbf{x}_0$ or if
$\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathbf{0}$. A point at which the gradient exists and
is zero is called a **stationary point**.

</Statement>

Critical points include points where differentiability fails because such
points can still be extrema. The stationary-point equations locate only the
critical points with zero gradient; boundary points are handled separately in
a global-extrema problem.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="multivariable-fermat-condition" of="multivariable-critical-point">

### The multivariable Fermat condition

<Statement>

If all first partial derivatives of $f$ exist at an interior local maximum or
local minimum $\mathbf{x}_0$, then

$$
{\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathbf{0}}.
$$

</Statement>

Differentiability is sufficient for the partial derivatives to exist, but is
stronger than what this coordinatewise condition needs. The condition is
necessary, not a classification: a stationary point may be a local maximum, a
local minimum, a saddle, or a point requiring higher-order analysis.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="multivariable-fermat-condition">

Fix a coordinate direction and vary only that coordinate through
$\mathbf{x}_0$. Since $\mathbf{x}_0$ is an interior local extremum of $f$, the
resulting one-variable function also has a local extremum at the corresponding
coordinate value. The one-variable Fermat condition makes the associated
partial derivative zero. Repeating this for every coordinate gives

$$
f_{x_1}(\mathbf{x}_0)=\cdots=f_{x_n}(\mathbf{x}_0)=0,
$$

which is exactly $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathbf{0}$.

</Proof>

<Method slug="find-stationary-points" of="multivariable-fermat-condition">

### Find stationary points

<Statement>

For a function $f(x,y)$:

1. calculate $f_x$ and $f_y$;
2. solve
   $$
   f_x(x,y)=0,
   \qquad
   f_y(x,y)=0;
   $$
3. retain the simultaneous solutions in the domain;
4. classify each candidate using local geometry or the Hessian test.

</Statement>

For a complete search for interior extrema, also inspect points where $f$ is
not differentiable; the stationary equations cannot detect those points.

</Method>

<Example slug="stationary-points-polynomial" of="find-stationary-points" difficulty="routine">

### Finding two stationary points

Let

$$
f(x,y)=8x^3+y^2-6xy-2y+3.
$$

Then

$$
f_x=24x^2-6y,
\qquad
f_y=2y-6x-2.
$$

The stationary equations give

$$
y=4x^2,
\qquad
y=3x+1.
$$

Hence

$$
4x^2=3x+1,
\qquad
(4x+1)(x-1)=0.
$$

The stationary points are

$$
{(1,4)}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
{\left(-\frac14,\frac14\right)}.
$$

</Example>

The Fermat condition needs only the coordinate partial derivatives, but their
existence does not guarantee differentiability, which the tangent-plane and
directional-derivative formulas require. Partial derivatives inspect only the
coordinate paths and may miss behaviour along other directions.

<Example slug="zero-gradient-without-continuity" of="multivariable-fermat-condition" difficulty="challenge">

### Zero partial derivatives without differentiability

Define

$$
f(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2},&(x,y)\ne(0,0),\\[6pt]
0,&(x,y)=(0,0).
\end{cases}
$$

Along the coordinate axes,

$$
f(h,0)=0,
\qquad
f(0,h)=0,
$$

so

$$
f_x(0,0)=0,
\qquad
f_y(0,0)=0.
$$

However, along $y=x$,

$$
f(t,t)=\frac{t^2}{2t^2}=\frac12
\qquad(t\ne0).
$$

The function is therefore not continuous at the origin and cannot be
differentiable there, even though its two partial derivatives exist and
vanish.

</Example>

## The geometry of a stationary point

At a stationary point $(x_0,y_0)$ of the graph $z=f(x,y)$, the tangent plane
has equation

$$
z=f(x_0,y_0),
$$

because both first partial derivatives vanish.

A **saddle point** is a point $\mathbf{x}_0$ for which every neighbourhood
within the domain contains points with both larger and smaller function
values than $f(\mathbf{x}_0)$. A saddle is neither a local maximum nor a
local minimum.

<Example slug="product-saddle" of="multivariable-fermat-condition" difficulty="routine">

### A saddle from two directions

For

$$
f(x,y)=xy,
$$

the only stationary point is $(0,0)$ because

$$
\nabla f(x,y)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
y\\
x
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Along $y=x$,

$$
f(t,t)=t^2>0
\qquad(t\ne0),
$$

whereas along $y=-x$,

$$
f(t,-t)=-t^2<0.
$$

Every neighbourhood of the origin contains larger and smaller values than
$f(0,0)=0$, so the origin is a saddle point.

</Example>

## Classifying stationary points

The gradient gives the linear part of the local model. At a stationary point
that part vanishes, so the Hessian supplies the next term to inspect. If the
quadratic part also vanishes in some directions, higher-order terms may matter.

<Recall of="multivariable-hessian" />

<Theorem slug="multivariable-second-derivative-test" of="multivariable-hessian">

### The second-order partial derivative test

<Statement>

Let $\mathbf{x}_0=(x_0,y_0)$ be a stationary point. Assume that $f$ has
continuous second-order partial derivatives on a neighbourhood of
$\mathbf{x}_0$ (so $f$ is $C^2$ there). Define

$$
D
=\det H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)
=f_{xx}(\mathbf{x}_0)f_{yy}(\mathbf{x}_0)
-f_{xy}(\mathbf{x}_0)^2.
$$

Then:

- if $D>0$ and $f_{xx}(\mathbf{x}_0)>0$, $\mathbf{x}_0$ is a local minimum;
- if $D>0$ and $f_{xx}(\mathbf{x}_0)<0$, $\mathbf{x}_0$ is a local maximum;
- if $D<0$, $\mathbf{x}_0$ is a saddle point;
- if $D=0$, the test is inconclusive.

</Statement>

At a stationary point, the quadratic Taylor term is

$$
\frac12\mathbf{h}^{\mathsf T}H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)\mathbf{h}.
$$

For a symmetric Hessian, this quadratic form is **positive definite** if it
is positive for every nonzero $\mathbf{h}$, **negative definite** if it is
negative for every nonzero $\mathbf{h}$ and **indefinite** if it takes both
positive and negative values. The test uses $D$ and $f_{xx}$ to tell these
cases apart.

When $D>0$, the quadratic form has one sign in all non-zero directions and
$f_{xx}$ determines whether that sign is positive or negative. When $D<0$,
the form changes sign, which is the algebraic signature of a saddle. If
$D=0$, higher-order terms or direct comparisons along curves are required.

To see the determinant rule when $D>0$, write
$a=f_{xx}(\mathbf{x}_0)$, $b=f_{xy}(\mathbf{x}_0)$ and
$c=f_{yy}(\mathbf{x}_0)$. Then $a\ne0$ and the quadratic part satisfies

$$
ah^2+2bhk+ck^2
=a\left(h+\frac ba k\right)^2+\frac Da k^2.
$$

Both coefficients on the right have the sign of $a$, so small nonzero
displacements change $f$ in the same direction.

For intuition, a symmetric Hessian has principal directions described by its
[eigenvectors](/algebra/eigenvalues/eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors). Its
eigenvalues give the curvatures in those directions: both positive suggests
a local minimum, both negative a local maximum and opposite signs a saddle.
In two variables, $D$ is the product of those two eigenvalues, while
$f_{xx}$ picks the sign when they agree.

</Theorem>

<Method slug="classify-stationary-points" of="find-stationary-points,multivariable-second-derivative-test">

### Classify a stationary point

<Statement>

For each stationary point:

1. calculate the Hessian entries at the point;
2. compute $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$;
3. use the sign of $D$ and then the sign of $f_{xx}$ when $D>0$;
4. if $D=0$, inspect higher-order terms or compare the function along
   carefully chosen curves.

</Statement>

</Method>

<Example slug="standard-quadratic-classifications" of="classify-stationary-points" difficulty="routine">

### Standard quadratic surfaces

For

$$
f(x,y)=x^2+y^2,
$$

the Hessian at the origin is

$$
H_f(0,0)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&0\\
0&2
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D=4>0,
\qquad
f_{xx}=2>0.
$$

Thus the origin is a local minimum; $f(x,y)\ge0$ everywhere, so it
is also a global minimum.

For

$$
g(x,y)=-x^2-y^2,
$$

we have $D=4>0$ and $g_{xx}=-2<0$, so the origin is a local maximum.

For

$$
h(x,y)=x^2-y^2,
$$

the Hessian is

$$
H_h(0,0)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&0\\
0&-2
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D=-4<0.
$$

The origin is therefore a saddle; the two coordinate axes show this directly.

</Example>

<Example slug="cubic-hessian-classification" of="classify-stationary-points" difficulty="exam">

### Classifying the stationary points of a cubic

Let

$$
f(x,y)=x^3-y^3+3xy.
$$

The first derivatives are

$$
f_x=3x^2+3y,
\qquad
f_y=-3y^2+3x.
$$

Thus

$$
y=-x^2,
\qquad
x=y^2,
$$

so $x=x^4$. The stationary points are $(0,0)$ and $(1,-1)$.

The Hessian is

$$
H_f(x,y)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
6x&3\\
3&-6y
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At $(0,0)$,

$$
\det H_f(0,0)=-9<0,
$$

so the origin is a saddle. At $(1,-1)$,

$$
H_f(1,-1)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
6&3\\
3&6
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det H_f(1,-1)=27>0,
\qquad
f_{xx}(1,-1)=6>0.
$$

Therefore $(1,-1)$ is a local minimum. Its value is

$$
f(1,-1)=1-(-1)^3+3(1)(-1)=-1.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="positive-factor-saddle" of="classify-stationary-points" difficulty="challenge">

### Checking a positive factor for new stationary points

Let

$$
f(x,y)=xy\,e^{x^2+y^2}.
$$

The derivatives factor as

$$
f_x=y\,e^{x^2+y^2}(1+2x^2),
\qquad
f_y=x\,e^{x^2+y^2}(1+2y^2).
$$

The exponential factors and the terms $1+2x^2$, $1+2y^2$ are always positive.
Both derivatives therefore vanish only at $(0,0)$. At that point,

$$
H_f(0,0)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det H_f(0,0)=-1.
$$

The origin is a saddle. In this example, the derivative factors prove that
the positive exponential adds no stationary points; positivity of a factor
alone would not guarantee that for every product.

</Example>

<Example slug="revisit-polynomial-stationary-points" of="classify-stationary-points" difficulty="exam">

### Revisiting the polynomial stationary points

For

$$
f(x,y)=8x^3+y^2-6xy-2y+3,
$$

the Hessian and its determinant are

$$
H_f(x,y)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
48x&-6\\
-6&2
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det H_f(x,y)=96x-36.
$$

At $(1,4)$,

$$
\det H_f(1,4)=60>0,
\qquad
f_{xx}(1,4)=48>0,
$$

so $(1,4)$ is a local minimum. At
$(-1/4,1/4)$,

$$
\det H_f\left(-\frac14,\frac14\right)=-60<0,
$$

so that point is a saddle.

</Example>

Local classification compares values only near one point. On a closed and
bounded region, the absolute maximum and minimum also require every boundary
piece; the [global
extrema](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/global-extrema) page sets out
that search.
