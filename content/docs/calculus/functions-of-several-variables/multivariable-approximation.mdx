This page reads the [tangent
plane](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/geometry-and-approximation) as
the first-order Taylor approximation of a function and then adds terms of
higher degree. The tangent plane captures change that is linear in the
displacement from the base point. It is usually enough for a nearby estimate,
but a higher-order Taylor polynomial is needed when the first-order change
cancels or when curvature controls the error.

The construction extends the one-variable [Taylor
polynomial](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-polynomials): the term
$\frac12f''(a)(x-a)^2$ becomes the quadratic form
$\frac12\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}H_f(\mathbf a)\mathbf h$, so the
[Hessian](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/local-extrema) is the
multivariable curvature matrix.

## Linear approximation and the tangent-plane model

<Method slug="multivariable-linear-approximation" of="graph-tangent-plane">

### Approximate a function linearly

<Statement>

For a differentiable $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$ and a base point
$\mathbf{a}=(x_0,y_0)$, approximate nearby values by

$$
f(\mathbf{x})
\approx
f(\mathbf{a})
+\nabla f(\mathbf{a})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}).
$$

To use the approximation, evaluate $f$ and its first partial derivatives at
$\mathbf{a}$, then substitute the displacement $\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}$.

</Statement>

The expression is the equation of the tangent plane with the output written
as a function of the input. Differentiability means that the omitted error,
divided by $\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}\|$, tends to zero as
$\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{a}$. Thus one linear model works for changes in every
direction, not just along the coordinate axes.

</Method>

<Example slug="quadratic-linear-approximation" of="multivariable-linear-approximation" difficulty="routine">

### Linearise $(x-y)^2$

Let

$$
f(x,y)=x^2+y^2-2xy=(x-y)^2.
$$

At $(1,3)$,

$$
f(1,3)=4,
\qquad
f_x=2x-2y,
\qquad
f_y=2y-2x,
$$

so

$$
f_x(1,3)=-4,
\qquad
f_y(1,3)=4.
$$

The tangent-plane approximation is

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(x,y)
&=4-4(x-1)+4(y-3)\\
&=-4x+4y-4.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, close to $(1,3)$,

$$
{(x-y)^2\approx -4x+4y-4}.
$$

Writing $x=1+h$ and $y=3+k$ shows exactly what the linear model omits:

$$
f(1+h,3+k)=4-4h+4k+(h-k)^2.
$$

The approximation keeps the constant and first-degree terms and discards the
quadratic error $(h-k)^2$. This also shows why the estimate improves faster
than the displacement as $(h,k)\to(0,0)$.

</Example>

## Higher-order terms record curvature

To record curvature and interactions between coordinate changes, we keep
monomials up to a chosen total degree and collect the second-order derivatives
in a matrix.

<Definition slug="total-degree">

### Total degree

<Statement>

The total degree of the monomial $x^ny^m$ is $n+m$. The total degree of a
polynomial is the largest total degree among its monomial terms.

</Statement>

For example, in

$$
p(x,y)=1+x-y+x^2-xy+x^3y^2,
$$

the term $x^3y^2$ has total degree $5$, so $\deg p=5$. This is the degree
used when deciding which terms belong in a multivariable Taylor polynomial.

</Definition>

<Definition slug="multivariable-hessian">

### Hessian matrix

<Statement>

For $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$, the Hessian at
$\mathbf{x}_0=(x_0,y_0)$ is

$$
H_f(\mathbf{x}_0)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
f_{xx}(\mathbf{x}_0)&f_{xy}(\mathbf{x}_0)\\
f_{yx}(\mathbf{x}_0)&f_{yy}(\mathbf{x}_0)
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

</Statement>

When the mixed partials are continuous, $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$, so the Hessian is
symmetric. It records the second-order changes omitted by the linear
approximation and its determinant classifies [stationary points](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/local-extrema) through the quadratic part of the local Taylor model.

</Definition>

<Definition slug="taylor-polynomial-several-variables" of="multivariable-linear-approximation,multivariable-hessian">

### Taylor polynomial through degree three

<Statement>

Let $\mathbf{a}=(x_0,y_0)$,
$h_x=x-x_0$ and $h_y=y-y_0$. If $f$ has continuous partial
derivatives through order three near $\mathbf{a}$, its Taylor polynomial through total degree
three is

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_3(x,y)
={}&f(\mathbf{a})
+f_x(\mathbf{a})h_x
+f_y(\mathbf{a})h_y\\
&+\frac{1}{2!}
\left(
f_{xx}(\mathbf{a})h_x^2
+2f_{xy}(\mathbf{a})h_xh_y
+f_{yy}(\mathbf{a})h_y^2
\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{3!}
\left(
f_{xxx}(\mathbf{a})h_x^3
+3f_{xxy}(\mathbf{a})h_x^2h_y
+3f_{xyy}(\mathbf{a})h_xh_y^2
+f_{yyy}(\mathbf{a})h_y^3
\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The degree-two part can be written with the Hessian as

$$
T_2(\mathbf{x})
=
f(\mathbf{a})
+\nabla f(\mathbf{a})\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a})
+\frac12
(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a})^{\mathsf T}
H_f(\mathbf{a})
(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}).
$$

</Statement>

The quadratic and cubic parts refine the tangent-plane model by the terms of
total degree two and three.

To see where the mixed coefficients come from, apply the one-variable Taylor
formula to $g(t)=f(\mathbf{a}+t\mathbf h)$. Then
$g''(0)=f_{xx}h_x^2+2f_{xy}h_xh_y+f_{yy}h_y^2$;
the two mixed terms arise by differentiating once in each coordinate.
The cubic coefficients count the three orders in which two $x$-derivatives
and one $y$-derivative can occur, or vice versa.

Under the stated $C^3$ assumption, the omitted remainder satisfies

$$
f(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)=T_3(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)+o(\|\mathbf h\|^3)
\qquad(\mathbf h\to\mathbf0).
$$

If $f$ is $C^4$ near $\mathbf a$, the remainder improves to
$O(\|\mathbf h\|^4)$ for sufficiently small $\mathbf h$.

</Definition>

## Construct multivariable Taylor polynomials

<Method slug="construct-multivariable-taylor-polynomial" of="taylor-polynomial-several-variables">

### Construct a multivariable Taylor polynomial

<Statement>

To construct the Taylor polynomial through degree $n\le3$ about
$\mathbf{a}$:

1. choose the centre and the required total degree;
2. calculate the partial derivatives through that order;
3. evaluate them at $\mathbf{a}$;
4. retain the terms through total degree $n$ in the formula above.

</Statement>

The result agrees with $f$ at $\mathbf{a}$ and matches the relevant partial
derivatives there. It is a local approximation, not generally an identity on
the whole domain.

</Method>

<Example slug="exponential-difference-taylor" of="construct-multivariable-taylor-polynomial" difficulty="routine">

### $e^{x-y}$ near the origin

Let

$$
f(x,y)=e^{x-y}.
$$

Set $u=x-y$. The one-variable exponential expansion in $u$ gives

$$
{
T_3(x,y)
=1+(x-y)+\frac{(x-y)^2}{2}
+\frac{(x-y)^3}{6}
}.
$$

Each power $(x-y)^k$ has total degree $k$, so truncating the series in $u$
after $u^3$ keeps exactly the terms of total degree at most three.

</Example>

<Example slug="polynomial-taylor-exact" of="construct-multivariable-taylor-polynomial" difficulty="routine">

### A function that is already a polynomial

For

$$
f(x,y)=x^2y,
$$

the function has total degree three. Hence its Taylor polynomial through
degree three about $(0,0)$ is exact:

$$
{T_3(x,y)=x^2y}.
$$

The derivative construction gives the same result: all lower-degree terms
vanish at the origin and the third-order term reproduces $x^2y$.

</Example>

<Example slug="three-variable-exponential-taylor" of="construct-multivariable-taylor-polynomial" difficulty="routine">

### An exponential of three variables

For

$$
f(x,y,z)=e^{x+y+z}
$$

near $(0,0,0)$, set $s=x+y+z$. The degree-two Taylor polynomial is

$$
{
T_2(x,y,z)
=1+(x+y+z)+\frac12(x+y+z)^2
}.
$$

The total degree counts the total number of factors drawn from all coordinate
increments, so the same construction works in any number of variables.

</Example>

Like the one-variable [Taylor polynomials](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-polynomials), this construction matches derivatives at one centre; [interpolation](/algebra/vector-spaces/data-fitting) instead matches values at distinct nodes. When the gradient vanishes, the quadratic part above becomes the leading term that classifies [stationary points](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/local-extrema).
