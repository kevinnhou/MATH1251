This page sets up first-order calculus for functions of several variables,
where a change in the input has a direction as well as a size. [Partial
derivatives](#partial-derivatives-and-the-gradient) measure change along the
coordinate directions and the [gradient](#partial-derivatives-and-the-gradient)
collects them into one vector.

The gradient also gives geometry: it is normal to every [level
set](#functions-graphs-and-level-sets), meaning perpendicular to all its
tangent directions. That fact leads to the [tangent
plane](#tangent-plane-to-a-graph), the first local model of a surface.

## Functions, graphs and level sets

<Definition slug="several-variable-function">

### Function of several variables

<Statement>

A function of several variables is a map

$$
f:D\to\mathbb{R},\qquad D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n,
\qquad
\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\mapsto f(\mathbf{x}),
$$

where $n\ge2$ and $D$ is the domain of allowed inputs.

</Statement>

When $n=2$, the graph of $f$ is the surface $z=f(x,y)$ in
$\mathbb{R}^3$. A function of three variables has a graph in
$\mathbb{R}^4$, which is harder to visualise, but the coordinate and vector
formulas remain the same.

</Definition>

An explicit graph solves for one coordinate. An implicit description instead
keeps all coordinates in one equation; this is the setting for level-set
normals.

<Definition slug="multivariable-level-set">

### Level set

<Statement>

For $F:D\to\mathbb{R}$, with $D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ and $c\in\mathbb{R}$, the level set of $F$
at level $c$ is

$$
\{\mathbf{x}\in D:F(\mathbf{x})=c\}.
$$

</Statement>

For example, the graph $z=x^2-y^2$ is the level set
$x^2-y^2-z=0$ and the unit sphere is the level set
$x^2+y^2+z^2=1$. The function defining the level set need not be the same
function whose graph we are studying.

</Definition>

Level sets also appear in [exact differential
equations](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/seen#seen-exact-equations-linear-approximation-level-curves):
the solution curves of an exact equation with potential $F$ are the level
curves $F(x,y)=c$, so the geometry below applies to them.

## Partial derivatives and the gradient

<Definition slug="multivariable-partial-derivative">

### Partial derivative

<Statement>

For $f:D\to\mathbb{R}$ with $D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$, at an interior point
$\mathbf{x}\in D$, the partial derivative with respect to
$x_i$ is the one-variable derivative obtained by varying
$x_i$ and holding every other coordinate fixed:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\mathbf{x})
=
\lim_{h\to0}
\frac{
f(x_1,\ldots,x_i+h,\ldots,x_n)-f(\mathbf{x})
}{h},
$$

when the limit exists.

</Statement>

The ordinary sum, product, quotient and chain rules still apply. Treat every
variable other than the one being differentiated as a constant. For two
variables we write
$f_x=\partial f/\partial x$ and $f_y=\partial f/\partial y$.

</Definition>

<Definition slug="multivariable-gradient" of="multivariable-partial-derivative">

### Gradient

<Statement>

For $g:D\to\mathbb{R}$ with $D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$, at points where all
first partial derivatives exist, the gradient is the vector-valued function

$$
\nabla g(\mathbf{x})
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x_n}(\mathbf{x})
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

</Statement>

When $g$ is differentiable, the gradient has a second interpretation. For a
unit direction $\mathbf u$, the rate of change along the line
$\mathbf x+t\mathbf u$ is

$$
D_{\mathbf u}g(\mathbf x)
=\left.\frac{d}{dt}g(\mathbf x+t\mathbf u)\right|_{t=0}
=\nabla g(\mathbf x)\cdot\mathbf u.
$$

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives
$|D_{\mathbf u}g|\le\|\nabla g\|$. When the gradient is non-zero, the
maximum directional derivative is attained by taking $\mathbf u$ in the
gradient direction (and the most negative rate in the opposite direction).
Thus $\nabla g$ points in the direction of steepest increase and its length is
that maximum directional rate.

The linear map that gives first-order change is the **total derivative** (or
derivative map). At a differentiable point it is represented by the gradient:

$$
Dg(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{h}]
=\nabla g(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{h},
\qquad
D_{\mathbf{u}}g(\mathbf{x})=Dg(\mathbf{x})[\mathbf{u}].
$$

Differentiability means this one linear map approximates change in every
direction. The coordinate partial derivatives alone do not establish that, so
they do not guarantee the directional-derivative formula above.

</Definition>

<Example slug="multivariable-gradient-calculation" of="multivariable-gradient" difficulty="routine">

### Computing a gradient

Let

$$
f(x,y,z)=x^2y+2xz+\cos(z^3).
$$

Holding the other variables fixed gives

$$
f_x=2xy+2z,
\qquad
f_y=x^2,
\qquad
f_z=2x-3z^2\sin(z^3).
$$

Therefore

$$
\nabla f(x,y,z)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2xy+2z\\
x^2\\
2x-3z^2\sin(z^3)
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At a specified point this vector also predicts directional change. For
example,

$$
\nabla f(1,0,0)
=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\2\end{pmatrix},
$$

so a small displacement $\mathbf h$ from $(1,0,0)$ changes $f$ to first
order by $\nabla f(1,0,0)\cdot\mathbf h$. This is the same linear term used
later in the tangent-plane approximation.

</Example>

Second derivatives describe how the first-order rates change. The order of
mixed differentiation can be exchanged when the relevant mixed partials are
continuous, but domain restrictions must still be recorded.

<Example slug="mixed-partial-calculation" of="multivariable-partial-derivative" difficulty="routine">

### First and second partial derivatives

Consider

$$
f(x,y)=e^{xy}+x^2y.
$$

Treating the other coordinate as constant gives

$$
f_x=ye^{xy}+2xy,
\qquad
f_y=xe^{xy}+x^2.
$$

Differentiate $f_x$ with respect to $y$ and $f_y$ with respect to $x$:

$$
f_{xy}=e^{xy}+xye^{xy}+2x
=f_{yx}.
$$

The two mixed partials agree, as Clairaut's theorem guarantees because they
are continuous everywhere. The other second partials are
$f_{xx}=y^2e^{xy}+2y$ and $f_{yy}=x^2e^{xy}$.

</Example>

<Example slug="domain-restricted-partials" of="multivariable-partial-derivative" difficulty="routine">

### Domain restrictions are part of the derivative

For

$$
f(x,y,z)=\frac{x\ln z}{y^2},
$$

the domain requires $y\ne0$ and $z>0$. The first partial derivatives are

$$
f_x=\frac{\ln z}{y^2},
\qquad
f_y=-\frac{2x\ln z}{y^3},
\qquad
f_z=\frac{x}{y^2z},
$$

and the second partial derivatives are

$$
f_{xx}=0,
\qquad
f_{xy}=-\frac{2\ln z}{y^3},
\qquad
f_{xz}=\frac{1}{y^2z},
$$

$$
f_{yy}=\frac{6x\ln z}{y^4},
\qquad
f_{yz}=-\frac{2x}{y^3z},
\qquad
f_{zz}=-\frac{x}{y^2z^2}.
$$

</Example>

## Gradients and level-set geometry

Suppose $\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)$ is a differentiable curve that stays in a
level set. The value of the defining function does not change along the
curve, so differentiating that constant value forces the gradient to be
orthogonal to the curve's tangent.

<Theorem slug="gradient-normal-level-set" of="multivariable-gradient,multivariable-level-set">

### Gradient normal to a level set

<Statement>

Let $F:D\to\mathbb{R}$ be $C^1$ on an open neighbourhood of
$\mathbf{x}_0$ and let

$$
S=\{\mathbf{x}\in D:F(\mathbf{x})=c\}.
$$

If $\mathbf{x}_0\in S$ and
$\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_0)\ne\mathbf{0}$, then
$\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_0)$ is normal to $S$ at $\mathbf{x}_0$.

</Statement>

The non-zero-gradient condition makes the level set regular at the point.
When the gradient vanishes, the level set may have a corner, crossing or
other singular behaviour for which no single gradient direction gives a
normal.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="gradient-normal-level-set">

Let $\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)$ be a differentiable curve in $S$ with
$\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t_0)=\mathbf{x}_0$. Since $F$ has the constant value $c$
along the curve,

$$
F(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))=c.
$$

The chain rule gives

$$
\nabla F(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t_0))
\cdot
\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t_0)=0.
$$

Thus the gradient is perpendicular to every tangent vector supplied by a
curve in the level set. It is therefore a normal vector at
$\mathbf{x}_0$.

</Proof>

<Example slug="circle-level-set-normal" of="gradient-normal-level-set" difficulty="routine">

### The normal to the unit circle

The unit circle is the level set

$$
F(x,y)=x^2+y^2-1=0.
$$

Its gradient is

$$
\nabla F(x,y)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2x\\
2y
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At

$$
\mathbf{x}_0=(\cos\varepsilon,\sin\varepsilon),
$$

a normal vector is

$$
\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_0)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2\cos\varepsilon\\
2\sin\varepsilon
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Any non-zero scalar multiple is also normal, so the radius
$(\cos\varepsilon,\sin\varepsilon)$ gives the same direction.

</Example>

An explicit graph can be treated as a level surface by moving everything to
one side:

$$
F(x,y,z)=z-f(x,y)=0.
$$

The resulting normal is $(-f_x,-f_y,1)$. Equivalently, parametrising the graph
by $\mathbf{r}(x,y)=(x,y,f(x,y))$ gives tangent vectors

$$
\mathbf{v}_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
1\\
0\\
f_x
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{v}_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
1\\
f_y
\end{pmatrix},
$$

whose cross product is the same normal vector.

<Theorem slug="graph-tangent-plane" of="gradient-normal-level-set">

### Tangent plane to a graph

<Statement>

If $f$ is differentiable at $\mathbf{a}=(x_0,y_0)$ and
$z_0=f(x_0,y_0)$, then the tangent plane to $z=f(x,y)$ at
$(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ is

$$
z
=f(x_0,y_0)
+f_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0)
+f_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0).
$$

</Statement>

The point-normal form uses the normal $(-f_x(\mathbf{a}),-f_y(\mathbf{a}),1)$.
The formula is also the first-order Taylor approximation, so the geometric
tangent plane and the analytic linear model are the same object.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="graph-tangent-plane">

The graph is the level surface $F=z-f(x,y)=0$. Its gradient at the base point
is

$$
\nabla F(x_0,y_0,z_0)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
-f_x(x_0,y_0)\\
-f_y(x_0,y_0)\\
1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The point-normal equation is therefore

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
-f_x(x_0,y_0)\\
-f_y(x_0,y_0)\\
1
\end{pmatrix}
\cdot
\begin{pmatrix}
x-x_0\\
y-y_0\\
z-z_0
\end{pmatrix}
=0.
$$

Solving for $z$ and using $z_0=f(x_0,y_0)$ gives the stated equation.

</Proof>

<Example slug="implicit-tangent-plane" of="gradient-normal-level-set" difficulty="exam">

### Tangent plane to an implicit surface

Consider

$$
x^2+y^2-xyz=1.
$$

Set

$$
F(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2-xyz-1.
$$

Then

$$
\nabla F(x,y,z)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2x-yz\\
2y-xz\\
-xy
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The point $(1,2,2)$ lies on the surface because $1^2+2^2-(1)(2)(2)=1$.
At this point,

$$
\nabla F(1,2,2)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
-2\\
2\\
-2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The parallel vector $(1,-1,1)$ is a simpler normal. Hence the tangent plane
through $(1,2,2)$ is

$$
(1,-1,1)\cdot
\begin{pmatrix}
x-1\\
y-2\\
z-2
\end{pmatrix}
=0,
$$

or

$$
{x-y+z=1}.
$$

</Example>
