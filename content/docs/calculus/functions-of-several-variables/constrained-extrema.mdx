This page optimises a function on a curve given by an equation $g(x,y)=0$,
without parameterising the curve. The feasible points form a level curve of
$g$, so the [gradient-normal
theorem](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/geometry-and-approximation)
makes $\nabla g$ normal to it. At a constrained extremum, $\nabla f$ must point
along the same normal.

The resulting Lagrange multiplier method replaces the [boundary
parameterisation](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/global-extrema)
whenever $g=0$ is the natural description of a boundary. The page ends with
distance problems.

## The multiplier condition

<Definition slug="constrained-extremum">

### Constrained extremum

<Statement>

Let $f$ be an objective function and let

$$
C=\{(x,y):g(x,y)=0\}
$$

be its feasible set. A constrained maximum or minimum of $f$ is a maximum or
minimum of $f$ when the comparison is restricted to points in $C$.

</Statement>

The constraint limits the directions in which we may move. A point can fail
to be an unconstrained stationary point and still be a constrained extremum,
because the directions in which $f$ would increase or decrease are not
feasible.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="lagrange-multiplier-theorem" of="constrained-extremum,gradient-normal-level-set">

### Lagrange multipliers

<Statement>

Suppose $C=\{(x,y):g(x,y)=0\}$ is a nonempty closed and bounded curve and
$f$ and $g$ have continuous first partial derivatives on an open set
containing $C$. Then $f$ attains a maximum and a minimum on $C$. At each
constrained extremum where

$$
\nabla g(x,y)\ne\mathbf{0},
$$

there is a scalar $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ such that

$$
{\nabla f(x,y)=\lambda\nabla g(x,y)}
$$

together with

$$
{g(x,y)=0}.
$$

</Statement>

The equation says that the gradients are parallel, not that they have equal
length: $\lambda$ records a scale and possibly a reversal of direction.

The regularity condition $\nabla g\ne\mathbf{0}$ is essential. A feasible
point where $\nabla g=\mathbf{0}$ is a singular constraint point, where the
multiplier condition is not guaranteed, so it must be checked separately
along with any corners or endpoints introduced by an inequality constraint.
Compactness and continuity still guarantee that the extrema exist.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="lagrange-multiplier-theorem">

At a regular point of $C$, at least one of $g_x,g_y$ is nonzero, so the
constraint is locally a smooth curve. A tangent vector $\mathbf{v}$ to that
curve satisfies

$$
\nabla g\cdot\mathbf{v}=0
$$

because $g$ is constant along the constraint. At a constrained extremum, the
first-order change of $f$ must also vanish for every feasible tangent vector:

$$
\nabla f\cdot\mathbf{v}=0.
$$

In the plane, both gradients are therefore normal to the same tangent line.
Since $\nabla g\ne\mathbf{0}$, the two normals are parallel, so
$\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ for some scalar $\lambda$. The existence of
maximum and minimum values follows by applying the extreme-value theorem to
the closed and bounded constraint curve.

</Proof>

<Method slug="lagrange-multiplier-method" of="lagrange-multiplier-theorem">

### Optimise subject to one equality constraint

<Statement>

To optimise $f$ subject to $g=0$:

1. check where $\nabla g=\mathbf{0}$ on the constraint and retain any such
   singular feasible points for separate analysis;
2. compute $\nabla f$ and $\nabla g$;
3. solve
   $$
   \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g,
   \qquad
   g=0;
   $$
4. evaluate $f$ at every solution;
5. compare the values to identify the constrained maximum and minimum.

</Statement>

The system has three unknowns $x$, $y$ and $\lambda$ in the two-variable
case. Symmetry and factorisation often reduce the algebra. Solving the
multiplier equations produces candidates; it does not replace checking
singular points or comparing objective values.

</Method>

<Example slug="disk-lagrange-calculation" of="lagrange-multiplier-method" difficulty="exam">

### Re-solving the disk problem with multipliers

Optimise

$$
f(x,y)=x^2+4xy+y^2
$$

subject to

$$
g(x,y)=x^2+y^2-1=0.
$$

The gradients are

$$
\nabla f
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2x+4y\\
4x+2y
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\nabla g
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2x\\
2y
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The multiplier equations are

$$
2x+4y=2\lambda x,
\qquad
4x+2y=2\lambda y,
\qquad
x^2+y^2=1.
$$

After dividing the first two equations by $2$,

$$
(1-\lambda)x+2y=0,
\qquad
2x+(1-\lambda)y=0.
$$

The unit-circle constraint rules out $(x,y)=(0,0)$, so the coefficient
matrix must have determinant zero:

$$
(1-\lambda)^2-4=0.
$$

Thus $\lambda=3$ or $\lambda=-1$.

If $\lambda=3$, then $y=x$. The constraint gives $2x^2=1$, so the two
same-sign points

$$
\left(\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac1{\sqrt2}\right),
\qquad
\left(-\frac1{\sqrt2},-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)
$$

give $f=3$.

If $\lambda=-1$, then $y=-x$. The two opposite-sign points

$$
\left(\frac1{\sqrt2},-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right),
\qquad
\left(-\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)
$$

give $f=-1$. This reproduces the boundary part of the [closed-disk
example](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/global-extrema), found there
by parameterisation.

</Example>

## Distance optimisation

<Method slug="squared-distance-method" of="constrained-extremum">

### Minimise a distance through its square

<Statement>

For a fixed point $\mathbf{p}$, minimise the squared distance

$$
D(\mathbf{x})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{p}\|^2
$$

instead of the distance $\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{p}\|$. Since the square-root
function is increasing on non-negative values, the points that minimise or
maximise the two objectives are the same.

</Statement>

The squared expression avoids a square root and usually gives a polynomial
objective. If the constraint has a visible normal direction, the shortest
segment can sometimes be found geometrically before writing the multiplier
system.

</Method>

<Example slug="closest-point-on-plane" of="squared-distance-method,gradient-normal-level-set" difficulty="exam">

### The closest point on a plane

Find the point on

$$
2x-y+z=3
$$

closest to

$$
\mathbf{p}=(-1,1,2).
$$

The plane has normal vector

$$
\mathbf{n}=(2,-1,1).
$$

The shortest segment from $\mathbf{p}$ to the plane is perpendicular to the
plane, so the desired point has the form

$$
\mathbf{x}
=\mathbf{p}+t\mathbf{n}
=(-1,1,2)+t(2,-1,1).
$$

Substituting into the plane equation gives

$$
2(-1+2t)-(1-t)+(2+t)=3,
$$

so $-1+6t=3$ and $t=2/3$. Therefore

$$
{
\mathbf{x}
=\left(\frac13,\frac13,\frac83\right)
}.
$$

This nearest point is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{p}$ onto the
plane. The same point results from minimising

$$
(x+1)^2+(y-1)^2+(z-2)^2
$$

subject to $2x-y+z=3$, but the normal direction makes the geometric solution
shorter.

</Example>

<Example slug="quartic-distance-extrema" of="squared-distance-method,lagrange-multiplier-method" difficulty="challenge">

### Closest and furthest points on a quartic curve

Find the points on

$$
x^4+7x^2y^2+y^4=1
$$

that are closest to and furthest from the origin. Minimise and maximise

$$
d^2=x^2+y^2.
$$

Both expressions depend only on $x^2$ and $y^2$. Set

$$
u=x^2,
\qquad
v=y^2,
\qquad
u,v\ge0.
$$

The problem becomes optimising $s=u+v$ subject to

$$
u^2+7uv+v^2=1.
$$

At an interior constrained extremum in the first quadrant,

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1\\
1
\end{pmatrix}
=\mu
\begin{pmatrix}
2u+7v\\
7u+2v
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The two components on the right must be equal, so

$$
2u+7v=7u+2v,
\qquad
u=v.
$$

Substituting into the constraint gives

$$
9u^2=1,
\qquad
u=v=\frac13,
$$

and hence $d^2=u+v=2/3$.

The boundary cases in the first quadrant are $u=0$ or $v=0$. The constraint
then gives the other variable as $1$, so $d^2=1$. The feasible set in
$(u,v)$ is closed and bounded and these interior and boundary cases exhaust
its extrema. Restoring the signs independently, the closest points are

$$
{
\left(\pm\frac1{\sqrt3},\pm\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)
}
$$

with distance $\sqrt{2/3}$, while the furthest points are

$$
{(\pm1,0),\ (0,\pm1)}
$$

with distance $1$.

</Example>

<Example slug="parabola-distance-problem" of="squared-distance-method" difficulty="challenge">

### The closest point on a parabola

Let

$$
\mathbf{x}_0=(0,a),
\qquad
a>0,
$$

and find the point on $y=x^2$ closest to $\mathbf{x}_0$. A point on the
parabola is

$$
\mathbf{p}(x)=(x,x^2).
$$

The squared distance is

$$
D(x)
=\|\mathbf{p}(x)-\mathbf{x}_0\|^2
=x^2+(x^2-a)^2.
$$

Differentiate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D'(x)
&=2x+4x(x^2-a)\\
&=2x(1+2x^2-2a).
\end{aligned}
$$

The stationary values satisfy

$$
x=0
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
x^2=a-\frac12.
$$

The second possibility exists only when $a\ge1/2$. Since
$D(x)\to\infty$ as $|x|\to\infty$, compare the stationary candidates:

$$
D(0)=a^2,
$$

whereas at $x^2=a-1/2$,

$$
D(x)=a-\frac14.
$$

Their difference is

$$
a^2-\left(a-\frac14\right)
=\left(a-\frac12\right)^2\ge0.
$$

Therefore, if $0<a\le1/2$, the closest point is

$$
{(0,0)}.
$$

If $a>1/2$, the two closest points are

$$
{
\left(
\pm\sqrt{a-\frac12},
\ a-\frac12
\right)
}.
$$

At $a=1/2$ the two non-zero candidates merge with the origin.

</Example>
