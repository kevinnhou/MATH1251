### A composite function and a partial differential equation

Let $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be an odd differentiable function. Define

$$
F:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R,
\qquad
F(x,y)=f(x^2+xy+2y^2).
$$

(a) Differentiating $F$ with respect to $y$ gives

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}
=g(x,y)f'(x^2+xy+2y^2).
$$

Find $g(x,y)$.

(b) $F$ satisfies

$$
g(x,y)\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}
-h(x,y)\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}=0.
$$

Find a polynomial $h(x,y)$ that makes this identity hold for every such $f$.

(c) Given that $F(x,x)=-6x^2$ for all $x\in\mathbb R$, find the rule for $f$ and then the rule for $F$.

#### Solution

Set

$$
q(x,y)=x^2+xy+2y^2,
\qquad
F(x,y)=f(q(x,y)).
$$

The function $q$ is a polynomial, so it is differentiable everywhere. Since $f$ is differentiable everywhere, the multivariable chain rule applies throughout $\mathbb R^2$:

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}=f'(q)\frac{\partial q}{\partial x},
\qquad
\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}=f'(q)\frac{\partial q}{\partial y}.
$$

#### (a) Finding $g$

Differentiate the inner function with respect to $y$, holding $x$ fixed:

$$
\frac{\partial q}{\partial y}=x+4y.
$$

Therefore

$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}
=(x+4y)f'(q(x,y))
=(x+4y)f'(x^2+xy+2y^2),
$$

so

$$
{g(x,y)=x+4y}.
$$

#### (b) Finding $h$

The other partial derivative is

$$
\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}=2x+y,
\qquad
\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}
=(2x+y)f'(q(x,y)).
$$

Substituting both derivatives into the proposed equation gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(x,y)\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}
-h(x,y)\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}
&=(x+4y)(2x+y)f'(q)\\
&\quad{}-h(x,y)(x+4y)f'(q).
\end{aligned}
$$

Choosing

$$
{h(x,y)=2x+y}
$$

makes the two terms identical, so their difference is zero:

$$
(x+4y)(2x+y)f'(q)-(2x+y)(x+4y)f'(q)=0.
$$

For a particular $f$, the equation need not determine $h$: if $f$ is constant,
both partial derivatives of $F$ vanish and any $h$ works. The displayed
polynomial works for every differentiable $f$.

#### (c) Recovering $f$ and $F$

Putting $y=x$ in the definition of $F$ gives

$$
x^2+xy+2y^2\big|_{y=x}
=x^2+x^2+2x^2
=4x^2.
$$

The supplied condition therefore becomes

$$
f(4x^2)=-6x^2
\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb R.
$$

The quantity $4x^2$ ranges over $[0,\infty)$. If $t\ge0$, choose
$x=\frac{\sqrt t}{2}$ to obtain

$$
f(t)=-6\left(\frac{\sqrt t}{2}\right)^2
= -\frac32t.
$$

The hypothesis that $f$ is odd extends this rule to negative inputs. If
$t<0$, then $-t>0$, so

$$
f(t)=-f(-t)
=-\left(-\frac32(-t)\right)
=-\frac32t.
$$

The same formula also gives $f(0)=0$, so altogether

$$
{f(t)=-\frac32t\qquad(t\in\mathbb R)}.
$$

Substitution into $F(x,y)=f(q(x,y))$ gives

$$
{
F(x,y)
=-\frac32(x^2+xy+2y^2)
}.
$$

As a check,

$$
F(x,x)=-\frac32(4x^2)=-6x^2,
$$

and the partial derivatives of this final expression satisfy the equation by the cancellation in part (b).

The diagonal condition samples $f$ only at non-negative arguments, since
$x^2+xy+2y^2=(x+y/2)^2+7y^2/4\ge0$. Oddness supplies the missing negative half-line.

The key course idea is the [partial derivative](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/geometry-and-approximation) of a composite function: differentiate the outer function and multiply by the partial derivative of the inner function.

### Critical points depending on a parameter

Consider

$$
f(x,y)=x^4+y^4-a(x-y)^2,
\qquad a\ne0.
$$

#### (a)

Find $a$ such that $f$ has at least two critical points. Give the answer as a set or an interval.

#### (b)

For the case where $f$ has multiple critical points, classify the saddle points, local minimum points and local maximum points and give their coordinates.

#### Solution

Because $f$ is a polynomial, its gradient exists everywhere, so the critical
points are exactly the solutions of $f_x=f_y=0$. These equations also give the
condition on $a$. The Hessian then classifies the non-zero candidates; at the
origin the Hessian test is inconclusive, so we compare values along two lines.

The first partial derivatives are

$$
f_x=4x^3-2a(x-y),
\qquad
f_y=4y^3+2a(x-y).
$$

At a stationary point,

$$
4x^3-2a(x-y)=0,
\qquad
4y^3+2a(x-y)=0.
$$

Adding these equations eliminates the parameter term:

$$
4x^3+4y^3=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x^3+y^3=0.
$$

Over the reals,

$$
x^3+y^3=(x+y)(x^2-xy+y^2),
$$

and

$$
x^2-xy+y^2
=\frac12\bigl((x-y)^2+x^2+y^2\bigr)
\ge0,
$$

with equality only at $(x,y)=(0,0)$. Hence every real stationary point
satisfies $y=-x$. Substituting this into the first stationary equation gives

$$
4x^3-2a(2x)=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
4x(x^2-a)=0.
$$

Thus $(0,0)$ is always a critical point. The other possibilities satisfy

$$
x^2=a.
$$

Since $x^2\ge0$ and $a\ne0$, these additional real points exist exactly when
$a>0$. Therefore the answer to part (a) is

$$
{a\in(0,\infty)}.
$$

For every such $a$, put $s=\sqrt a$. The complete list of critical points is

$$
(0,0),\qquad (s,-s),\qquad (-s,s).
$$

To classify the two non-zero points, compute the second partial derivatives:

$$
f_{xx}=12x^2-2a,
\qquad
f_{yy}=12y^2-2a,
\qquad
f_{xy}=f_{yx}=2a.
$$

At either point with $x^2=y^2=a$, the Hessian is

$$
H_f=
\begin{pmatrix}
10a&2a\\
2a&10a
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D=\det(H_f)=100a^2-4a^2=96a^2>0.
$$

Also, $f_{xx}=10a>0$. The second-order partial derivative test therefore
classifies both points as local minima:

$$
{(x,y)=(\sqrt a,-\sqrt a),\ (-\sqrt a,\sqrt a)
\text{ are local minimum points}.}
$$

At the origin,

$$
H_f(0,0)=
\begin{pmatrix}
-2a&2a\\
2a&-2a
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det H_f(0,0)=0,
$$

so the Hessian test does not decide the classification. Compare the function
along two lines through the origin. On $y=x$,

$$
f(t,t)=t^4+t^4=2t^4>0
\qquad(t\ne0),
$$

whereas on $y=-x$,

$$
f(t,-t)=2t^4-4at^2
=2t^2(t^2-2a)<0
$$

whenever $0<|t|<\sqrt{2a}$. Since $f(0,0)=0$, every neighbourhood of the
origin contains points where $f$ is larger and points where $f$ is smaller
than its value at the origin. Thus the origin is a saddle point.

There are no other critical points, so the requested classification for
$a>0$ is

$$
{\text{one saddle point at }(0,0),}
$$

$$
{\text{two local minimum points at }
(\sqrt a,-\sqrt a),\ (-\sqrt a,\sqrt a),}
$$

and

$$
{\text{no local maximum points}.}
$$

This follows the [local
extrema](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/local-extrema) workflow: the
stationary equations supply every candidate and the Hessian test classifies
the two non-zero points. Where the determinant is zero, a direct comparison
along lines exhibits the opposite signs that define a saddle. The parameter
$a$ decides whether the two minima exist.

### Finding and classifying a critical point

Let $f$ be the function

$$
f(x,y)=-4x^2+3y^2+x+3y-30.
$$

#### (a) Finding the single critical point

Find the single critical point of $f$.

Because $f$ is a polynomial, its first partial derivatives exist everywhere in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Compute them by treating the other variable as constant:

$$
f_x(x,y)=-8x+1,
\qquad
f_y(x,y)=6y+3.
$$

A stationary point and hence a critical point, must satisfy

$$
f_x(x,y)=0,
\qquad
f_y(x,y)=0.
$$

Solving the two equations separately gives

$$
-8x+1=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x=\frac18,
$$

and

$$
6y+3=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
y=-\frac12.
$$

Therefore the single critical point is

$$
{(x,y)=\left(\frac18,-\frac12\right)}.
$$

#### (b) Classifying the critical point

The possible choices are:

- local minimum;
- local maximum;
- inconclusive;
- saddle point.

To classify the point, use the second-order partial derivative test. The Hessian matrix is constant:

$$
H_f(x,y)
=
\begin{pmatrix}
f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\
f_{yx}&f_{yy}
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
-8&0\\
0&6
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its determinant is

$$
D=\det H_f
=(-8)(6)-0^2
=-48<0.
$$

Since $D<0$, the Hessian is indefinite and the second-order test classifies the critical point as a saddle point. Thus the correct option is **saddle point**.

The classification can be checked directly by completing the square:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x,y)
&=-4\left(x-\frac18\right)^2
+3\left(y+\frac12\right)^2
-\frac{491}{16}.
\end{aligned}
$$

At the critical point,

$$
f\left(\frac18,-\frac12\right)=-\frac{491}{16}.
$$

If we move in the $x$-direction by setting
$x=\frac18+t$ and $y=-\frac12$, then

$$
f\left(\frac18+t,-\frac12\right)
=-\frac{491}{16}-4t^2
<-\frac{491}{16}
\qquad(t\ne0).
$$

If instead we move in the $y$-direction by setting
$x=\frac18$ and $y=-\frac12+t$, then

$$
f\left(\frac18,-\frac12+t\right)
=-\frac{491}{16}+3t^2
>-\frac{491}{16}
\qquad(t\ne0).
$$

Arbitrarily close to the critical point there are therefore both larger and smaller function values, which is the defining behaviour of a saddle point.

The [local extrema](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/local-extrema) method locates the candidate with the stationary-point equations and classifies it with the Hessian determinant. The completed-square form shows the same thing directly: the surface curves down in one coordinate direction and up in the other.

</Example>
