This page answers two questions that the solution methods leave open. Does an
initial value problem have a solution near the initial point and do the data
determine it uniquely? For linear equations, it also explains why every
solution is one particular solution plus a homogeneous one.

The existence theorem accounts for the different IVP outcomes in [ODE
fundamentals](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/ode-fundamentals) and
its Lipschitz condition underlies the error estimates for [numerical
solutions](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/numerical-solutions). The
operator structure is the one used for [second-order constant-coefficient
equations](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/second-order-constant-coefficient-equations).

## Existence and uniqueness

<Definition slug="lipschitz-in-dependent-variable">

### Lipschitz condition in the dependent variable

<Statement>

A function $f(x,y)$ satisfies a Lipschitz condition in $y$ on a region when
there is a constant $M$ such that, for each fixed $x$ and all $y_1,y_2$ with
$(x,y_1)$ and $(x,y_2)$ in the region,

$$
|f(x,y_1)-f(x,y_2)|
\leq M|y_1-y_2|
$$

</Statement>

The condition controls how quickly the slope field can change as $y$ changes. It is stronger than continuity in $y$ and prevents two solution curves through the same initial point from separating while they remain in the region.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="local-existence-uniqueness" of="lipschitz-in-dependent-variable">

### Local existence and uniqueness

<Statement>

For the IVP

$$
y'=f(x,y),\qquad y(x_0)=y_0,
$$

if $f$ is continuous and satisfies a Lipschitz condition in $y$ on a
rectangle contained in its domain and containing $(x_0,y_0)$, then the IVP has
a unique solution on some interval around $x_0$. The conclusion is local; a
global solution needs the hypotheses to remain valid along the solution.

</Statement>

The Lipschitz condition is sufficient, not necessary: if it fails, uniqueness
may still hold.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="existence-uniqueness-examples" of="local-existence-uniqueness" difficulty="challenge">

### Reading the hypotheses

The theorem explains the four outcomes from ODE fundamentals:

- For $f(x,y)=x$, the function is continuous and independent of $y$, so the IVP $y'=x$, $y(0)=1$ is uniquely determined.
- For $f(y)=3y^{2/3}$ (interpreting the real power as
  $|y|^{2/3}$), the Lipschitz condition fails near $y=0$. This failure alone
  does not imply nonuniqueness, but here the IVP $y'=3y^{2/3}$, $y(0)=0$ has
  the distinct solutions $y=0$ and $y=x^3$, since
  $(x^3)'=3x^2=3|x^3|^{2/3}$.
- In $xy'=y-1$, the coefficient of $y'$ vanishes at $x=0$, so the equation cannot be put into the usual normal form $y'=f(x,y)$ at the initial point.
- The equation $|y'|+|y|=0$ forces $y=0$, which is incompatible with $y(0)=1$; no solution satisfies that data.

</Example>

## Linear differential operators

<Definition slug="linear-differential-operator">

### Linear differential operator

<Statement>

On an interval $I$ where its leading coefficient is nonzero, a linear
differential operator of order $n$ can be divided by that coefficient and
written in the normalised form

$$
T[y]
=y^{(n)}+a_{n-1}(x)y^{(n-1)}
+\cdots+a_1(x)y'+a_0(x)y.
$$

It satisfies

$$
T[y_1+y_2]=T[y_1]+T[y_2],
\qquad
T[\lambda y_1]=\lambda T[y_1].
$$

</Statement>

Linearity follows by distributing derivatives and collecting terms. The constant-coefficient operator $T[y]=y''+ay'+by$ is the operator used in [second-order constant-coefficient equations](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/second-order-constant-coefficient-equations) and its characteristic equation.

</Definition>

<Definition slug="operator-kernel" see="kernel-of-linear-map">

### Kernel of an operator

<Statement>

The kernel of a linear differential operator $T$ is

$$
\ker(T)=\{y:T[y]=0\}.
$$

</Statement>

The kernel is exactly the set of homogeneous solutions. Adding an element of
the kernel to a particular solution leaves the forcing unchanged. This is the
differential-equation version of the [kernel of a linear map](/algebra/linear-transformations/kernel-image-rank):
the kernel contains the inputs the operator sends to zero.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="affine-solution-set" of="linear-differential-operator,operator-kernel" see="solution-classes-of-ax-b">

### All forced solutions are an affine translate

<Statement>

If $T[y]=f$ and $y_p$ is one particular solution, then

$$
\{\text{all solutions of }T[y]=f\}
=y_p+\ker(T).
$$

Equivalently, every solution is $y_p+y_h$ for some homogeneous solution $y_h$.

</Statement>

The solution set is generally not a vector space when $f\neq0$, because it need not contain the zero function. It is a translate of the homogeneous solution space, as for [solution classes of linear systems](/algebra/linear-transformations/rank-nullity).

</Theorem>

<Proof of="affine-solution-set">

If $y_h\in\ker(T)$, then

$$
T[y_p+y_h]=T[y_p]+T[y_h]=f+0=f,
$$

so every element of $y_p+\ker(T)$ is a solution. Conversely, if $y$ is any solution of $T[y]=f$, then

$$
T[y-y_p]=T[y]-T[y_p]=f-f=0.
$$

Thus $y-y_p\in\ker(T)$, so $y=y_p+y_h$ for some $y_h\in\ker(T)$.

</Proof>

<Example slug="operator-first-order-example" of="affine-solution-set" difficulty="routine">

### A particular solution translated by the kernel

For

$$
y'+\frac{y}{x}=x,
$$

on an interval with $x\neq0$, take

$$
T[y]=y'+\frac{y}{x}.
$$

The homogeneous equation $T[y]=0$ has solution

$$
y_h=\frac{C}{x}.
$$

A particular solution is $y_p=x^2/3$, since

$$
T\left[\frac{x^2}{3}\right]
=\frac{2x}{3}+\frac{x}{3}
=x.
$$

Therefore the general solution is

$$
y=\frac{C}{x}+\frac{x^2}{3}.
$$

</Example>
