### Exact equations and level-set geometry

Answer the following questions about exact differential equations.

#### (a)

First classify the differential equation

$$
\left(3x^2y^2+7\cos(6x)\right)\,dx
+\left(\frac32x^4y+7e^y\right)\,dy=0.
$$

Select all options which correctly describe this equation:

- non-linear;
- first order;
- second order;
- initial value problem;
- boundary value problem;
- linear;
- constant coefficient;
- fifth order.

Now consider a differential equation of the form

$$
M(x,y)\,dx+N(x,y)\,dy=0. \tag{1}
$$

Here $M,N:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ are functions which are continuous
everywhere, differentiable everywhere and whose first-order partial
derivatives are also continuous everywhere.

#### (b)

Suppose equation (1) is not exact. To use the integrating-factor method,
determine when multiplying by a differentiable, nonvanishing function
$u(x,y)$ produces an equivalent exact equation. State a necessary condition.

#### (c)

Now suppose equation (1) is exact. For a constant $c$, its solution may be
written in the form

$$
F(x,y)=c,
$$

where $F(x,y)$ is to be constructed from $M$ and $N$.

#### (d)

Suppose (1) is an exact differential equation with solution $F(x,y)=c$, where
$c$ is a constant. This solution can be interpreted as a level curve of the
surface

$$
z=F(x,y).
$$

To understand the behaviour of the solution near a given point $(x_0,y_0)$,
Liu wishes to use Taylor polynomials to find an approximation.
Give the linear approximation to the surface near $(x_0,y_0)$.

#### (e)

For the geometry of solutions, consider the following example.

Let $F:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ be a potential for the exact differential
form below, with continuous second-order derivatives. Consider its level curve

$$
F(x,y)=20
$$

which passes through the point $(5,4)$ and for which locally the function
$y(x)$ is implicitly defined by the exact differential equation

$$
6x^2y^6\,dx+12x^3y^5\,dy=0.
$$

Compute a normal vector to the surface $z=F(x,y)$ at the point $(5,4,20)$.

#### Solution

The first equation is in differential form, so it contains $dx$ and $dy$ but
no derivative of order greater than one. It is first order. It is non-linear
because, after writing it as an equation for $dy/dx$, the terms $y^2$ and
$e^y$ occur non-linearly. It is neither an initial-value nor a boundary-value
problem because no data select a particular solution and it is not a
constant-coefficient linear equation. The correct selections in part (a) are

$$
{\text{non-linear and first order}.}
$$

For equation (1), multiplying by $u$ gives

$$
uM\,dx+uN\,dy=0.
$$

Exactness requires the mixed partials of the new coefficients to agree.
Therefore, a necessary condition is

$$
{
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\bigl(uM\bigr)
=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigl(uN\bigr)
}.
$$

Expanding both sides gives the equivalent first-order equation for the
integrating factor:

$$
M\,u_y+u\,M_y=N\,u_x+u\,N_x.
$$

The unknown function $u$ must satisfy this equation: multiplying by an
arbitrary non-zero function is not enough, because the new coefficients must
pass the exactness test. On a simply connected domain, with the required
continuous first partial derivatives, this equality is also sufficient for a
potential; nonvanishing $u$ is what preserves the original solution curves.

If the original equation is exact, there is a potential function $F$ such
that

$$
F_x=M,\qquad F_y=N.
$$

Integrating $F_x=M$ with respect to $x$, while treating $y$ as constant,
gives

$$
F(x,y)=\int M(x,y)\,dx+h(y),
$$

where $h$ is an arbitrary function of $y$. Differentiating this expression
with respect to $y$ gives

$$
F_y(x,y)
=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\int M(x,y)\,dx\right)+h'(y).
$$

Matching this with $N(x,y)$ determines $h'(y)$ and hence $h(y)$. Therefore
the requested construction is

$$
{
F(x,y)=\int M(x,y)\,dx+h(y),
\qquad F_y=N.
}
$$

The function $F$ is determined only up to an additive constant, which is
absorbed into the constant $c$ in $F(x,y)=c$.

For small changes $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$, the partial derivatives give the
first-order change in height:

$$
F(x_0+\Delta x,y_0+\Delta y)
\approx F(x_0,y_0)+F_x(x_0,y_0)\Delta x+F_y(x_0,y_0)\Delta y.
$$

Replacing $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ by $x-x_0$ and $y-y_0$ gives the tangent
plane to $z=F(x,y)$ at $(x_0,y_0,F(x_0,y_0))$:

$$
{
z=F(x_0,y_0)
+F_x(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0)
+F_y(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0)
}.
$$

Since $F_x=M$ and $F_y=N$, the same plane can be written as

$$
z=F(x_0,y_0)
+M(x_0,y_0)(x-x_0)
+N(x_0,y_0)(y-y_0).
$$

For part (e), the differential equation identifies the first partial
derivatives of a potential:

$$
F_x=6x^2y^6,
\qquad
F_y=12x^3y^5.
$$

Integrating the first expression with respect to $x$ gives

$$
F(x,y)=2x^3y^6+h(y).
$$

Differentiating with respect to $y$ gives

$$
F_y=12x^3y^5+h'(y).
$$

Comparison with the coefficient of $dy$ gives $h'(y)=0$, so $h$ is a
constant. That constant can be chosen so that $F(5,4)=20$.

For the graph $z=F(x,y)$, at $(x,y,F(x,y))$ changing $x$ while holding $y$
fixed gives the tangent direction $(1,0,F_x(x,y))$; changing $y$ while
holding $x$ fixed gives $(0,1,F_y(x,y))$. A normal vector must be
perpendicular to both. The vector $(-F_x(x,y),-F_y(x,y),1)$ has zero dot
product with each direction, since
$-F_x+F_x=0$ and $-F_y+F_y=0$. Thus one normal vector is

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
-F_x(x,y)\\
-F_y(x,y)\\
1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At $(5,4)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_x(5,4)
&=6(5)^2(4)^6
=150\cdot4096
=614400,\\
F_y(5,4)
&=12(5)^3(4)^5
=1500\cdot1024
=1536000.
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently, one normal vector at $(5,4,20)$ is

$$
{
\mathbf n=
\begin{pmatrix}
-614400\\
-1536000\\
1
\end{pmatrix}.
}
$$

An exact differential form gives a level curve of a potential. The graph of
the same potential is a surface in three dimensions and its partial
derivatives supply the tangent-plane and normal information. The linear
approximation is the tangent plane, while the original solution curve is obtained by intersecting that
surface with the horizontal plane $z=c$.

### First- and second-order price models

Suppose a price $B(t)$ is modelled by

$$
\frac{dB}{dt}=-a(t)\bigl(B(t)-G(t)\bigr)+P(t). \tag{1}
$$

where $G(t)$ is a target price, $a(t)$ is a relaxation rate and $P(t)$ is an
additional price-change rate. These are prescribed functions of $t$, not of
$B$. Their particular values are not specified in part (a).

#### (a)

Which classes must Equation (1) belong to? Select all that apply:

- linear;
- non-linear;
- homogeneous;
- inhomogeneous;
- an exact differential equation;
- a separable differential equation.

#### (b)

At the launch of the coin, measurements gave

$$
a(t)=t^9,
\qquad
G(t)=2\sqrt[5]{2t^4+4t+5},
\qquad
P(t)=3\cos(t).
$$

Rewrite the equation as

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\phi(t)B(t)\bigr)
=\phi(t)\bigl(a(t)G(t)+P(t)\bigr),
$$

so that

$$
B(t)=\frac1{\phi(t)}
\left(\int\phi(t)\bigl(a(t)G(t)+P(t)\bigr)\,dt+C\right).
$$

Find $\phi(t)$.

#### (c)

A second illustrative price $N(t)$ satisfies

$$
7N''(t)=-70N'(t)-175N(t)+8e^{-5t}.
$$

##### (i)

To find the homogeneous equation, solve the associated characteristic equation,
written in terms of a variable $\nu$:

$$
0=\ ?
$$

##### (ii)

Find a particular solution $N_p$ that contains no undetermined coefficients.

#### Solution

Expand the right-hand side of (1) and collect the terms involving $B$:

$$
\frac{dB}{dt}+a(t)B(t)=a(t)G(t)+P(t).
$$

This is linear in the unknown function $B$. It is not necessarily
inhomogeneous: $aG+P$ could vanish identically (for example, $a=G=1$ and
$P=-1$). Nor must it be separable, although some choices of the prescribed
functions make it so. In differential form,

$$
\bigl(a(t)B-a(t)G(t)-P(t)\bigr)\,dt+dB=0,
$$

the partial derivative of the $dt$ coefficient with respect to $B$ is
$a(t)$, whereas the partial derivative of the $dB$ coefficient with respect
to $t$ is zero. Exactness is therefore not guaranteed either. The only
classification that must hold in part (a) is

$$
{\text{linear}.}
$$

For part (b), the coefficient of $B$ in standard linear form is
$p(t)=a(t)=t^9$. The integrating factor is

$$
\phi(t)=e^{\int t^9\,dt}
=e^{t^{10}/10}.
$$

Since $\phi'(t)=t^9\phi(t)$, multiplying the standard equation by $\phi$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi B'+t^9\phi B
&=\phi B'+\phi'B\\
&=\frac{d}{dt}(\phi B)\\
&=\phi\bigl(t^9G(t)+3\cos t\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus,

$$
{\phi(t)=\exp(t^{10}/10).}
$$


The general solution includes an integration constant:

$$
B(t)
=e^{-t^{10}/10}
\left(
\int e^{t^{10}/10}
\bigl(t^9G(t)+3\cos t\bigr)\,dt+C
\right).
$$

For part (c), first move all terms involving $N$ to the left:

$$
7N''+70N'+175N=8e^{-5t}.
$$

The associated homogeneous equation is

$$
7N''+70N'+175N=0.
$$

Using the trial solution $N=e^{\nu t}$ gives

$$
7\nu^2e^{\nu t}+70\nu e^{\nu t}+175e^{\nu t}=0.
$$

Since $e^{\nu t}\ne0$, the characteristic equation is

$$
{0=7\nu^2+70\nu+175
=7(\nu+5)^2.}
$$

The repeated root is $\nu=-5$, so the homogeneous solution is

$$
N_h=(C_1+C_2t)e^{-5t}.
$$

The forcing term $8e^{-5t}$ has the same exponential as the repeated
characteristic root. Both the usual trial $Ce^{-5t}$ and the once-corrected
trial $Cte^{-5t}$ belong to the homogeneous solution space, so use

$$
N_p=At^2e^{-5t}.
$$

Write the differential operator as

$$
7D^2+70D+175=7(D+5)^2,
\qquad D=\frac{d}{dt}.
$$

For $N_p=At^2e^{-5t}$,

$$
(D+5)\bigl(At^2e^{-5t}\bigr)=2At\,e^{-5t},
$$

and hence

$$
(D+5)^2\bigl(At^2e^{-5t}\bigr)
=2A e^{-5t}.
$$

Substitution into the left-hand side gives

$$
7(D+5)^2N_p=14A e^{-5t}.
$$

Matching this with $8e^{-5t}$ gives

$$
14A=8
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
A=\frac47.
$$

Therefore

$$
{N_p(t)=\frac47t^2e^{-5t}.}
$$


The complete general solution is

$$
{
N(t)=\left(C_1+C_2t+\frac47t^2\right)e^{-5t}.
}
$$

The first model is a first-order linear equation: the integrating factor
creates the product rule. The second uses the constant-coefficient method for
a second-order equation. Its repeated characteristic root explains the factors
$1,t$ in the homogeneous solution and the extra factor $t^2$ in the
particular trial.

</Example>
