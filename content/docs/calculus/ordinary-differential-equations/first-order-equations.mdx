This page solves first-order equations by recognising a structure that can be
integrated directly: separable, linear or exact. Substitutions handle
equations that reduce to one of these three.

The three tests are independent rather than mutually exclusive, so one
equation can pass more than one. The
[method-selection workflow](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/pipeline)
gives the order in which to try them and the equations produced in
[modelling with first-order equations](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/first-order-modelling)
need the same check.

## Separable equations

<Definition slug="separable-equation">

### Separable equation

<Statement>

A first-order ODE is separable when it can be written as

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x)g(y).
$$

</Statement>

On an interval where $g(y)\neq0$, the variables can be placed on opposite sides. Zeros of $g$ must be checked before division because they may give constant solutions that the separated calculation does not contain.

</Definition>

<Method slug="separation-of-variables" of="separable-equation">

### Separation of variables

<Statement>

To solve $\frac{dy}{dx}=f(x)g(y)$ on a branch where $g(y)\neq0$:

1. Write $\frac{dy}{g(y)}=f(x)\,dx$.
2. Integrate both sides:
   $$
   \int\frac{dy}{g(y)}=\int f(x)\,dx+C.
   $$
3. Apply initial or boundary data and state the interval or branch on which the result is valid.
4. Check separately any constant solutions arising from $g(y)=0$.

</Statement>

The result is often implicit. Taking logarithms can hide a sign choice and the initial data select the branch, subject to the hypotheses in [existence, uniqueness and linear operators](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/existence-uniqueness-and-linear-operators).

</Method>

<Example slug="separable-exponential" of="separation-of-variables" difficulty="routine">

### Exponential growth with an initial value

For

$$
y'=y(x+3x^2),\qquad y(0)=1,
$$

the initial value is nonzero, so separate on the branch $y\neq0$:

$$
\frac{dy}{y}=(x+3x^2)\,dx.
$$

Therefore

$$
\ln|y|=\frac{x^2}{2}+x^3+C.
$$

Since $y(0)=1>0$, the positive branch is selected and the constant is zero:

$$
y=e^{x^2/2+x^3}.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="separable-square-root" of="separation-of-variables" difficulty="exam">

### A lost equilibrium and a real-domain restriction

For

$$
y'=x\sqrt{1-y^2},
$$

the nonconstant solutions on a real branch satisfy

$$
\frac{dy}{\sqrt{1-y^2}}=x\,dx.
$$

Integration gives

$$
\arcsin y=\frac{x^2}{2}+C.
$$

The square root requires $|y|\leq1$. Because $\arcsin y$ takes values in $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$, the implicit relation describes a branch only while $x^2/2+C$ stays in that range.

The constant solutions $y=1$ and $y=-1$ were lost on division. At a point where a nonconstant branch reaches either level, it can also join a constant branch: the right side is zero there and uniqueness is not guaranteed at $|y|=1$.

</Example>

<Example slug="separable-circles" of="separation-of-variables" difficulty="routine">

### A family of circles

For

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{x}{y},
$$

multiply by $y\,dx$ and integrate:

$$
y\,dy=-x\,dx
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{y^2}{2}=C.
$$

After absorbing the factor of $2$ into the constant,

$$
x^2+y^2=C.
$$

The solution curves are circles, but the original differential equation is
undefined when $y=0$. Thus a circle is represented by an upper or lower
graph branch on an interval that does not cross its points on the $x$-axis.
The initial points $(0,7)$, $(2,3)$ and $(-6,-8)$ select respectively

$$
x^2+y^2=49,\qquad
x^2+y^2=13,\qquad
x^2+y^2=100.
$$

</Example>

## First-order linear equations

When the variables cannot be separated, test next whether the equation is linear in $y$. Mixing and cooling laws in [modelling with first-order equations](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/first-order-modelling) lead to this case.

<Definition slug="first-order-linear-equation">

### First-order linear equation

<Statement>

A first-order linear ODE in normal form is

$$
y'+p(x)y=q(x).
$$

</Statement>

The term $p(x)y$ couples $y$ to $y'$, so direct integration of $y'$ and $q(x)$ separately does not solve the equation. An integrating factor makes the left side a product-rule derivative.

</Definition>

<Method slug="integrating-factor" of="first-order-linear-equation">

### Integrating factor

<Statement>

For $y'+p(x)y=q(x)$ on an interval where $p$ and $q$ are continuous:

1. Choose
   $$
   \mu(x)=e^{\int p(x)\,dx}.
   $$
2. Since $\mu'=p\mu$, multiply the equation by $\mu$:
   $$
   \mu y'+\mu p y=(\mu y)'=\mu q.
   $$
3. Integrate:
   $$
   \mu y=\int\mu q\,dx+C.
   $$
4. Divide by $\mu$:
   $$
   y=\frac1{\mu}\left(\int\mu q\,dx+C\right).
   $$

</Statement>

The integrating factor is not a new solution of the ODE; it is chosen to produce the product rule. If the coefficient of $y'$ is not $1$, first divide by it on an interval where it is nonzero.

</Method>

<Example slug="linear-preweighted" of="integrating-factor" difficulty="routine">

### An equation already in product-rule form

For

$$
e^{x^2}y'+2xe^{x^2}y=x,
$$

the left side is already $(e^{x^2}y)'$. Hence

$$
(e^{x^2}y)'=x
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
e^{x^2}y=\frac{x^2}{2}+C,
$$

and

$$
y=e^{-x^2}\left(\frac{x^2}{2}+C\right).
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="linear-standard" of="integrating-factor" difficulty="routine">

### A standard first-order linear equation

For

$$
y'+3y=e^{-x},
$$

the integrating factor is $\mu=e^{3x}$. Multiplication gives

$$
(e^{3x}y)'=e^{2x}.
$$

Therefore

$$
e^{3x}y=\frac12e^{2x}+C,
$$

so

$$
y=\frac12e^{-x}+Ce^{-3x}.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="linear-singular-coefficient" of="integrating-factor" difficulty="exam">

### A singular normal form with a smooth continuation

For

$$
xy'+(x+1)y=2,
$$

divide by $x$ only on $x>0$ or $x<0$:

$$
y'+\left(1+\frac1x\right)y=\frac2x.
$$

On either interval, an integrating factor is

$$
\mu(x)=e^{x+\ln|x|}.
$$

Multiplying by a nonzero constant does not change an integrating factor, so we may take $\mu=xe^x$ on each chosen interval. Then

$$
(xe^xy)'=2e^x,
\qquad
xe^xy=2e^x+C.
$$

Thus

$$
y=\frac2x+\frac{Ce^{-x}}{x}.
$$

The normalised equation is valid only for $x\ne0$, but the original equation
at $x=0$ still imposes $y(0)=2$. Choosing
$C=-2$ gives

$$
y=\frac{2(1-e^{-x})}{x},
\qquad y(0)=2
$$

by continuous extension. Other values of $C$ do not extend continuously
through $x=0$, so the interval restriction created by division must still be
reported.

</Example>

## Exact equations

When the equation is neither separable nor linear, test whether $M\,dx+N\,dy=0$ is a total differential. If the test fails, try a substitution or a [numerical method](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/numerical-solutions).

<Definition slug="exact-differential-equation">

### Exact differential equation

<Statement>

An equation in differential form

$$
M(x,y)\,dx+N(x,y)\,dy=0
$$

is exact on a domain when there is a potential function $F(x,y)$ such that

$$
F_x=M,\qquad F_y=N.
$$

Here $F_x$ means differentiate with respect to $x$ while holding $y$ fixed,
and $F_y$ means differentiate with respect to $y$ while holding $x$ fixed.
The later [multivariable-calculus chapter](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/geometry-and-approximation)
develops this notation and the level-curve geometry in more detail.

</Statement>

Along a curve $y=y(x)$, the differential equation becomes

$$
M(x,y)+N(x,y)y'=0.
$$

A potential turns this into the statement that $F$ is constant along the curve, as shown after the theorem below.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="exact-potential" of="exact-differential-equation">

### The exactness test and potential

<Statement>

Suppose $M$ and $N$ have continuous first partial derivatives on an open simply connected domain $D$. If

$$
M_y=\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}
=N_x=\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}
$$

throughout $D$, then the equation $M\,dx+N\,dy=0$ has a potential $F$ on $D$ and its solution curves satisfy

$$
F(x,y)=C.
$$

</Statement>

Matching partial derivatives guarantees a single-valued potential on a simply
connected domain, while holes in the domain can obstruct a global potential.

</Theorem>

One direction is short. If a potential $F$ exists and $M,N$ have continuous
first partial derivatives, then equality of mixed partials gives

$$
M_y=F_{xy}=F_{yx}=N_x,
$$

so $M_y=N_x$ is necessary. The converse is the substantive part of the
theorem: the simply connected hypothesis rules out holes that can prevent
locally compatible derivatives from coming from one global potential. The
course uses the theorem as a test rather than developing the line-integral
theory behind that global step.

In practice, the construction below produces a candidate $F$ directly and
checking $F_x=M$ and $F_y=N$ verifies it without that further theory. Once a
potential is known, along a solution curve the chain rule gives

$$
\frac{d}{dx}F(x,y(x))
=F_x+F_y y'
=M+Ny'
=0.
$$

Therefore $F$ is constant along each solution curve, so the curves are level
curves $F(x,y)=C$. This explains why a potential solves the ODE, not why one
exists.

Level curves connect exact equations to gradients. In two variables, the
gradient is the vector of partial derivatives, $\nabla F=(F_x,F_y)$. At a
regular point of a level curve $F=C$ (where this vector is nonzero), it is
normal to the curve. Since $(F_x,F_y)=(M,N)$, the equation $M+Ny'=0$ says that
the graph's tangent direction $(1,y')$ is perpendicular to the gradient. An
exact equation is therefore a level-curve problem whose slope field is
orthogonal to the gradient. Points where $\nabla F=0$ need separate attention.
The [multivariable-calculus chapter](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/geometry-and-approximation)
develops this geometry further.

<Method slug="solve-exact-equation" of="exact-potential">

### Constructing the potential

<Statement>

To solve an exact equation:

1. Check that $M_y=N_x$ on the domain.
2. Integrate $M$ with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as constant:
   $$
   F(x,y)=\int M(x,y)\,dx+h(y).
   $$
3. Differentiate this expression with respect to $y$.
4. Match $F_y$ with $N$ to determine $h(y)$.
5. Write the implicit solution as $F(x,y)=C$.

</Statement>

The arbitrary function $h(y)$ is needed because integration with respect to $x$ leaves every function of $y$ unchanged. Omitting it can make the final expression fail to reproduce $N$.

</Method>

<Example slug="exact-linear-coefficients" of="solve-exact-equation" difficulty="routine">

### A polynomial potential

For

$$
(2x+y+3)\,dx+(2y+x-7)\,dy=0,
$$

we have

$$
M_y=1=N_x,
$$

so the equation is exact. Integrate $M$ with respect to $x$:

$$
F=x^2+xy+3x+h(y).
$$

Then

$$
F_y=x+h'(y)=x+2y-7,
$$

which gives $h'(y)=2y-7$ and hence $h(y)=y^2-7y$. The implicit general solution is

$$
x^2+xy+3x+y^2-7y=C.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="exact-hyperbolic" of="solve-exact-equation" difficulty="exam">

### A potential involving hyperbolic functions

Rewrite

$$
(x^2y+y^3)y'+xy^2+\sinh(2x)=0
$$

as

$$
\bigl(xy^2+\sinh(2x)\bigr)\,dx
+\bigl(x^2y+y^3\bigr)\,dy=0.
$$

Here

$$
M_y=2xy=N_x.
$$

Integrating $M$ with respect to $x$ gives

$$
F=\frac12x^2y^2+\frac12\cosh(2x)+h(y).
$$

Matching

$$
F_y=x^2y+h'(y)=x^2y+y^3
$$

gives $h(y)=y^4/4$. Therefore

$$
\frac12x^2y^2+\frac12\cosh(2x)+\frac14y^4=C.
$$

</Example>

If the partial derivatives do not match, the equation is not exact in its current form. It may still be solvable by separation, a substitution or an integrating factor, but the exact method needs the equation to be changed first.

## Homogeneous substitutions

When none of these structures is visible, a change of variables may reveal one. A degree-zero ratio $y/x$ calls for $y=xv$ and a power $y^n$ calls for the Bernoulli substitution below. Either way, the substitution turns the equation into an earlier case rather than starting a new family.

<Definition slug="homogeneous-function">

### Homogeneous function

<Statement>

A function $F(x,y)$ is homogeneous of degree $k$ when

$$
F(\lambda x,\lambda y)=\lambda^kF(x,y)
$$

for nonzero scalars $\lambda$ wherever both sides are defined.

</Statement>

When the right side of $y'=F(x,y)$ is homogeneous of degree $0$, scaling $(x,y)$ leaves it unchanged. On a suitable domain, it can therefore be expressed as a function of $y/x$, which suggests the substitution $y=xv$.

</Definition>

<Method slug="homogeneous-substitution" of="homogeneous-function">

### The substitution $y=xv$

<Statement>

For an equation of the form

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=f\left(\frac{y}{x}\right),
$$

set $y=xv(x)$. Then

$$
y'=v+xv',
$$

so

$$
xv'=f(v)-v,
\qquad
\frac{dv}{dx}=\frac{f(v)-v}{x}.
$$

The resulting equation is separable on an interval where $x\neq0$ and the required denominators are nonzero.

</Statement>

The substitution works because $y/x$ becomes $v$. The point $x=0$ is excluded from the interval used for the substitution unless the original equation is analysed separately there.

</Method>

<Example slug="homogeneous-ode-example" of="homogeneous-substitution" difficulty="exam">

### A degree-zero right side

For

$$
2xy\,y'+x^2+y^2=0,
$$

solve for the derivative:

$$
y'=-\frac{x^2+y^2}{2xy}.
$$

The right side is homogeneous of degree $0$. Work on an interval where
$x\ne0$ and $y\ne0$, so the displayed normal form and the substitution
$v=y/x$ are valid. Put $y=xv$, so $y'=v+xv'$. Substitution gives

$$
2x(xv)(v+xv')+x^2+x^2v^2=0,
$$

and, after division by $x^2$,

$$
2xvv'+1+3v^2=0.
$$

Separate and integrate:

$$
\frac{2v}{1+3v^2}\,dv=-\frac{dx}{x}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac13\ln(1+3v^2)=-\ln|x|+C.
$$

Exponentiating gives $(1+3v^2)|x|^3=C_1$. The sign of $x$ is fixed on the chosen interval, so that sign can be absorbed into the constant. Substituting $v=y/x$ gives the equivalent implicit solution

$$
x^3+3xy^2=C
$$

on a chosen interval with $x\neq0$ and $y\neq0$. Differentiating this relation gives

$$
3x^2+3y^2+6xy\,y'=0,
$$

which is equivalent to the original equation.

</Example>

## Bernoulli equations

The substitution $y=xv$ uses scaling in both variables. A Bernoulli substitution instead changes the dependent variable so that a nonlinear power becomes a first-order linear equation.

<Definition slug="bernoulli-equation">

### Bernoulli equation

<Statement>

A Bernoulli equation has the form

$$
y'+p(x)y=q(x)y^n,
\qquad n\neq0,1.
$$

</Statement>

The cases $n=0$ and $n=1$ are already first-order linear equations. For
$n\neq0,1$, the nonlinear power can be converted into a new dependent
variable on a real branch where the required powers of $y$ are defined.

</Definition>

<Method slug="bernoulli-substitution" of="bernoulli-equation,integrating-factor">

### The Bernoulli substitution

<Statement>

For a Bernoulli equation, on a nonzero real branch where $y^{1-n}$ is
defined, set

$$
v=y^{1-n}.
$$

Since

$$
v'=(1-n)y^{-n}y',
$$

multiplying the equation by $(1-n)y^{-n}$ gives the linear equation

$$
v'+(1-n)p(x)v=(1-n)q(x).
$$

Solve this equation with an integrating factor, then substitute back. Check
$y=0$ separately because the substitution divides by a power of $y$; it is
an admissible solution only when the original equation is defined there and
both sides agree.

</Statement>

</Method>

<Example slug="bernoulli-example" of="bernoulli-substitution" difficulty="exam">

### A Bernoulli equation with a zero solution

For

$$
2xy'-y=10x^3y^5,
$$

use $v=y^{-4}$ on a nonzero branch. Since $v'=-4y^{-5}y'$, multiplying by $y^{-5}$ and rearranging gives

$$
v'+\frac2xv=-20x^2.
$$

The integrating factor is $x^2$, so

$$
(x^2v)'=-20x^4
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x^2v=-4x^5+C.
$$

Thus

$$
y^{-4}=v=-4x^3+\frac{C}{x^2},
$$

or, on intervals where the right side gives a real nonzero solution,

$$
y=\pm\left(-4x^3+\frac{C}{x^2}\right)^{-1/4}.
$$

These real branches require $x\neq0$ and $-4x^3+C/x^2>0$. The solution $y=0$ is also present in the original equation but is not represented by $y^{-4}$.

</Example>
