This page approximates solutions when no closed-form method applies, the last step of the [method-selection workflow](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/pipeline). The methods in [first-order methods](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/first-order-equations) and [second-order constant-coefficient equations](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/second-order-constant-coefficient-equations) can fail even for an equation that is easy to state and sometimes the derivative is known only from a calculation or a measurement.

A direction field gives a qualitative picture. Euler's and Heun's methods turn local slope information into a sequence of approximations.

## Direction fields

<Definition slug="direction-field">

### Direction field

<Statement>

For

$$
y'=f(x,y),
$$

the direction field assigns a short line segment of slope $f(x,y)$ to each point $(x,y)$ on a grid. A solution curve follows these local directions and an initial condition selects the curve passing through the prescribed point.

</Statement>

The field gives qualitative information before any numerical calculation. It can show increasing and decreasing regions, equilibrium levels and the way nearby solution curves behave.

</Definition>

<Example slug="direction-field-example" of="direction-field" difficulty="routine">

### A family of translated curves

For

$$
y'=x^2,
$$
every point with the same $x$-coordinate has the same slope. Integrating gives

$$
y=\frac{x^3}{3}+C.
$$

The curves through $y(0)=1$ and $y(0)=3$ are vertical translates of one another. The direction field displays this common slope pattern even though the initial condition chooses only one curve.

</Example>

A direction field is most useful when no formula is available. No general elementary procedure in [first-order methods](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/first-order-equations) applies to $y'=\sin(xy)$, for instance, although $y\equiv0$ is a particular solution.

## Euler's method

<Method slug="euler-method">

### Euler's method

<Statement>

For the IVP

$$
y'=f(x,y),\qquad y(x_0)=y_0,
$$

choose a step size $h$ and define

$$
x_{k+1}=x_k+h,
\qquad
y_{k+1}=y_k+h f(x_k,y_k).
$$

The update uses the tangent line at the left endpoint of each step.

</Statement>

The formula uses the current slope over one short step: the tangent line
through $(x_k,y_k)$ has value $y_k+h f(x_k,y_k)$ at $x_k+h$. This is the
degree-one [Taylor model](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-polynomials) at the
current point and [Taylor's theorem](/calculus/taylor-series/taylors-theorem)
describes the remainder omitted by one such step.

For an exact starting value $y_k=y(x_k)$, the fundamental theorem of calculus
gives

$$
y(x_k+h)=y_k+\int_{x_k}^{x_k+h}y'(s)\,ds.
$$

Euler replaces the changing slope inside this integral by $y'(x_k)$. If
$|y''|\le M$ on the step, then
$|y'(s)-y'(x_k)|\le M|s-x_k|$, so this *local* error is at most
$Mh^2/2$.

In an actual computation $y_k$ is already approximate, so errors also
propagate from previous steps. If the slope field changes at most
proportionally with $y$ (the [Lipschitz condition](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/existence-uniqueness-and-linear-operators))
and the solution is smooth on a fixed interval, standard stability estimates
bound the largest accumulated error by a constant times $h$ as $h$ tends to
zero. Thus, for a sufficiently small step size and a fixed interval, halving
$h$ roughly halves Euler's global error. This numerical stability concerns
error propagation in the method, not whether solutions of the ODE grow or
decay.

</Method>

<Example slug="euler-large-step" of="euler-method" difficulty="routine">

### A visibly large step

For

$$
y'=x^2,\qquad y(0)=1,\qquad h=1,
$$

the first point is $(x_0,y_0)=(0,1)$. Since $f(0,1)=0$, the next point is $(1,1)$. The following slopes are $f(1,1)=1$ and $f(2,2)=4$, giving

$$
(0,1)\longrightarrow(1,1)\longrightarrow(2,2)\longrightarrow(3,6).
$$

The exact solution is $y=1+x^3/3$, whose value at $x=3$ is $10$. The step size is too large to track the curvature accurately.

</Example>

<Example slug="euler-small-step" of="euler-method" difficulty="routine">

### A smaller step

For

$$
y'=x+y,\qquad y(0)=1,\qquad h=0.1,
$$

Euler's update gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_1&=1+0.1(0+1)=1.1,\\
y_2&=1.1+0.1(0.1+1.1)=1.22,\\
y_3&=1.22+0.1(0.2+1.22)=1.362.
\end{aligned}
$$

The exact solution is

$$
y=2e^x-1-x,
$$

whose value at $x=0.3$ is approximately $1.3997$. The Euler value $1.362$ is below it by about $0.0377$.

</Example>

## Improved Euler method

Euler drifts when the slope changes across a step. Heun's method corrects this by averaging the slopes at both ends of the step. It costs two evaluations of $f$ per step but attains higher order, so prefer it when $f$ is smooth and evaluations are cheap; keep Euler when a coarse qualitative trace of the direction field suffices.

<Method slug="heun-method" of="euler-method">

### Heun's method

<Statement>

Starting from $(x_k,y_k)$, first predict the next value with Euler:

$$
u_{k+1}=y_k+h f(x_k,y_k).
$$

Then correct using the average of the slope at the current point and the predicted endpoint:

$$
y_{k+1}
=y_k+\frac h2
\left[
f(x_k,y_k)+f(x_{k+1},u_{k+1})
\right].
$$

</Statement>

Where Euler uses only the left-endpoint slope, Heun's method applies the trapezoidal rule to

$$
y(x_{k+1})=y(x_k)+\int_{x_k}^{x_{k+1}}f(x,y(x))\,dx,
$$

but the endpoint value is unknown, so the Euler prediction supplies it. Using both slopes makes Heun more accurate at the same step size.

When $f$ and the exact solution are sufficiently smooth and the usual
Lipschitz and error-stability conditions hold on a fixed interval, Euler has
local error $O(h^2)$ and global error $O(h)$, whereas Heun has local error
$O(h^3)$ and global error $O(h^2)$. Thus halving $h$ roughly quarters Heun's
global error (until round-off or other errors dominate).

</Method>

<Example slug="heun-comparison" of="heun-method" difficulty="exam">

### Comparing Euler and Heun

Consider

$$
y'=\frac{1}{1+x^2}-2y^2,\qquad y(0)=0,
$$

whose exact solution is

$$
y(x)=\frac{x}{1+x^2}.
$$

With $h=0.2$, $f(0,0)=1$. Euler predicts $u_1=0.2$;
the predicted endpoint slope is
$f(0.2,0.2)=1/1.04-2(0.2)^2\approx0.88154$. Heun therefore gives
$y_1\approx0.2(1+0.88154)/2=0.18815$, closer to the exact value
$y(0.2)=0.2/1.04\approx0.19231$ than Euler's $0.2$.
Repeating the updates on $[0,1]$ gives these maximum grid-point errors:

$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
h & \text{Euler} & \text{Heun}\\
\hline
0.2 & 0.0545 & 0.0105\\
0.1 & 0.0259 & 0.0023
\end{array}
$$

Reducing $h$ lowers the error for both methods and Heun keeps the smaller error.

</Example>

To implement either method on $[a,b]$ with $N$ equal intervals, use

$$
h=\frac{b-a}{N},
\qquad
x_k=a+kh,
$$

and store the successive approximations $y_k$.

The methods also cover second-order problems, even though the formulas above
begin with $y'=f(x,y)$. For $y''=F(x,y,y')$, set $v=y'$: the pair $(y,v)$
satisfies $(y,v)'=(v,F(x,y,v))$ and each numerical update is applied to both
components. The same componentwise formulas apply to any first-order vector
system.
