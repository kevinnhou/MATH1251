This page puts [polar form](/algebra/complex-numbers/polar-form) to work in two ways. Powers of $e^{i\theta}$ are easy to compute, so they give identities for sines and cosines. In the [Argand plane](/algebra/complex-numbers/complex-arithmetic), conditions on modulus and argument describe regions.

## Exponential trigonometry

<Proposition slug="euler-trigonometric-identities" of="polar-form">

### Exponential forms of sine and cosine

<Statement>

For $\theta\in\mathbb R$,
$$
\cos\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}2,
\qquad
\sin\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}.
$$

</Statement>

The two identities follow by adding and subtracting Euler's formula and its conjugate form. They let powers of sine and cosine be expanded with complex arithmetic, where powers of $e^{i\theta}$ combine easily:
$(e^{i\theta})^n=e^{in\theta}$.

</Proposition>

<Theorem slug="binomial-theorem" of="complex-number">

### Binomial theorem over the complex numbers

<Statement>

For $a,b\in\mathbb C$ and every non-negative integer $n$,
$$
(a+b)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n{n\choose k}a^{n-k}b^k,
\qquad
{n\choose k}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.
$$

</Statement>

The theorem holds over $\mathbb C$ because complex addition and multiplication obey the same commutative and distributive laws as real arithmetic. The coefficients can be read from Pascal's triangle.

</Theorem>

<Definition slug="trigonometric-polynomial">

### Trigonometric polynomial

<Statement>

A *trigonometric polynomial* is an expression of the form
$$
a_0+\sum_{k=1}^N\bigl(a_k\cos(k\theta)+b_k\sin(k\theta)\bigr),
\qquad \theta\in\mathbb R,
$$
where the coefficients $a_k,b_k$ are complex numbers.

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Recall of="de-moivre" />

<Method slug="trigonometric-power-expansion" of="euler-trigonometric-identities,binomial-theorem">

### Expand powers of sine and cosine

<Statement>

Replace $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ by their exponential forms, expand with the binomial theorem and collect conjugate exponential terms back into sines and cosines.

</Statement>

The result is a trigonometric polynomial. The reverse calculation starts with De Moivre's formula and compares real or imaginary parts.

</Method>

<Example slug="sine-cube-expansion" of="trigonometric-power-expansion" difficulty="routine">

### Expand $\sin^3\theta$

Using the exponential form,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin^3\theta
&=\left(\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}\right)^3\\
&=\frac{e^{3i\theta}-3e^{i\theta}+3e^{-i\theta}-e^{-3i\theta}}{(2i)^3}\\
&=\frac{3\sin\theta-\sin(3\theta)}4.
\end{aligned}
$$

For the last step, group opposite exponents and use
$e^{ik\theta}-e^{-ik\theta}=2i\sin(k\theta)$, together with
$(2i)^3=-8i$. This step turns the exponential calculation back into a real
trigonometric identity.

For the reverse direction, the imaginary part of
$$
(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^3
$$
gives
$$
\sin(3\theta)=3\cos^2\theta\sin\theta-\sin^3\theta.
$$
Using $\cos^2\theta=1-\sin^2\theta$ recovers the same cubic identity.

Either way, the cube of one oscillation becomes a combination of
oscillations at frequencies $1$ and $3$.

</Example>

## Loci in the Argand plane

A *locus* is the set of points whose coordinates satisfy a stated geometric
condition. In the Argand plane, modulus conditions describe distances and
argument conditions describe directions.

<Method slug="complex-locus" of="argand-plane,complex-modulus,complex-argument">

### Read loci in the Argand plane

<Statement>

Translate a condition on $z$ into a geometric condition. For $a\in\mathbb C$ and
$b>0$,
$$
|z-a|\le b
$$
is the closed disc with centre $a$ and radius $b$. For $z\ne a$ and
$-\pi<\alpha<\beta\le\pi$,
$$
\alpha\le\operatorname{Arg}(z-a)<\beta
$$
is the angular region between the rays from $a$ at arguments $\alpha$ and
$\beta$, including the $\alpha$ boundary and excluding the $\beta$ boundary.

</Statement>

The modulus measures Euclidean distance between points because
$$
|z-w|
=\sqrt{\bigl(\operatorname{Re}(z)-\operatorname{Re}(w)\bigr)^2
       +\bigl(\operatorname{Im}(z)-\operatorname{Im}(w)\bigr)^2}.
$$
When $z\ne w$, the argument of $z-w$ is the directed angle made by the
line from $w$ to $z$ with the positive real axis. At $z=w$, the difference
is zero and has no argument.

</Method>

<Example slug="complex-locus-examples" of="complex-locus" difficulty="routine">

### Interpret a combined locus

Describe
$$
S=\left\{z\in\mathbb C:|z-(1+i)|\le2,\quad
0\le\operatorname{Arg}(z-(1+i))<\frac\pi2\right\}.
$$
Writing $w=z-(1+i)$ moves the centre to the origin. The modulus condition
restricts $w$ to the closed disc of radius $2$; the argument condition keeps
only directions from the positive real axis up to, but not including, the
positive imaginary axis. Thus $S$ is the quarter-disc of radius $2$ lying
above and to the right of $1+i$, with its boundary only partly included:

- the horizontal radial edge is included, except for the centre;
- the circular arc is included, except for its upper endpoint;
- the vertical radial edge is excluded.

The centre is omitted because $\operatorname{Arg}(0)$ is undefined, a detail
the disc condition alone would miss.

</Example>
