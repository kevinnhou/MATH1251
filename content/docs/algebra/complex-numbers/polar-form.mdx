This page describes a complex number by its distance from the origin and its direction, instead of by its [Cartesian](/algebra/complex-numbers/complex-arithmetic) components. In these polar coordinates on the [Argand plane](/algebra/complex-numbers/complex-arithmetic), multiplication multiplies moduli and adds arguments, which makes powers and roots straightforward.

For $z=x+iy\ne0$ with polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$,
$$
r=|z|,\qquad x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta.
$$

## Argument and polar form

<Recall of="complex-number" />
<Recall of="complex-modulus" />

<Definition slug="complex-argument" of="complex-number,complex-modulus">

### Argument

<Statement>

For $z\ne0$, an *argument* of $z$ is a real number $\theta$ satisfying
$$
\cos\theta=\frac{\operatorname{Re}(z)}{|z|},
\qquad
\sin\theta=\frac{\operatorname{Im}(z)}{|z|}.
$$
Arguments are determined up to addition of $2\pi k$, where $k\in\mathbb Z$. The *principal argument* $\operatorname{Arg}(z)$ is the unique argument in
$$
-\pi<\operatorname{Arg}(z)\le\pi.
$$

</Statement>

Choose the argument from the point's quadrant, not from $\tan\theta$ alone:
tangent has period $\pi$, so it cannot distinguish opposite quadrants.

The principal argument is one conventional choice; every argument remains
valid. The number $z=0$ has no argument, because the origin does not
determine a direction.

</Definition>

<Definition slug="polar-form" of="complex-number,complex-modulus,complex-argument">

### Polar form

<Statement>

For $z\ne0$, the *polar form* of $z$ is
$$
z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=re^{i\theta},
\qquad r=|z|>0,
$$
where $\theta$ is an argument of $z$. The expressions $a+bi$ and $re^{i\theta}$ are two writings of the same complex number.

</Statement>

Euler's formula,
$$
e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta,
$$
identifies the two expressions in the statement. Replacing $\theta$ by $\theta+2\pi k$ leaves $z$ unchanged.

Euler's formula is taken as given here rather than derived. The
[Taylor series](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-series) of the exponential,
sine and cosine are the usual route to a proof.

Multiplication by $e^{i\theta}$ rotates a point through angle $\theta$
without changing its modulus; multiplication by $re^{i\theta}$ also scales it
by $r$. In coordinates, for $z=x+iy$,
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta}z)\\
\operatorname{Im}(e^{i\theta}z)
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}
$$

</Definition>

<Example slug="polar-form-conversion" of="polar-form" difficulty="routine">

### Convert between the two forms

For $z=1+i$,
$$
|z|=\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2.
$$
The point lies in the first quadrant and $\tan\theta=1$, so $\theta=\pi/4$ is a principal argument. Hence
$$
1+i=\sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}.
$$

Conversely,
$$
\begin{aligned}
2e^{-3\pi i/4}
&=2\left(\cos\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)
+i\sin\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)\right)\\
&=-\sqrt2-\sqrt2\,i.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Example>

<Theorem slug="modulus-laws" of="complex-modulus">

### Modulus laws

<Statement>

For $z,z_1,z_2\in\mathbb C$ with $z_2\ne0$,
$$
z\overline z=|z|^2,\qquad
|z_1z_2|=|z_1||z_2|,\qquad
\left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right|=\frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|},
$$
$$
|\operatorname{Re}(z)|\le|z|,
\qquad
|\operatorname{Im}(z)|\le|z|,
\qquad
|z_1+z_2|\le|z_1|+|z_2|,
$$
$$
|z_1-z_2|\ge\bigl||z_1|-|z_2|\bigr|,
\qquad
\left|\sum_{j=1}^N z_j\right|
\le\sum_{j=1}^N|z_j|\quad(N\ge1).
$$

</Statement>

The inequality for $z_1+z_2$ is the triangle inequality in the Argand plane;
the finite-sum version follows by applying it repeatedly. The reverse triangle
inequality follows from
$$
|z_1|=|(z_1-z_2)+z_2|\le |z_1-z_2|+|z_2|
$$
and the same argument with $z_1$ and $z_2$ interchanged.

</Theorem>

## Multiplication and division in polar form

<Method slug="polar-arithmetic" of="polar-form">

### Multiply and divide in polar form

<Statement>

If $z_1=r_1e^{i\theta_1}$ and $z_2=r_2e^{i\theta_2}$, then
$$
z_1z_2=r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}
$$
and, for $z_2\ne0$,
$$
\frac{z_1}{z_2}
=\frac{r_1}{r_2}e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)}.
$$
In particular,
$$
(re^{i\theta})^{-1}=\frac1r e^{-i\theta}.
$$

</Statement>

Geometrically, a product multiplies the moduli and adds the arguments and a quotient divides the moduli and reverses the second rotation.

To see why the angles add, multiply
$(\cos\theta_1+i\sin\theta_1)(\cos\theta_2+i\sin\theta_2)$ and use the
angle-addition identities: its real and imaginary parts become
$\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$ and $\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$. The quotient rule
then follows by multiplying by the inverse.

</Method>

<Example slug="polar-product-and-quotient" of="polar-arithmetic" difficulty="routine">

### Perform product and quotient calculations

Let
$$
z_1=\sqrt2\,e^{3\pi i/2},
\qquad
z_2=\pi e^{3\pi i/2}.
$$
Both factors point down the imaginary axis. Their arguments add to $3\pi$,
so the product points along the negative real axis:
$$
z_1z_2=\pi\sqrt2\,e^{3\pi i}= -\pi\sqrt2.
$$

If instead $z_2=\pi e^{3\pi i/4}$, then
$$
\frac{z_1}{z_2}
=\frac{\sqrt2}{\pi}e^{i(3\pi/2-3\pi/4)}
=\frac{\sqrt2}{\pi}e^{3\pi i/4}.
$$

</Example>

<Proposition slug="principal-argument-laws" of="complex-argument,polar-arithmetic">

### Principal arguments of products and quotients

<Statement>

For non-zero $z_1,z_2\in\mathbb C$, there are integers $k,\ell\in\mathbb Z$
such that

$$
\operatorname{Arg}(z_1z_2)
=\operatorname{Arg}(z_1)+\operatorname{Arg}(z_2)+2\pi k,
$$
$$
\operatorname{Arg}\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)
=\operatorname{Arg}(z_1)-\operatorname{Arg}(z_2)+2\pi\ell.
$$

</Statement>

Adding or subtracting principal arguments gives an argument of the result,
but it may lie outside the principal interval $(-\pi,\pi]$. The integers $k$
and $\ell$ record the reduction back into that interval. For instance,
$\operatorname{Arg}((-1)(-1))=0$, whereas
$\operatorname{Arg}(-1)+\operatorname{Arg}(-1)=2\pi$; the latter is an
argument of the product, but not its principal argument.

Two polar forms describe the same non-zero number exactly when their moduli
agree and their arguments differ by an integer multiple of $2\pi$.

</Proposition>

## Powers through De Moivre's formula

<Theorem slug="de-moivre" of="polar-arithmetic">

### De Moivre's formula

<Statement>

For $\theta\in\mathbb R$ and $n\in\mathbb Z$,
$$
\left(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\right)^n
=\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta).
$$
More generally, if $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $r>0$, then
$$
z^n=r^ne^{in\theta}.
$$

</Statement>

For positive integers, use induction. The case $n=1$ is immediate and if the formula holds at $n=k$, multiplying by $re^{i\theta}$ gives
$$
z^{k+1}=r^ke^{ik\theta}re^{i\theta}=r^{k+1}e^{i(k+1)\theta}.
$$
The zero case is $z^0=1$ and the negative-integer case follows from the
multiplicative inverse. The condition $r>0$ is essential for negative powers,
because $0$ has no multiplicative inverse.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="polar-power-calculation" of="de-moivre" difficulty="routine">

### Calculate a power

For $z=\sqrt2\,e^{3\pi i/4}$,
$$
z^{104}
=(\sqrt2)^{104}e^{104(3\pi i/4)}
=2^{52}e^{78\pi i}
=2^{52}.
$$
Polar form handles the large exponent by treating the modulus and the argument separately.

</Example>

## Roots of complex numbers

<Definition slug="complex-nth-roots" of="polar-form">

### An nth root of a complex number

<Statement>

For a complex number $z_0$ and a positive integer $n$, an *$n$th root* is a
complex number $z$ satisfying
$$
z^n=z_0.
$$

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="complex-nth-root-formula" of="complex-nth-roots,polar-form">

### Find the nth roots

<Statement>

If $z_0\ne0$, $n$ is a positive integer and $z_0=r_0e^{i\theta_0}$, its
distinct $n$th roots are
$$
z_k=\sqrt[n]{r_0}\exp\left(i\frac{\theta_0+2\pi k}{n}\right),
\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1.
$$
They lie on a circle of radius $\sqrt[n]{r_0}$ centred at the origin and
are equally spaced by an angle of $2\pi/n$. A non-zero complex number has
exactly $n$ distinct $n$th roots.

</Statement>

To derive the formula, write $z=\rho e^{i\phi}$. If $z^n=z_0$, equality of moduli gives
$\rho^n=r_0$, while equality of arguments gives
$n\phi=\theta_0+2\pi k$. Taking $k=0,\ldots,n-1$ produces all distinct
possibilities: two listed angles differ by less than $2\pi$, so they cannot
describe the same point. Replacing $k$ by $k+n$ adds $2\pi$ to the root's
angle, so any other integer $k$ repeats one of them. If $z_0=0$, the
only $n$th root is $z=0$.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="eighth-roots-of-unity" of="complex-nth-root-formula" difficulty="routine">

### Find the eighth roots of unity

Since $1=e^{2\pi i k}$ for every integer $k$, the eighth roots of unity are
$$
z_k=e^{2\pi ik/8},\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,7.
$$
They are the eight equally spaced points on the unit circle. For example,
$$
z_0=1,\qquad
z_1=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}i,\qquad
z_2=i,
$$
and the remaining roots continue around the circle.

</Example>

The same polar multiplication gives the trigonometric identities in
[trigonometric and geometric applications](/algebra/complex-numbers/trigonometric-and-geometric-applications).
An $n$th root solves $z^n=z_0$; [polynomial
roots](/algebra/complex-numbers/complex-polynomials) extend the question to
zeros of general polynomials.
