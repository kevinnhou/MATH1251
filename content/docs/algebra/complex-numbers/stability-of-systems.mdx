This page uses the roots of [complex polynomials](/algebra/complex-numbers/complex-polynomials) to describe how linear systems evolve. A discrete system is built from modes $r^n$ and a continuous system from modes $e^{rt}$, where $r$ is a characteristic root; a zero root needs separate treatment. The modulus $|r|$ decides whether a discrete mode decays and the real part $\operatorname{Re}(r)$ decides the same for a continuous mode.

In the mode calculations, $r$ denotes a characteristic root, not the polar radius used in [polar form](/algebra/complex-numbers/polar-form).

## General discrete systems

<Recall of="complex-polynomial" />
<Recall of="polar-form" />

<Definition slug="discrete-time-system">

### Discrete-time system

<Statement>

A discrete-time system describes values $x_n$ at times $n=0,1,2,\ldots$ through a recurrence
$$
x_{n+1}=f(n,x_n,x_{n-1},\ldots).
$$
A first-order system uses $x_{n+1}=f(n,x_n)$ and needs one initial value; a second-order system uses $x_{n+1}=f(n,x_n,x_{n-1})$ and needs two.

</Statement>

</Definition>

The simplest linear case is the first-order recurrence
$$
x_{n+1}=a x_n.
$$
Repeated substitution gives $x_n=a^n x_0$ for $n\ge1$, where $x_0$ is the
initial value. So $|a|<1$ gives decay, $|a|>1$ gives growth and $|a|=1$
gives boundary behaviour, such as a constant or oscillating mode that does
not decay. Compound interest is the case $a=1+r$, so its long-term behaviour
depends only on the powers of one number.

Nonlinear recurrences fall outside the characteristic-root method.

<Example slug="logistic-iteration" of="discrete-time-system" difficulty="routine">

### A nonlinear non-example: logistic iteration

For the parameterised logistic recurrence
$$
x_{n+1}=r x_n(1-x_n),\qquad x_0=\frac13,
$$
the first terms for $r=2$ are
$$
x_1=\frac49,\qquad
x_2=2\left(\frac49\right)\left(1-\frac49\right)
=\frac{40}{81}.
$$
The factor $1-x_n$ depends on the current value, so this recurrence is not
linear and has no characteristic polynomial of the kind used below.
The delayed logistic recurrence
$$
x_{n+1}=rx_n(1-x_{n-1}),\qquad
x_0=\frac15,\quad x_1=\frac14
$$
requires both $x_0$ and $x_1$ before it can be iterated.

</Example>

## Linear recurrences and characteristic modes

<Definition slug="discrete-linear-system" of="discrete-time-system">

### Discrete linear system

<Statement>

A homogeneous second-order discrete linear system has the form

$$
a x_{n+1}+b x_n+c x_{n-1}=0,
\qquad n\ge1.
$$

Here $a,b,c\in\mathbb C$, $a\ne0$ and the coefficients are constant, so a
characteristic-root calculation can replace the recurrence by an algebraic
equation.

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Method slug="discrete-characteristic-root-method" of="discrete-linear-system,complex-polynomial">

### Find discrete modes from a characteristic equation

<Statement>

For $a\ne0$ and
$$
a x_{n+1}+b x_n+c x_{n-1}=0,
$$
try a non-trivial solution $x_n=K r^n$ with $K\ne0$ and $r\ne0$. Substitution gives
$$
K r^{n-1}(ar^2+br+c)=0.
$$
For this nonzero mode, the prefactor is nonzero, so its characteristic equation is
$$
ar^2+br+c=0.
$$
The zero-root case, if it occurs, is not covered by this $r^{n-1}$
factorisation and must be checked directly in the recurrence.
When the non-zero roots $r_1,r_2$ are distinct, the general solution is
$$
x_n=K_1r_1^n+K_2r_2^n.
$$
If there is a repeated non-zero root $r$, the general solution is instead
$$
x_n=(K_1+K_2n)r^n.
$$

</Statement>

The expression $ar^2+br+c$ is a complex polynomial. Its non-zero roots are
exactly the numbers for which the proposed exponential mode satisfies the
substitution. The factor $n$ in the repeated-root solution matters for
stability: even when $|r|=1$, the term $nr^n$ is unbounded.

If $c=0$, then zero is a characteristic root and the recurrence reduces to
$a x_{n+1}+b x_n=0$ for $n\ge1$. Here $x_0$ and $x_1$ are independent
initial values. For $b\ne0$, the later terms are
$x_n=x_1(-b/a)^{n-1}$ for $n\ge1$; for $b=0$, all terms from $x_2$ onward
are zero. This handles the zero-root case without using $0^0$.

</Method>

## Discrete stability

<Definition slug="discrete-stability">

### Discrete stability

<Statement>

For the linear systems considered here, a system is *stable* when every
solution satisfies $|x_n|\to0$ as $n\to\infty$ and *unstable* when some
solution is unbounded.

</Statement>

Here *stable* means asymptotically stable. A system whose solutions stay
bounded but do not all decay is a boundary case, neither stable nor unstable
under this convention.

Informally, “stable” sometimes means only “bounded”. The stricter convention
stops a unit-modulus mode from being mistaken for a decaying one.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="discrete-root-stability" of="discrete-characteristic-root-method,discrete-stability,complex-modulus">

### Discrete stability from root moduli

<Statement>

Write a characteristic root in polar form as
$$
r_j=\rho_j e^{i\theta_j}.
$$
Then
$$
|r_j^n|=\rho_j^n.
$$
Thus $\rho_j<1$ gives a decaying mode, $\rho_j>1$ gives an unbounded mode and
$\rho_j=1$ gives a non-decaying mode.

</Statement>

For the whole system, every solution decays precisely when all characteristic
roots satisfy $|r_j|<1$. A root with $|r_j|>1$ supplies an unbounded solution.
If all roots satisfy $|r_j|\le1$ and a unit-modulus root is present, its
multiplicity decides the remaining case: when each such root is simple,
every solution is bounded but some do not decay; a repeated unit-modulus
root instead produces an unbounded term $nr_j^n$.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="unstable-discrete-recurrence" of="discrete-root-stability" difficulty="routine">

### Test a recurrence

Consider
$$
x_{n+1}=x_n+x_{n-1}.
$$
The characteristic equation is
$$
r^2-r-1=0,
$$
whose roots are
$$
r_1=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},
\qquad
r_2=\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}.
$$
Since $|r_1|>1$, the corresponding mode grows. The system is unstable.
The second root has modulus less than $1$, but one growing mode is enough:
choosing initial data with a non-zero coefficient of $r_1^n$ produces an
unbounded solution.

</Example>

This page keeps the mode calculation scalar. For vector states, eigenvalues
take over the role of characteristic roots once a matrix system is
diagonalised: in [matrix powers](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications),
in [Markov systems](/algebra/eigenvalues/markov-systems) and, in continuous
time, in [linear ODE systems](/algebra/eigenvalues/linear-ode-systems). The
equations differ: a recurrence gives a polynomial in $r$ directly, while a
matrix $A$ uses $p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)$ in
[the eigenvalue test](/algebra/eigenvalues/eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors).

## Continuous systems and characteristic modes

This section can be read now or deferred until after
[second-order constant-coefficient equations](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/second-order-constant-coefficient-equations)
in Calculus. The trial-function calculation it needs is stated below.

<Definition slug="continuous-linear-system">

### Continuous-time system

<Statement>

A continuous-time system describes a function $x(t)$ through a differential
equation with $t\in\mathbb R$. For $a,b,c\in\mathbb C$ with $a\ne0$,
consider the second-order homogeneous equation
$$
a x''(t)+b x'(t)+cx(t)=0.
$$
The exponential trial function $x(t)=Ke^{rt}$ leads to the algebraic equation
$$
ar^2+br+c=0.
$$

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Method slug="continuous-characteristic-root-method" of="continuous-linear-system,complex-polynomial">

### Find continuous modes from a characteristic equation

<Statement>

For distinct roots $r_1,r_2$ of $ar^2+br+c=0$, the solution has the form
$$
x(t)=K_1e^{r_1t}+K_2e^{r_2t}.
$$
For a repeated root $r$, the solution has the form
$$
x(t)=(K_1+K_2t)e^{rt}.
$$
If $r=\alpha+i\beta$, then
$$
e^{rt}=e^{\alpha t}e^{i\beta t}.
$$

</Statement>

The factor $e^{\alpha t}$ controls growth or decay, while $e^{i\beta t}$
supplies oscillation. When the coefficients and required solution are real, a
conjugate pair $\alpha\pm i\beta$ (with $\beta\ne0$) combines into
$$
e^{\alpha t}\bigl(A\cos(\beta t)+B\sin(\beta t)\bigr),
\qquad A,B\in\mathbb R.
$$

The [constant-coefficient ODE method](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/second-order-constant-coefficient-equations)
in Calculus is the same calculation, with repeated roots treated in more
detail. The damped oscillator below shows each case.

</Method>

## Continuous stability

<Definition slug="continuous-stability">

### Continuous stability

<Statement>

For the linear systems considered here, a continuous system is *stable* when
every solution decays to zero as $t\to\infty$ and *unstable* when some
solution is unbounded.

</Statement>

As in the discrete case, this is asymptotic stability; bounded non-decaying
solutions form a boundary case.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="continuous-root-stability" of="continuous-characteristic-root-method,continuous-stability">

### Continuous stability from real parts

<Statement>

For a characteristic root $r=\alpha+i\beta$,
$$
|e^{rt}|=e^{\alpha t}.
$$
Therefore $\alpha<0$ gives decay, $\alpha>0$ gives an unbounded mode and, for
a simple root, $\alpha=0$ gives a non-decaying oscillatory or constant mode.
A repeated root on the imaginary axis introduces a term $t e^{rt}$, whose
magnitude is unbounded.

</Statement>

For the whole system, every solution decays precisely when all characteristic
roots have negative real part. A root with positive real part supplies an
unbounded solution. If no root has positive real part but a root lies on the
imaginary axis and each such root is simple, every solution is bounded but
some do not decay. A repeated root there gives an unbounded solution.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="damped-oscillator-root-classification" of="continuous-root-stability" difficulty="routine">

### Classify a damped oscillator

For
$$
m x''+c x'+kx=0,
$$
the roots satisfy
$$
mr^2+cr+k=0.
$$
Take $m=k=1$ and vary the damping $c$.

- If $c>2$, the roots are distinct negative real numbers: the motion is
  overdamped and decays without oscillating.
- If $c=2$, the repeated root is $r=-1$, so $(K_1+K_2t)e^{-t}\to0$: the
  motion is critically damped and decays.
- If $0<c<2$, the roots are a complex conjugate pair with negative real
  part: the motion is underdamped and oscillates while its amplitude decays.
- If $c=0$, the roots are $r=\pm i$, so the motion is periodic rather than
  decaying.
- If $-2<c<0$, the conjugate roots have positive real part $-c/2$, so
  oscillations grow and the system is unstable.

</Example>
