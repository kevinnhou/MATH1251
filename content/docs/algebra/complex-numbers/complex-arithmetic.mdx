This page introduces the complex numbers and their arithmetic. The real
numbers contain no solution of $x^2+1=0$. Adjoining a number $i$ with
$i^2=-1$ supplies one while keeping the usual rules of addition and
multiplication. A complex number then has a real component and a component
along the new $i$ direction.

## Cartesian form

<Definition slug="complex-number">

### Complex number

<Statement>

A *complex number* is a number of the form
$$
z=a+bi,\qquad a,b\in\mathbb R,\qquad i^2=-1.
$$
The expression $a+bi$ is the Cartesian form of $z$. Its real part is $\operatorname{Re}(z)=a$ and its imaginary part is $\operatorname{Im}(z)=b$.

</Statement>

Every real number is a complex number with imaginary part zero. Equality is componentwise:
$$
a+bi=c+di\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a=c\text{ and }b=d.
$$

When simplifying powers of $i$, use the four-term cycle
$$
i^0=1,\qquad i^1=i,\qquad i^2=-1,\qquad i^3=-i,
$$
which then repeats because $i^4=1$.

</Definition>

## The Argand plane

<Definition slug="argand-plane" of="complex-number">

### Argand plane

<Statement>

The *Argand plane* represents $z=a+bi$ by the point $(a,b)$, with the real axis horizontal and the imaginary axis vertical.

</Statement>

Under this identification, addition of complex numbers is vector addition,
and $z-w$ is the displacement from $w$ to $z$. So $|z-w|$ will measure the
distance between the two points. The picture also makes modulus and argument
geometric quantities in [polar form](/algebra/complex-numbers/polar-form).

The point represents the number but does not replace its algebra: complex
multiplication still combines both components according to $i^2=-1$.

</Definition>

## Conjugation and modulus

<Definition slug="complex-conjugate" of="complex-number">

### Complex conjugate

<Statement>

If $z=a+bi$, its *complex conjugate* is
$$
\overline z=a-bi.
$$

</Statement>

Conjugation changes the sign of the imaginary part and leaves the real part unchanged, so it reflects $z$ in the real axis. The product of $z$ and its conjugate is real:
$$
z\overline z=(a+bi)(a-bi)=a^2+b^2.
$$

</Definition>

<Definition slug="complex-modulus" of="complex-number">

### Complex modulus

<Statement>

The *modulus* of $z=a+bi$ is
$$
|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}.
$$

</Statement>

In the Argand plane, $|z|$ is the distance from $z$ to the origin. Division
and [polar form](/algebra/complex-numbers/polar-form) both use the identity
$z\overline z=|z|^2$.

</Definition>

## Arithmetic and conjugation laws

<Method slug="complex-arithmetic" of="complex-number">

### Add, multiply and divide complex numbers

<Statement>

For $z_1=x_1+iy_1$ and $z_2=x_2+iy_2$,
$$
z_1+z_2=(x_1+x_2)+i(y_1+y_2),
$$
$$
z_1z_2=(x_1x_2-y_1y_2)+i(x_1y_2+y_1x_2).
$$
The additive inverse is $-z_1$. For a non-zero $z=x+iy$,
$$
z^{-1}=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}-i\frac{y}{x^2+y^2},
\qquad zz^{-1}=1.
$$
Division is defined using this inverse. In practice, when $z_2\ne0$,
$$
\frac{z_1}{z_2}
=\frac{z_1\overline{z_2}}{z_2\overline{z_2}}.
$$

</Statement>

The multiplication formula follows by expanding and replacing $i^2$ by $-1$. The conjugate in the division formula makes the denominator real:
$$
z_2\overline{z_2}=|z_2|^2.
$$

</Method>

<Example slug="complex-arithmetic-calculation" of="complex-arithmetic" difficulty="routine">

### Calculate with Cartesian forms

Let $z_1=1+i$ and $z_2=\sqrt2-\pi i$. Then
$$
z_1+z_2=(1+\sqrt2)+(1-\pi)i,
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
z_1z_2
&=(1+i)(\sqrt2-\pi i)\\
&=(\sqrt2+\pi)+(\sqrt2-\pi)i.
\end{aligned}
$$

For subtraction,
$$
z_1-z_2=(1-\sqrt2)+(1+\pi)i.
$$
For division, multiply numerator and denominator by
$$
\overline{z_2}=\sqrt2+\pi i:
$$
$$
\frac{z_1}{z_2}
=\frac{(1+i)(\sqrt2+\pi i)}{(\sqrt2-\pi i)(\sqrt2+\pi i)}
=\frac{(\sqrt2-\pi)+(\sqrt2+\pi)i}{2+\pi^2}.
$$

</Example>

<Theorem slug="conjugation-rules" of="complex-conjugate">

### Rules for conjugation

<Statement>

For $z,z_1,z_2\in\mathbb C$ with $z_2\ne0$,
$$
\overline{\overline z}=z,\qquad
\overline{z_1+z_2}=\overline{z_1}+\overline{z_2},\qquad
\overline{z_1-z_2}=\overline{z_1}-\overline{z_2},\qquad
\overline{z_1z_2}=\overline{z_1}\,\overline{z_2},
$$
$$
\overline{\frac{z_1}{z_2}}
=\frac{\overline{z_1}}{\overline{z_2}},
\qquad
\operatorname{Re}(z)=\frac{z+\overline z}{2},
\qquad
\operatorname{Im}(z)=\frac{z-\overline z}{2i}.
$$

</Statement>

Each identity can be checked by writing the numbers in Cartesian form. The last two recover the real and imaginary parts of $z$ from $z$ and $\overline z$.

</Theorem>

## Fields and number systems

<Definition slug="field">

### Field

<Statement>

A *field* is a non-empty set equipped with addition and multiplication satisfying closure, associativity and commutativity for both operations, distinct additive and multiplicative identities $0\ne1$, additive inverses, multiplicative inverses for non-zero elements and the two distributive laws.

</Statement>

In a field we can add, subtract, multiply and divide by any non-zero element
without leaving the set. The real and complex numbers are fields. The natural
numbers fail the additive inverse axiom: $1\in\mathbb N$ but
$-1\notin\mathbb N$. The integers fail the multiplicative inverse axiom:
$2\in\mathbb Z$, but $2^{-1}=1/2\notin\mathbb Z$.

</Definition>

The number systems used in the course form the chain

$$
\mathbb N\subseteq\mathbb Z\subseteq\mathbb Q\subseteq\mathbb R\subseteq\mathbb C.
$$

Here $\mathbb N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$; the convention matters when deciding which
systems contain a solution. Each enlargement supplies solutions that the
previous system lacks. For a quadratic with real coefficients, the last step
is decided by the discriminant: a negative discriminant gives no real roots
but does give complex roots.

<Example slug="equations-over-number-systems" of="complex-number" difficulty="routine">

### Compare solutions across number systems

The equation $2x+4=8$ has the solution $x=2$ in every number system listed
above. The equation $2x+4=-8$ first becomes solvable in $\mathbb Z$, where
$x=-6$, while $2x+4=7$ first becomes solvable in $\mathbb Q$, where
$x=3/2$. The equation $2x^2+4=7$ has real solutions
$x=\pm\sqrt{3/2}$, which are irrational, whereas

$$
2x^2+4=3
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x=\pm\frac{i}{\sqrt2}
$$

requires $\mathbb C$.

A solution in one system also belongs to every larger system in the chain,
so each comparison identifies the smallest system that contains the
solution.

</Example>

[Polar form](/algebra/complex-numbers/polar-form) describes the same numbers by distance from the origin and direction, which simplifies multiplication and roots.
