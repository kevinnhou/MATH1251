This page writes a linear map $T:V\to W$ between finite-dimensional spaces as a matrix. A [basis](/algebra/vector-spaces/basis-and-dimension) gives each vector unique coordinates and a linear map is fixed by its values on a basis, so the matrix records the [coordinate vectors](/algebra/vector-spaces/coordinates-and-bases) of the images of the domain basis. The page then reads injectivity, surjectivity and invertibility of $T$ from its matrix.

## The coordinate matrix

<Recall of="linear-map" />

<Recall of="coordinate-vector" />

<Recall of="basis" />

The matrix depends on the chosen bases, but the linear map $T$ does not. Changing a basis changes the coordinate columns used to write $T$, not which vectors $T$ sends to which outputs.

Using the matrix takes three steps: write the input as a coordinate column in
$B_V$, multiply by $A$ and read the result as a coordinate column in $B_W$.

<Theorem slug="matrix-of-linear-map" of="linear-map,basis,coordinate-vector,linear-map-determined-by-basis">

### Matrix representation of a linear map

<Statement>

Let $T:V\to W$ be a linear map, where $\dim(V)=n$ and $\dim(W)=m$. For ordered bases

$$
B_V=(v_1,\ldots,v_n),\qquad B_W=(w_1,\ldots,w_m),
$$

form the coordinate columns

$$
a_j=[T(v_j)]_{B_W}\qquad (j=1,\ldots,n).
$$

Then

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}a_1&\cdots&a_n\end{pmatrix}
$$

is the unique $m\times n$ matrix satisfying

$$
[T(v)]_{B_W}=A[v]_{B_V}\qquad\text{for every }v\in V.
$$

We call $A$ the matrix representation of $T$ relative to $B_V$ and $B_W$ and may write it as $[T]_{B_V}^{B_W}$.

With this notation, the subscript names the domain basis and the superscript
names the codomain basis, matching the direction
$[v]_{B_V}\mapsto[T(v)]_{B_W}$ in the displayed coordinate identity.

</Statement>

The $j$th column is the coordinate vector of $T(v_j)$ in the codomain basis. Thus the number of columns is the dimension of the domain and the number of entries in each column is the dimension of the codomain.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="matrix-of-linear-map">

Write

$$
v=c_1v_1+\cdots+c_nv_n.
$$

Then

$$
[v]_{B_V}=
\begin{pmatrix}
c_1\\
\vdots\\
c_n
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Preservation of finite linear combinations gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
[T(v)]_{B_W}
&=c_1[T(v_1)]_{B_W}+\cdots+c_n[T(v_n)]_{B_W}\\
&=c_1a_1+\cdots+c_na_n\\
&=A[v]_{B_V}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This proves the coordinate identity. If another matrix has the same identity, apply it to each $v_j$. Since $[v_j]_{B_V}$ is the $j$th standard coordinate vector, its $j$th column must be $[T(v_j)]_{B_W}=a_j$. Every column is therefore forced, so $A$ is unique.

</Proof>

The theorem turns the basis-data principle into a construction. The order of both bases matters: it fixes the order of the entries in each coordinate column and the order of the columns themselves.

## Building and using the matrix

<Method slug="construct-matrix-representation" of="matrix-of-linear-map,coordinate-vector">

### Construct a matrix representation

<Statement>

For a linear map $T:V\to W$:

1. choose an ordered basis $B_V=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$ for $V$ and an ordered basis $B_W=(w_1,\ldots,w_m)$ for $W$;
2. evaluate $T(v_j)$ for each domain basis vector;
3. compute the codomain coordinate vector $[T(v_j)]_{B_W}$ for each image;
4. place these coordinate vectors, in the same order, as the columns of the $m\times n$ matrix.

</Statement>

</Method>

<Example of="construct-matrix-representation,matrix-of-linear-map" difficulty="routine">

### Differentiation on polynomial spaces

For $n\ge 1$, differentiation is the real-linear map

$$
D:P_n(\mathbb R)\to P_{n-1}(\mathbb R),\qquad D(p)=p'.
$$

Take $n=4$ and let

$$
T:P_4(\mathbb R)\to P_3(\mathbb R),\qquad T(p)=\frac{dp(x)}{dx},
$$

and use the ordered standard polynomial bases

$$
B_V=(1,x,x^2,x^3,x^4),\qquad B_W=(1,x,x^2,x^3).
$$

Evaluate $T$ on each domain basis vector and find the coordinates of the result in $B_W$:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{domain vector} & \text{image} & \text{codomain coordinate vector}\\
\hline
1 & T(1)=0 & \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\\[6pt]
x & T(x)=1 & \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\\[6pt]
x^2 & T(x^2)=2x & \begin{pmatrix}0\\2\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\\[6pt]
x^3 & T(x^3)=3x^2 & \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\3\\0\end{pmatrix}\\[6pt]
x^4 & T(x^4)=4x^3 & \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\\4\end{pmatrix}
\end{array}
$$

These coordinate vectors, in order, are the columns of the matrix representation:

$$
A=[T]_{B_V}^{B_W}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0&0&0\\
0&0&2&0&0\\
0&0&0&3&0\\
0&0&0&0&4
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For

$$
p=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3+a_4x^4,
$$

$$
[p]_{B_V}=
\begin{pmatrix}
a_0\\a_1\\a_2\\a_3\\a_4
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A[p]_{B_V}=
\begin{pmatrix}
a_1\\2a_2\\3a_3\\4a_4
\end{pmatrix}
=[T(p)]_{B_W}.
$$

This agrees with

$$
T(p)=a_1+2a_2x+3a_3x^2+4a_4x^3.
$$

The zero first column records $T(1)=0$, so differentiation is not injective. The four pivot columns give rank $4=\dim(P_3(\mathbb R))$, so it is surjective. In words, differentiation loses the constant direction but still reaches every polynomial of degree at most $3$.

</Example>

With standard bases, a vector in $\mathbb R^n$ equals its own coordinate column and the matrix representation is the [matrix map](/algebra/linear-transformations/geometric-examples) from the geometric examples. For these maps, operations on maps match operations on matrices.

<Proposition slug="matrix-operations-for-linear-maps" of="matrix-map-linear">

### Operations on matrix representations

<Statement>

Let $T_A:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ and $T_B:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ be the standard-coordinate matrix maps

$$
T_A(x)=Ax,\qquad T_B(x)=Bx.
$$

Then

$$
(T_A+T_B)(x)=(A+B)x,\qquad
(\omega T_A)(x)=(\omega A)x.
$$

If $T_A:\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R^m$ and $T_B:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^p$, then

$$
(T_A\circ T_B)(y)=A(By)=(AB)y.
$$

Thus the matrix of a sum, scalar multiple, or composition is respectively the sum, scalar multiple, or product of the matrices, with the product ordered to match the composition.

</Statement>

</Proposition>

For non-standard bases, the same rules hold when the matrices use the same
domain and codomain bases for a sum and a matching intermediate basis for a
composition. Otherwise the coordinate columns refer to different bases and
cannot be combined directly.

## Reading map properties in coordinates

The coordinate identity transfers questions about the [kernel](/algebra/linear-transformations/kernel-image-rank) and [image](/algebra/linear-transformations/kernel-image-rank) of $T$ to its matrix, where [rank-nullity](/algebra/linear-transformations/rank-nullity) applies to coordinate columns. This section defines injective, surjective and inverse maps and reads each property from the matrix.

<Definition slug="injective-map" of="linear-map">

### Injective map

<Statement>

A map $T:V\to W$ is *injective*, or one-to-one, when

$$
T(v_1)=T(v_2)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_1=v_2.
$$

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Corollary slug="injective-if-trivial-kernel" of="kernel-of-linear-map,injective-map">

### Injectivity and the kernel

<Statement>

A linear map $T:V\to W$ is injective if and only if

$$
\ker(T)=\{0\}.
$$

If $V$ is finite-dimensional, this is equivalent to
$\operatorname{nullity}(T)=0$.

</Statement>

If $T(v_1)=T(v_2)$, then $T(v_1-v_2)=0$. A trivial kernel therefore gives
$v_1=v_2$. Conversely, injectivity forces any vector with $T(v)=T(0)$ to be
$v=0$.

</Corollary>

<Definition slug="surjective-map" of="linear-map">

### Surjective map

<Statement>

A map $T:V\to W$ is *surjective*, or onto, when every $w\in W$ equals
$T(v)$ for some $v\in V$. Equivalently, $\operatorname{im}(T)=W$.

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Definition slug="bijective-map" of="injective-map,surjective-map">

### Bijective map

<Statement>

A map is *bijective* when it is both injective and surjective.

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Definition slug="identity-map" of="linear-map">

### Identity map

<Statement>

The identity map on $V$ is

$$
\operatorname{id}_V:V\to V,\qquad \operatorname{id}_V(v)=v.
$$

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Definition slug="inverse-map" of="bijective-map">

### Inverse map

<Statement>

For a map $T:V\to W$, a map $S:W\to V$ is an inverse of $T$ when

$$
S\circ T=\operatorname{id}_V,
\qquad
T\circ S=\operatorname{id}_W.
$$

</Statement>

The first composition is the left-inverse condition and the second is the
right-inverse condition. An inverse satisfies both.

</Definition>

Do not confuse the transpose with the inverse: $A^{\mathsf T}$ transposes rows and
columns, while $A^{-1}$ reverses the map when a two-sided inverse exists.
Taking reciprocals of individual entries does not generally produce
$A^{-1}$. For an orthogonal matrix $Q$, the special identity
$Q^{-1}=Q^{\mathsf T}$ follows from its orthonormal columns; see the
[symmetric-matrix diagonalisation](/algebra/eigenvalues/diagonalisation).

<Corollary slug="matrix-representation-properties" of="matrix-of-linear-map,kernel-of-linear-map,image-of-linear-map,rank-nullity-matrix,rank-nullity-theorem,injective-if-trivial-kernel">

### Properties read from a matrix representation

<Statement>

Let $A=[T]_{B_V}^{B_W}$ be the matrix representation of $T:V\to W$ over a
scalar field $\mathbb F$, with $\dim(V)=n$ and $\dim(W)=m$. Then

$$
\ker(A)=\{[v]_{B_V}:v\in\ker(T)\}
$$

and

$$
\operatorname{im}(A)=\{[w]_{B_W}:w\in\operatorname{im}(T)\}.
$$

Since a linear map is injective exactly when its kernel is trivial and
surjective exactly when its image is the codomain, these identities give the
coordinate tests $\ker(A)=\{0\}$ and $\operatorname{im}(A)=\mathbb F^m$.
Here $\ker(A)$ and $\operatorname{im}(A)$ are sets of coordinate columns;
the displayed equalities identify them with the corresponding abstract
subspaces through the coordinate maps.

Consequently,

$$
\operatorname{nullity}(A)=\operatorname{nullity}(T),
\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}(T).
$$

Hence $T$ is injective if and only if $\operatorname{nullity}(A)=0$ and $T$ is surjective if and only if $\operatorname{rank}(A)=m$. If $m=n$, then $T$ is bijective if and only if $A$ is invertible.

</Statement>

</Corollary>

<Example slug="integral-map-injective" of="injective-map,rank-nullity-theorem" difficulty="routine">

### Integration gives an injective map

Define

$$
I:P_n(\mathbb R)\to P_{n+1}(\mathbb R),
\qquad
I(p)(x)=\int_0^x p(t)\,dt.
$$

The integral is linear in $p$, so $I$ is a linear map.
If $I(p)$ is the zero polynomial, differentiating the identity
$I(p)(x)=0$ gives $p(x)=0$ for every $x$, so $\ker(I)=\{0\}$. Thus $I$ is
injective. Its rank is $\dim(P_n(\mathbb R))=n+1$, which is smaller than
$\dim(P_{n+1}(\mathbb R))=n+2$, so it is not surjective.
Concretely, every polynomial $I(p)$ vanishes at $x=0$, so the constant
polynomial $1$ is not in the image.

</Example>

## Invertibility in equal dimensions

When the domain and codomain have the same finite dimension, [rank-nullity](/algebra/linear-transformations/rank-nullity) makes injectivity and surjectivity equivalent. A bijective linear map has an inverse and in coordinates that inverse is given by the inverse matrix.

<Proposition slug="finite-dimensional-bijectivity" of="matrix-representation-properties,rank-nullity-theorem,injective-map,surjective-map">

### Bijectivity in equal dimensions

<Statement>

For a linear map $T:V\to W$ between finite-dimensional vector spaces:

1. if $T$ is bijective, then $\dim(V)=\dim(W)$;
2. if $\dim(V)=\dim(W)$ and $T$ is injective, then $T$ is surjective;
3. if $\dim(V)=\dim(W)$ and $T$ is surjective, then $T$ is injective.

</Statement>

Indeed, if $T$ is bijective, then its kernel is trivial and its image is all
of $W$. Rank-nullity therefore gives
$\dim(V)=\operatorname{rank}(T)=\dim(W)$. If the dimensions are equal and
$T$ is injective, then nullity is $0$, so rank equals $\dim(V)=\dim(W)$ and
$T$ is surjective. The surjective case is dual: rank equals $\dim(W)=\dim(V)$,
so nullity is $0$ and $T$ is injective.

</Proposition>

<Theorem slug="linear-map-invertibility" of="bijective-map,inverse-map,finite-dimensional-bijectivity">

### Equivalent invertibility conditions

<Statement>

For a linear map $T:V\to W$ between finite-dimensional spaces, the
following conditions are equivalent when $\dim(V)=\dim(W)$:

1. $T$ is invertible;
2. $T$ is bijective;
3. $T$ has a left inverse;
4. $T$ has a right inverse.

</Statement>

A left inverse makes $T$ injective and a right inverse makes $T$
surjective. In equal dimensions either property makes $T$ bijective, so the
one-sided inverse is then the two-sided inverse of $T$.

</Theorem>

<Theorem slug="inverse-matrix-of-linear-map" of="matrix-of-linear-map,matrix-representation-properties,finite-dimensional-bijectivity,linear-map-invertibility,inverse-map">

### Inverse maps in coordinates

<Statement>

Let $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces, let $T:V\to W$ be
bijective and fix ordered bases $B_V$ of $V$ and $B_W$ of $W$. Then
$T^{-1}:W\to V$ is linear. If

$$
A=[T]_{B_V}^{B_W},
$$

then $A$ is square and

$$
[T^{-1}]_{B_W}^{B_V}=A^{-1}.
$$

Equivalently, for every $w\in W$,

$$
[T^{-1}(w)]_{B_V}=A^{-1}[w]_{B_W}.
$$

Conversely, if the matrix representation $A$ is invertible, then $T$ is bijective.

</Statement>

The formula follows by writing $w=T(v)$, so that $[w]_{B_W}=A[v]_{B_V}$ and hence $[v]_{B_V}=A^{-1}[w]_{B_W}$. The two compositions are represented by $A^{-1}A$ and $AA^{-1}$, which return the original coordinate vector.
To check that $T^{-1}$ is linear, write $w_1=T(v_1)$ and $w_2=T(v_2)$.
Then $T(\alpha v_1+\beta v_2)=\alpha w_1+\beta w_2$ and uniqueness of
preimages gives
$T^{-1}(\alpha w_1+\beta w_2)=\alpha T^{-1}(w_1)+\beta T^{-1}(w_2)$.

</Theorem>

<Example of="inverse-matrix-of-linear-map" difficulty="routine">

### A scalar map and its inverse

Let $\alpha\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$ and define

$$
T:P_n(\mathbb R)\to P_n(\mathbb R),\qquad T(p)=\alpha p.
$$

Relative to the standard polynomial basis $(1,x,\ldots,x^n)$,

$$
A=[T]=\alpha I.
$$

The candidate inverse is

$$
S:P_n(\mathbb R)\to P_n(\mathbb R),\qquad S(p)=\frac{1}{\alpha}p,
$$

whose matrix is

$$
[S]=\frac{1}{\alpha}I=A^{-1}.
$$

For every $p\in P_n(\mathbb R)$,

$$
S(T(p))=S(\alpha p)=p,
\qquad
T(S(p))=T\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}p\right)=p.
$$

Thus $T$ is bijective with inverse $S$ and its matrix $[S]=A^{-1}$ matches the theorem.

</Example>
