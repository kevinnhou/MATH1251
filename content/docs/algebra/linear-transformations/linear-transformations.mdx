This page defines linear maps: functions between [vector spaces](/algebra/vector-spaces/vector-spaces) that preserve addition and scalar multiplication. It gives quick tests for linearity and shows that a linear map is fixed by its values on a basis, which is what later lets a map be written as a matrix.

## Defining and testing linearity

<Recall of="vector-space" />

<Definition slug="linear-map">

### Linear map

<Statement>

Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over the same field $F$. A function $T:V\to W$ is a *linear map* if

$$
T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v)\qquad\text{for all }u,v\in V,
$$
$$
T(\omega u)=\omega T(u)\qquad\text{for all }\omega\in F,\ u\in V.
$$

</Statement>

The first identity preserves addition and the second preserves scalar multiplication.
Together they make $T$ preserve every finite linear combination, so once the
images of a basis are known, no further choices are possible.

</Definition>

A linear map from a space to itself, $T:V\to V$, is also called a *linear
operator* or *endomorphism*. [Eigenvalues](/algebra/eigenvalues/eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors)
are defined for these same-space maps.

<Example of="linear-map" difficulty="routine">

### Scalar multiplication

Fix $a\in\mathbb R$ and define $T:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ by $T(x)=ax$. For $x_1,x_2\in\mathbb R$,

$$
T(x_1+x_2)=a(x_1+x_2)=ax_1+ax_2=T(x_1)+T(x_2).
$$

For $\lambda\in\mathbb R$,

$$
T(\lambda x)=a(\lambda x)=\lambda(ax)=\lambda T(x).
$$

Thus the scalar-multiplication map is linear.

</Example>

For the affine formula $T(x)=ax+b$, the scalar-compatibility expressions are

$$
T(\lambda x)=a\lambda x+b,
\qquad
\lambda T(x)=a\lambda x+\lambda b.
$$

When $b\ne0$, taking $\lambda=0$ makes these expressions unequal, so scalar compatibility fails. When $b=0$, the formula is the scalar-multiplication example above.

This is the difference between a linear map and a translation: adding a
non-zero constant moves the origin, whereas every linear map fixes it.

<Example of="linear-map" difficulty="routine">

### A matrix times a vector

For fixed $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb R$, define $T:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2$ by

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}ax_1+bx_2\\cx_1+dx_2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Write
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For $u,v\in\mathbb R^2$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb R$, matrix multiplication distributes over addition and scalar multiplication:

$$
T(u+v)=A(u+v)=Au+Av=T(u)+T(v),
\qquad
T(\lambda u)=A(\lambda u)=\lambda Au=\lambda T(u).
$$

Hence this matrix formula defines a linear map.

</Example>

<Proposition slug="linear-map-zero-and-negatives" of="linear-map">

### Zero and additive inverses

<Statement>

If $T:V\to W$ is a linear map, then

$$
T(0)=0,
\qquad
T(-u)=-T(u)\quad\text{for all }u\in V.
$$

</Statement>

</Proposition>

<Proof of="linear-map-zero-and-negatives">

Linearity gives
$$
T(0)=T(0+0)=T(0)+T(0),
$$
so cancellation in $W$ gives $T(0)=0$. Also,
$$
T(-u)+T(u)=T(-u+u)=T(0)=0,
$$
so $T(-u)$ is the additive inverse of $T(u)$.

</Proof>

So a map with $T(0)\ne0$ cannot be linear. This zero test is necessary but not sufficient: a formula may send $0$ to $0$ and still fail one of the two defining identities.

<Example of="linear-map" difficulty="routine">

### Testing proposed maps

The map $T:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ given by $T(x)=x^2$ sends $0$ to $0$, but it is not additive:

$$
T(1+1)=4\ne2=T(1)+T(1).
$$

The map $T(x)=x^3$ also sends $0$ to $0$, but scalar compatibility fails, for example,

$$
T(2\cdot1)=8\ne2=2T(1).
$$

Neither formula is a linear map.

</Example>

## Preserving arbitrary linear combinations

The two defining identities combine into one test. It is usually the
quickest way to apply linearity in a calculation: write the input as a linear
combination, then apply the same coefficients to the known outputs.

<Theorem slug="two-term-linear-combination" of="linear-map">

### Preservation of two-term linear combinations

<Statement>

A function $T:V\to W$ is a linear map if and only if

$$
T(\omega_1u+\omega_2v)
=
\omega_1T(u)+\omega_2T(v)
$$

for all $\omega_1,\omega_2\in F$ and $u,v\in V$.

</Statement>

The two-term identity is the definition in one line: additivity handles the sum and scalar compatibility handles the coefficients.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="two-term-linear-combination">

If $T$ is linear, then
$$
T(\omega_1u+\omega_2v)
=
T(\omega_1u)+T(\omega_2v)
=
\omega_1T(u)+\omega_2T(v).
$$
Conversely, choosing $(\omega_1,\omega_2)=(1,1)$ gives additivity. Choosing $(\omega_1,\omega_2)=(\lambda,0)$ gives
$$
T(\lambda u)=\lambda T(u)+0T(v)=\lambda T(u),
$$
so scalar compatibility also holds.

</Proof>

<Theorem slug="finite-linear-combination-preservation" of="linear-map,two-term-linear-combination">

### Preservation of finite linear combinations

<Statement>

If $T:V\to W$ is a linear map, $u_1,\ldots,u_n\in V$ and $\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_n\in F$, then

$$
T(\omega_1u_1+\cdots+\omega_nu_n)
=
\omega_1T(u_1)+\cdots+\omega_nT(u_n).
$$

</Statement>

This is the form used when a vector is written in a [basis](/algebra/vector-spaces/basis-and-dimension) or as a linear combination of other vectors.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="finite-linear-combination-preservation">

For $n=1$, the statement is scalar compatibility. Assume it holds for $n$ terms. Separating the last summand gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\omega_1u_1+\cdots+\omega_nu_n+\omega_{n+1}u_{n+1})
&=T(\omega_1u_1+\cdots+\omega_nu_n)
  +T(\omega_{n+1}u_{n+1})\\
&=\omega_1T(u_1)+\cdots+\omega_nT(u_n)
  +\omega_{n+1}T(u_{n+1}).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the identity holds for every finite number of summands.

</Proof>

## What basis data determines

Once a linear map's values on a basis are known, every other value is forced by the coefficients that express the input in that basis. Those coefficients form the [coordinate vector](/algebra/vector-spaces/coordinates-and-bases) of the input.

<Theorem slug="linear-map-determined-by-basis" of="linear-map,basis,finite-linear-combination-preservation">

### Values on a basis determine a linear map

<Statement>

Let $B=(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$ be a basis for $V$ and let $T:V\to W$ be linear. If

$$
v=\omega_1u_1+\cdots+\omega_nu_n,
$$

then

$$
T(v)=\omega_1T(u_1)+\cdots+\omega_nT(u_n).
$$

Therefore the values of $T$ on the basis vectors determine $T(v)$ for every $v\in V$.

</Statement>

Conversely, any chosen values $w_1,\ldots,w_n\in W$ on the basis vectors
define a linear map by
$T(\omega_1u_1+\cdots+\omega_nu_n)=\omega_1w_1+\cdots+\omega_nw_n$.
The rule is well defined because basis coordinates are unique. It is linear
because coordinates add and scale: if two vectors have coordinate lists
$(\omega_i)$ and $(\eta_i)$, then their sum has list $(\omega_i+\eta_i)$ and
$\lambda$ times the first has list $(\lambda\omega_i)$. So basis data both
constructs a linear map and determines it uniquely.

</Theorem>

<Example of="linear-map-determined-by-basis" difficulty="routine">

### A map from $\mathbb R^3$ to $\mathbb R$

Let $T:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R$ be linear and suppose, for the standard basis vectors,

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}=7,\qquad
T\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}=-2,\qquad
T\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}=-3.
$$

Since
$$
\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{pmatrix}
=
x_1\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
+x_2\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}
+x_3\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix},
$$
linearity gives

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{pmatrix}
=
7x_1-2x_2-3x_3.
$$

Substituting each standard basis vector recovers the three stated values.

</Example>

<Example of="linear-map-determined-by-basis" difficulty="routine">

### Non-standard basis data

Let $T:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R$ be linear and suppose

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}=7,\qquad
T\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}=-2,\qquad
T\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}=-3.
$$

The missing standard basis vector satisfies

$$
\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}
-\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
-\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

So the span of the three given inputs contains every standard basis vector,
and their images determine $T$ everywhere. Applying $T$ to the identity gives

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=-2-7-(-3)=-6.
$$

Therefore

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{pmatrix}
=
7x_1-6x_2-3x_3.
$$

As a check, the formula gives $7-6-3=-2$ at $(1,1,1)^T$, as required.

</Example>

<Example of="linear-map-determined-by-basis" difficulty="routine">

### A map from $\mathbb R^3$ to $\mathbb R^2$

Let $T:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2$ be linear and suppose

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}7\\-6\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
T\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}-2\\4\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
T\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}-3\\5\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Applying the basis formula componentwise gives

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
7x_1-2x_2-3x_3\\
-6x_1+4x_2+5x_3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The coefficient columns are the given images of the standard basis vectors. If $T$ were also required to satisfy

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},
$$

linearity would instead force

$$
\begin{pmatrix}7\\-6\end{pmatrix}
+
\begin{pmatrix}-2\\4\end{pmatrix}
+
\begin{pmatrix}-3\\5\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix},
$$

so no linear map can satisfy the additional condition.

</Example>
