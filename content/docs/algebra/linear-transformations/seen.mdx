These past exam questions move between the abstract and coordinate views of a linear transformation. They run from basis data and coordinates, through operators on function spaces and geometric projections, to rank and dimension arguments.

Some questions use eigenvalue or diagonalisation ideas from the [eigenvalues
chapter](/algebra/eigenvalues/diagonalisation). They are marked
**Later-topic preview**, so you can skip them on a first reading and return
after studying that chapter. For the geometric picture, review [geometric
examples](/algebra/linear-transformations/geometric-examples).

In each solution, first identify the structural fact being tested, such as
preservation of a linear combination or a kernel inclusion and only then
carry out the calculation.

### A linear transformation from a quadrilateral

A mapping $T:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2$ is a linear transformation. A
quadrilateral $ABCD$ and its image under $T$ have vertices

$$
A=(-3,1),\qquad B=(-1,1),\qquad C=(1,0),\qquad D=(1,-1),
$$

and

$$
T(A)=(-1,-3),\qquad T(B)=(-1,-1),\qquad
T(C)=(0,1),\qquad T(D)=(1,1).
$$

(a) Find the coefficients $\alpha_1,\beta_1,\alpha_2,\beta_2$ such that

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=\alpha_1
\begin{pmatrix}-3\\1\end{pmatrix}
+\beta_1
\begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\end{pmatrix};
\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
=\alpha_2
\begin{pmatrix}-3\\1\end{pmatrix}
+\beta_2
\begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

(b) Find a matrix $P$ such that $T(\mathbf{x})=P\mathbf{x}$.

#### Solution

The vectors on the right in part (a) are $A$ and $B$, so part (a) expresses the standard basis vectors $\mathbf e_1=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\mathbf e_2=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$ in terms of $A$ and $B$.
This is possible because $A$ and $B$ are not parallel: the determinant of
the matrix with columns $A$ and $B$ is $(-3)(1)-(-1)(1)=-2\ne0$.

For $\mathbf e_1$, comparing coordinates gives

$$
-3\alpha_1-\beta_1=1,
\qquad
\alpha_1+\beta_1=0.
$$

The second equation gives $\beta_1=-\alpha_1$. Substitution into the first equation gives

$$
-3\alpha_1+\alpha_1=1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
-2\alpha_1=1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\alpha_1=-\frac12,
\qquad
\beta_1=\frac12.
$$

For $\mathbf e_2$, the coordinate equations are

$$
-3\alpha_2-\beta_2=0,
\qquad
\alpha_2+\beta_2=1.
$$

Using $\beta_2=1-\alpha_2$ in the first equation gives

$$
-3\alpha_2-(1-\alpha_2)=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
-2\alpha_2-1=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\alpha_2=-\frac12,
\qquad
\beta_2=\frac32.
$$

Hence

$$
{
\alpha_1=-\frac12,\quad
\beta_1=\frac12,\quad
\alpha_2=-\frac12,\quad
\beta_2=\frac32
}.
$$

Because $T$ is linear, apply $T$ to these coordinate identities:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\mathbf e_1)
&=-\frac12T(A)+\frac12T(B)\\
&=-\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-3\end{pmatrix}
+\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\mathbf e_2)
&=-\frac12T(A)+\frac32T(B)\\
&=-\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-3\end{pmatrix}
+\frac32
\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The columns of the standard-coordinate matrix are the images of $\mathbf e_1$ and $\mathbf e_2$. Therefore

$$
{
P=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-1\\
1&0
\end{pmatrix}
}.
$$

For a check, this matrix sends the four given vertices to

$$
P
\begin{pmatrix}-3\\1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-3\end{pmatrix},
\quad
P
\begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\end{pmatrix},
$$

$$
P
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},
\quad
P
\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus the matrix agrees with every given image vertex. Part (a) recovers the
standard-basis images from $T(A)$ and $T(B)$ and those images become the
columns of $P$. This is the coordinate form of the fact that a linear map is
determined by its values on a basis.

</Example>

### Values of a partially specified linear transformation

Let $T:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2$ be a linear transformation such that

$$
T(\mathbf e_1)=
\begin{pmatrix}
-9\\
8
\end{pmatrix}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
T\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
1\\
1
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2\\
8
\end{pmatrix},
$$

where $\mathbf e_1=(1,0,0)^T$.

Using the information given, compute the following, or state that there is not enough information by entering the word **undetermined**.

#### (a)

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
0\\
0
\end{pmatrix}
=\ ?
$$

#### (b)

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
1\\
0
\end{pmatrix}
=\ ?
$$

#### (c)

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}
1\\
1\\
1
\end{pmatrix}
=\ ?
$$

Now suppose that you also know

$$
T(\mathbf e_3)=
\begin{pmatrix}
4\\
-9
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

#### (d)

Compute all of the vectors that you could not compute above:

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
0\\
0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
T\begin{pmatrix}
0\\
1\\
0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
T\begin{pmatrix}
1\\
1\\
1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

#### Solution

Rewrite each input as a linear combination of the vectors whose images are known. Linearity applies the same coefficients to the corresponding output vectors.

For (a), every linear transformation sends the zero vector to the zero vector:

$$
{
T\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}
}.
$$

For (b), the given information does not determine
$T(\mathbf e_2)$. Let
$\mathbf r=T(\mathbf e_3)$. Since

$$
\mathbf e_2
=
\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}
-\mathbf e_3,
$$

linearity gives

$$
T(\mathbf e_2)
=
T\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}
-T(\mathbf e_3)
=
\begin{pmatrix}2\\8\end{pmatrix}
-\mathbf r.
$$

The vector $\mathbf r$ is not known at this stage, so the answer to (b) is

$$
{\text{undetermined}}.
$$

For (c),

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}
=\mathbf e_1+
\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
T\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}
&=T(\mathbf e_1)
+T\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}-9\\8\end{pmatrix}
+\begin{pmatrix}2\\8\end{pmatrix}\\
&={\begin{pmatrix}-7\\16\end{pmatrix}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For (d), the additional information is

$$
T(\mathbf e_3)=
\begin{pmatrix}4\\-9\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For the previously undetermined vector, use

$$
\mathbf e_2+\mathbf e_3
=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\mathbf e_2)
&=T\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}-T(\mathbf e_3)\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}2\\8\end{pmatrix}
-\begin{pmatrix}4\\-9\end{pmatrix}\\
&={\begin{pmatrix}-2\\17\end{pmatrix}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The three requested entries in part (d) are therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
T\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
&=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},\\[2mm]
T\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}
&=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\17\end{pmatrix},\\[2mm]
T\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}
&=\begin{pmatrix}-7\\16\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first data set fixes $T(\mathbf e_1)$ and the sum
$T(\mathbf e_2)+T(\mathbf e_3)$, but not the two summands separately. The extra value of $T(\mathbf e_3)$ removes exactly that remaining freedom. Once the images of all three standard basis vectors are known, every value of the linear map is forced by its coordinates.

</Example>

### Trigonometric polynomials and a differential operator

> **Later-topic preview:** the interpretation in terms of eigenvalues uses
> ideas from the later eigenvalues chapter.

A real trigonometric polynomial of degree $N$, where $N$ is a positive integer, is a function $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ of the form

$$
f(x)=a_0+\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n\cos(nx)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_n\sin(nx),
\tag{1}
$$

where $a_n,b_n\in\mathbb R$ and the highest-degree coefficients $a_N,b_N$ are not both zero. For $N=0$, the polynomial has degree zero and $f(x)=a_0$ for all $x\in\mathbb R$.

#### (a)

Using Euler's formula, write the function in (1) as

$$
f(x)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}c_ne^{inx}.
$$

Find $c_0$ and $c_n$ for $n\ne0$.

#### (b)

The set of all real trigonometric polynomials of degree at most $45$, denoted by $\operatorname{Trig}_{45}(\mathbb R)$, is a vector space over $\mathbb R$ with the usual addition and scalar multiplication.

For the following statements, let $d$ be an integer satisfying $1\le d\le45$.

Consider the linear transformation

$$
T:\operatorname{Trig}_{45}(\mathbb R)\longrightarrow
\operatorname{Trig}_{45}(\mathbb R),
\qquad
T(f)=f''+16f.
$$

##### (i)

Choose all correct statements:

- For all such $d$, $\cos(3x)+\sin(dx)$ is in $\ker(T)$.
- For all such $d$, $\cos(3x)+\sin(dx)$ is not in $\ker(T)$.
- For some but not all such $d$, $\cos(3x)+\sin(dx)$ is in $\ker(T)$.
- For all such $d$, $\cos(3x)+\sin(dx)$ is in $\operatorname{im}(T)$.
- For all such $d$, $\cos(3x)+\sin(dx)$ is not in $\operatorname{im}(T)$.
- For some but not all such $d$, $\cos(3x)+\sin(dx)$ is in $\operatorname{im}(T)$.

##### (ii)

Find a basis for $\ker(T)$.

##### (iii)

Find $\operatorname{im}(T)$ and explain your answer.

#### Solution

For part (a), use

$$
\cos(nx)=\frac{e^{inx}+e^{-inx}}2,
\qquad
\sin(nx)=\frac{e^{inx}-e^{-inx}}{2i}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_n\cos(nx)+b_n\sin(nx)
&=\frac{a_n-ib_n}{2}e^{inx}
+\frac{a_n+ib_n}{2}e^{-inx}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The coefficient of the zero-frequency term is unchanged, so

$$
{c_0=a_0}.
$$

For $k=1,\ldots,N$, the positive-frequency coefficient is

$$
{c_k=\frac{a_k-ib_k}{2}.}
$$

The corresponding negative-frequency coefficient is

$$
{c_{-k}=\frac{a_k+ib_k}{2}.}
$$

As a check, because the original polynomial is real-valued,

$$
c_{-n}=\overline{c_n}.
$$

For part (b), the restriction $1\le d\le45$ ensures that $\cos(3x)+\sin(dx)$ belongs to $\operatorname{Trig}_{45}(\mathbb R)$.
The operator $T=D^2+16I$ is linear because differentiation and addition
preserve linear combinations.

For $k\ge1$,

$$
\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\cos(kx)=-k^2\cos(kx),
\qquad
\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\sin(kx)=-k^2\sin(kx).
$$

Consequently,

$$
T(\cos(kx))=(16-k^2)\cos(kx),
\qquad
T(\sin(kx))=(16-k^2)\sin(kx),
$$

and $T(1)=16$.

##### (b)(i)

For the proposed polynomial,

$$
T\bigl(\cos(3x)+\sin(dx)\bigr)
=7\cos(3x)+(16-d^2)\sin(dx).
$$

The coefficient of $\cos(3x)$ is always $7$, so this expression can never be zero. Hence $\cos(3x)+\sin(dx)$ is in the kernel for no integer $d$ with $1\le d\le45$.

The only frequency missing from the image is $4$, because

$$
16-4^2=0.
$$

If $d=4$, the term $\sin(4x)$ cannot be produced by $T$, so

$$
\cos(3x)+\sin(4x)\notin\operatorname{im}(T).
$$

If $d\ne4$, both frequencies $3$ and $d$ have non-zero multiplier $16-k^2$, so the polynomial is in the image. For example, when $d\ne4$,

$$
T\left(\frac17\cos(3x)+\frac1{16-d^2}\sin(dx)\right)
=\cos(3x)+\sin(dx).
$$

The correct selections are therefore

$$
{\text{for all integers }d\text{ with }1\le d\le45,\ 
\cos(3x)+\sin(dx)\notin\ker(T)}
$$

and

$$
{\text{for some but not all integers }d\text{ with }1\le d\le45,\ 
\cos(3x)+\sin(dx)\in\operatorname{im}(T)}.
$$

##### (b)(ii)

Write

$$
f(x)=a_0+\sum_{k=1}^{45}
\bigl(a_k\cos(kx)+b_k\sin(kx)\bigr).
$$

Then

$$
T(f)=16a_0+
\sum_{k=1}^{45}(16-k^2)
\bigl(a_k\cos(kx)+b_k\sin(kx)\bigr).
$$

The functions $1,\cos(kx),\sin(kx)$ at distinct frequencies are linearly
independent. One way to see this is to use part (a), multiply a zero
exponential sum by $e^{45ix}$ and obtain a complex polynomial in
$e^{ix}$ that vanishes at infinitely many points of the unit circle; a
non-zero polynomial cannot do that.

Therefore $T(f)=0$ forces every displayed coefficient to vanish, so the
constant coefficient and all frequencies except $k=4$ must be zero:

$$
a_0=0,\qquad a_k=b_k=0\quad(k\ne4).
$$

The coefficients $a_4,b_4$ are unrestricted because $16-4^2=0$. Hence

$$
\ker(T)=\operatorname{span}\{\cos(4x),\sin(4x)\},
$$

so one basis is

$$
{\{\cos(4x),\sin(4x)\}}.
$$

##### (b)(iii)

Every output of $T$ has zero $\cos(4x)$ and $\sin(4x)$ coefficients. Conversely, if

$$
g(x)=\gamma_0+
\sum_{\substack{1\le k\le45\\k\ne4}}
\bigl(\gamma_k\cos(kx)+\delta_k\sin(kx)\bigr),
$$

then the polynomial

$$
h(x)=\frac{\gamma_0}{16}
+\sum_{\substack{1\le k\le45\\k\ne4}}
\left(
\frac{\gamma_k}{16-k^2}\cos(kx)
+\frac{\delta_k}{16-k^2}\sin(kx)
\right)
$$

belongs to $\operatorname{Trig}_{45}(\mathbb R)$ and satisfies $T(h)=g$. Therefore

$$
{
\operatorname{im}(T)
=\operatorname{span}
\left(
\{1\}\cup
\{\cos(kx),\sin(kx):1\le k\le45,\ k\ne4\}
\right)
}.
$$

Equivalently, the image is the set of trigonometric polynomials in
$\operatorname{Trig}_{45}(\mathbb R)$ whose $\cos(4x)$ and $\sin(4x)$ coefficients are both zero. Its dimension is $91-2=89$, in agreement with rank-nullity.

The operator $D^2+16I$ acts diagonally, multiplying each frequency $k$ by $16-k^2$. Frequency $4$ has eigenvalue zero, so its two real directions form the kernel and removing exactly those two directions gives the image.

</Example>

### A projection, nullity and a composition

Suppose

$$
\mathbf b=
\begin{pmatrix}
-2\\
6\\
-2
\end{pmatrix},
$$

and let $T:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^3$ be the linear transformation defined by

$$
T(\mathbf x)=\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf b}(\mathbf x)
=\left(\frac{\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf b}{\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf b}\right)\mathbf b,
\qquad \mathbf x\in\mathbb R^3.
$$

Additionally, suppose

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
-7&-9&-4\\
5&-1&9\\
7&-2&8
\end{pmatrix},
$$

and let $S:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^3$ be the linear transformation defined by

$$
S(\mathbf x)=A\mathbf x,
\qquad \mathbf x\in\mathbb R^3.
$$

You are also given that $\det(A)\ne0$.

#### (a) Nullity of $T$

##### (i)

The nullity of $T$ is $\operatorname{nullity}(T)=$

##### (ii)

Give a brief explanation of how this value is obtained.

#### (b) Kernel of $S\circ T$

The kernel of $S\circ T$ is the set of all $\mathbf x=(x,y,z)^T\in\mathbb R^3$ satisfying a Cartesian equation.

Briefly explain how the formula for this equation is obtained.

#### Solution

Part (a) needs only the projection, whose kernel can be found directly. For part (b), $\det(A)\ne0$ makes $S$ invertible, so $S$ sends no non-zero vector to zero and composing with $S$ does not change the kernel of $T$.

First,

$$
\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf b=(-2)^2+6^2+(-2)^2=4+36+4=44.
$$

Every value of $T$ is a scalar multiple of $\mathbf b$, so
$\operatorname{im}(T)\subseteq\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf b\}$. Also,

$$
T(\mathbf b)
=\left(\frac{\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf b}{\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf b}\right)\mathbf b
=\mathbf b,
$$

so $\operatorname{im}(T)=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf b\}$ and
$\operatorname{rank}(T)=1$. Since the domain has dimension $3$, the rank-nullity theorem gives

$$
\operatorname{nullity}(T)
=\dim(\mathbb R^3)-\operatorname{rank}(T)
=3-1
={2}.
$$

This can also be checked by finding the kernel. For
$\mathbf x=(x,y,z)^T$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\mathbf x)=0
&\Longleftrightarrow
\left(\frac{\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf b}{44}\right)\mathbf b=0\\
&\Longleftrightarrow
\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf b=0,
\end{aligned}
$$

because $\mathbf b\ne0$. Therefore

$$
\ker(T)
=\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}
:-2x+6y-2z=0
\right\}.
$$

Dividing the equation by $-2$ gives $x-3y+z=0$, or $x=3y-z$. Hence

$$
\ker(T)
=\left\{
s\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}
+t\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}
:s,t\in\mathbb R
\right\},
$$

which is a two-dimensional plane, agreeing with the nullity calculation.

For part (b), $\det(A)\ne0$ means that $A$ is invertible. Thus

$$
\ker(S)=\{0\}.
$$

For any $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^3$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf x\in\ker(S\circ T)
&\Longleftrightarrow S(T(\mathbf x))=0\\
&\Longleftrightarrow A\,T(\mathbf x)=0\\
&\Longleftrightarrow T(\mathbf x)=0\\
&\Longleftrightarrow \mathbf x\in\ker(T).
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\ker(S\circ T)=\ker(T)
=\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}
:-2x+6y-2z=0
\right\}.
$$

The requested Cartesian equation is therefore

$$
{-2x+6y-2z=0},
$$

equivalently $x-3y+z=0$.

In summary, the kernel of the projection is the orthogonal complement of $\mathbf b$ and rank-nullity turns the one-dimensional image into nullity $2$. The determinant condition is used only to show that $S$ is injective, which gives $\ker(S\circ T)=\ker(T)$.

</Example>

### A projection onto a subspace

> **Later-topic preview:** parts (b) and (c) use eigenvalues and
> eigenvectors.

Suppose that $H$ is a subspace of $\mathbb R^3$ defined as follows:

$$
H=\operatorname{span}\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}-5\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}
\right\}.
$$

#### (a) Choose the correct statement about $H$

1. The subspace $H$ is a plane through the origin.
2. The subspace $H$ is a line which does **not** go through the origin.
3. The subspace $H$ is a plane which does **not** go through the origin.
4. The subspace $H$ is a line through the origin.
5. The subspace $H$ is the entire space $\mathbb R^3$.

The mapping $T:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^3$ is the *projection onto $H$*,
defined as follows: for any vector $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^3$, $T(\mathbf x)$
is the unique vector in $H$ such that $\mathbf x-T(\mathbf x)$ is
perpendicular to every vector in $H$.

It can be proved that $T$ is a linear transformation. It is also known that
$T(\mathbf n)=0$ for any normal $\mathbf n$ to $H$ and
$T(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x$ for any vector $\mathbf x\in H$. These facts do not
need to be proved here.

#### (b) Select all that apply

1. Any nonzero vector in $H$ is an eigenvector of $T$.
2. There exists a nonzero vector in $H$ that is not an eigenvector of $T$.
3. Any nonzero normal vector to $H$ is an eigenvector of $T$.
4. The linear transformation $T$ has at least one zero eigenvalue.
5. All eigenvalues of $T$ are zero.
6. All eigenvalues of $T$ are nonzero.
7. The linear transformation $T$ has at least one eigenvalue that equals $1$.
8. The rank of $T$ is $1$.
9. The rank of $T$ is $2$.
10. The nullity of $T$ is $1$.
11. The nullity of $T$ is $2$.

#### (c) Select all that apply

1. The vector
   $\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}$ is an eigenvector of $T$.
2. The vector
   $\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}$ is an eigenvector of $T$.
3. The vector
   $\begin{pmatrix}-2\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ is an eigenvector of $T$.

#### Solution

Let

$$
\mathbf u_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf u_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf u_3=\begin{pmatrix}-5\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The third vector is redundant because

$$
-3\mathbf u_1-2\mathbf u_2
=-3\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}
-2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-5\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}
=\mathbf u_3.
$$

The first two vectors are not scalar multiples, so they are linearly
independent. Therefore

$$
H=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u_1,\mathbf u_2\},
\qquad
\dim(H)=2.
$$

Since a two-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb R^3$ is a plane and every
subspace contains the zero vector, the correct statement in part (a) is

$$
{\text{The subspace $H$ is a plane through the origin.}}
$$

A normal vector is

$$
\mathbf n=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix},
$$

because

$$
\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf u_1=(-1)(1)+(-1)(0)+(-1)(-1)=0
$$

and

$$
\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf u_2=(-1)(1)+(-1)(-1)+(-1)(0)=0.
$$

Thus

$$
H=\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb R^3:x+y+z=0
\right\},
$$

and $\mathbb R^3$ decomposes as

$$
\mathbb R^3=H\oplus\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf n\}.
$$

The projection is the identity on $H$ and is zero on
$\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf n\}$. Relative to the basis
$(\mathbf u_1,\mathbf u_2,\mathbf n)$, its matrix is therefore

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The matrix shows the eigenvalues and the dimensions of the corresponding
eigenspaces:

$$
\lambda=1 \text{ on }H,
\qquad
\lambda=0 \text{ on }\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf n\}.
$$

Consequently, the statements selected in part (b) are

$$
{
\begin{gathered}
\text{Any nonzero vector in $H$ is an eigenvector of $T$},\\
\text{any nonzero normal vector to $H$ is an eigenvector of $T$},\\
\text{$T$ has at least one zero eigenvalue},\\
\text{$T$ has at least one eigenvalue equal to $1$},\\
\operatorname{rank}(T)=2,\qquad
\operatorname{nullity}(T)=1.
\end{gathered}
}
$$

Indeed, $\operatorname{im}(T)=H$, so $\operatorname{rank}(T)=\dim(H)=2$,
while $\ker(T)=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf n\}$, so
$\operatorname{nullity}(T)=1$.

For part (c), the first vector is $\mathbf n$, so

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}
=\mathbf 0
=0\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

It is an eigenvector for $\lambda=0$. The second vector is $\mathbf u_1\in H$,
so

$$
T\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix},
$$

and it is an eigenvector for $\lambda=1$.

For the third vector, let

$$
\mathbf w=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The orthogonal projection onto the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf n$ is

$$
T(\mathbf w)
=\mathbf w-\frac{\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf n}{\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf n}\mathbf n.
$$

Here $\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf n=3$ and $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf n=3$, so

$$
T(\mathbf w)
=\begin{pmatrix}-2\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}
-\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

This is not a scalar multiple of $\mathbf w$: comparing second coordinates
forces the scalar to be $0$, but then the first coordinates $-1$ and $0$
disagree. Therefore the third vector is not an eigenvector. The selected vectors in
part (c) are

$$
{
\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\\-1\end{pmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
}
$$

Geometrically, vectors in the plane are fixed, with eigenvalue $1$ and
vectors in the normal direction are sent to zero, with eigenvalue $0$. The
same split into a two-dimensional image and a one-dimensional kernel gives
the rank and nullity.

</Example>

### Kernels, ranks and powers of a matrix

Let $A$ and $B$ be square matrices of the same size $n\times n$. It is
generally true that

$$
\ker(B)\subseteq\ker(AB).
$$

#### (a)

##### (i)

Prove this result by completing the following argument:

If $v\in\ker(B)$ and so $Bv={\phantom{0}}$, then
$ABv={\phantom{0}}$ and hence $v\in\ker(AB)$.

##### (ii)

Complete the following statement. Some interesting special cases to consider
are when $A=O$ (the zero matrix) and $A=I$ (the identity matrix). In these
cases, $\ker(OB)={\phantom{\mathbb R^n}}$ and
$\ker(IB)={\phantom{\ker(B)}}$.

#### (b)

Choose all the correct statements, which are true for all $A$ and $B$:

- $\operatorname{nullity}(B)=\operatorname{nullity}(AB)$;
- $\operatorname{rank}(B)=\operatorname{rank}(AB)$;
- $\operatorname{rank}(B)\ge\operatorname{rank}(AB)$;
- $\operatorname{rank}(B)\le\operatorname{rank}(AB)$;
- $\operatorname{nullity}(B)\le\operatorname{nullity}(AB)$;
- $\operatorname{nullity}(B)\ge\operatorname{nullity}(AB)$.

#### (c)

Let $A$ be a square matrix and let

$$
r_k=\operatorname{rank}(A^k),
\qquad k=1,2,3,\ldots.
$$

Choose all the correct statements, which are true for all square matrices:

- The sequence $\{r_k\}$ is non-decreasing.
- The sequence $\{r_k\}$ is constant.
- The sequence $\{r_k\}$ is non-increasing.
- The sequence $\{r_k\}$ is bounded.
- The sequence $\{r_k\}$ is unbounded.
- $\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}r_k$ exists.
- $\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}r_k$ does not exist.

#### (d)

The following matrix computation and output is available:

```text
>> A = [5, 7, -6; 10, 12, -11; 20, 24, -22]

A =

     5     7    -6
    10    12   -11
    20    24   -22

>> A^2

ans =

   -25   -25    25
   -50   -50    50
  -100  -100   100

>> A^3 + 5*A^2

ans =

     0     0     0
     0     0     0
     0     0     0
```

Find the smallest integer $N$ such that

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A^k)=\operatorname{rank}(A^{k+1})
\qquad\text{for all }k\ge N.
$$

Justify the answer using the displayed computation.

#### Solution

The kernel inclusion in part (a) gives a nullity inequality. Because $B$ and
$AB$ have the same number of columns, rank-nullity turns it into a rank
inequality, which part (c) applies to consecutive powers of $A$.

#### (a) Kernel inclusion and the special cases

By definition,

$$
v\in\ker(B)\Longrightarrow Bv=0.
$$

Therefore

$$
ABv=A(Bv)=A0=0.
$$

This is exactly the condition $v\in\ker(AB)$. The completed argument is

$$
{Bv=0},
\qquad
{ABv=0}.
$$

If $A=O$, then $OB=O$, so every vector in $\mathbb R^n$ is sent to zero:

$$
{\ker(OB)=\mathbb R^n}.
$$

If $A=I$, then $IB=B$, so

$$
{\ker(IB)=\ker(B)}.
$$

The identity case shows that the inclusion can be an equality, while the
zero-matrix case shows that $\ker(AB)$ can be much larger.

#### (b) Comparing rank and nullity

The inclusion from part (a) implies

$$
\dim\ker(B)\le\dim\ker(AB),
$$

so

$$
{\operatorname{nullity}(B)
\le\operatorname{nullity}(AB)}.
$$

Both $B$ and $AB$ are $n\times n$, so rank-nullity gives

$$
\operatorname{rank}(B)
=n-\operatorname{nullity}(B),
\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(AB)
=n-\operatorname{nullity}(AB).
$$

Reversing the inequality after subtracting from $n$ gives

$$
{\operatorname{rank}(B)\ge\operatorname{rank}(AB)}.
$$

Thus the correct selections in part (b) are exactly

$$
{
\operatorname{rank}(B)\ge\operatorname{rank}(AB),
\qquad
\operatorname{nullity}(B)\le\operatorname{nullity}(AB).
}
$$

The equality statements are not always true: taking $A=O$ gives $AB=O$, so
both inequalities are strict whenever $B$ is non-zero.

#### (c) The rank sequence

Apply the rank inequality from part (b) with the left factor $A$ and the
right factor $A^k$:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A^{k+1})
=\operatorname{rank}(AA^k)
\le\operatorname{rank}(A^k).
$$

Therefore $\{r_k\}$ is non-increasing. Since an $n\times n$ matrix has rank
between $0$ and $n$,

$$
0\le r_k\le n
\qquad\text{for every }k,
$$

so the sequence is bounded. A non-increasing sequence of integers bounded
below must eventually be constant and therefore its limit exists.

The sequence need not be constant from the start. For example, a non-zero
nilpotent matrix can have $\operatorname{rank}(A)>\operatorname{rank}(A^2)$.
The correct selections are

$$
{
\text{the sequence is non-increasing, it is bounded and }
\lim_{k\to\infty}r_k\text{ exists}.
}
$$

#### (d) Finding the smallest stabilisation index

First determine $\operatorname{rank}(A)$ explicitly. Row operations give

$$
\begin{aligned}
A
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
5&7&-6\\
10&12&-11\\
20&24&-22
\end{pmatrix}\\
&\xrightarrow{\substack{R_2\leftarrow R_2-2R_1\\
R_3\leftarrow R_3-4R_1}}
\begin{pmatrix}
5&7&-6\\
0&-2&1\\
0&-4&2
\end{pmatrix}
\xrightarrow{R_3\leftarrow R_3-2R_2}
\begin{pmatrix}
5&7&-6\\
0&-2&1\\
0&0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

There are two pivots, so

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A)=2.
$$

The displayed value of $A^2$ is non-zero and its rows are multiples of the
first row:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
-25&-25&25\\
-50&-50&50\\
-100&-100&100
\end{pmatrix}
\sim
\begin{pmatrix}
-25&-25&25\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A^2)=1.
$$

The second MATLAB output says

$$
A^3+5A^2=O,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
A^3=-5A^2.
$$

Multiplying by $A^{k-2}$ for any $k\ge2$ gives

$$
A^{k+1}=A^{k-2}A^3
=-5A^{k-2}A^2
=-5A^k.
$$

Because multiplying a matrix by the non-zero scalar $-5$ does not change its
rank,

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A^{k+1})
=\operatorname{rank}(A^k)
\qquad(k\ge2).
$$

However,

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A)=2\ne1=\operatorname{rank}(A^2),
$$

so the equality does not hold at $k=1$. Therefore the smallest possible
integer is

$$
{N=2}.
$$

</Example>

### Ranks of products involving a matrix and its transpose

Wenbo is given the matrix

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1&2&12\\
-1&1&-2&-8\\
-1&-1&-8&-2\\
-5&-4&-37&-13
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Jess has found the eigenvalues of $A$ to be approximately

$$
0,\qquad -1.72981886,\qquad -18.03929570,\qquad 0.76911457,
$$

where any integer eigenvalues are exact, not approximations.

The eigenvalue list is supplied as data; the rank calculation below is
verified directly from $A$ by row reduction.

Calculate:

(a) $\operatorname{rank}(A^{\mathsf T}A)$;

(b) $\operatorname{rank}(AA^{\mathsf T})$.

(c) If $B$ is another $4\times4$ matrix with $\operatorname{rank}(B)=1$,
what are the possible values of $\operatorname{rank}(BA^{\mathsf T})$?

#### Solution

Find $\operatorname{rank}(A)$ first, then compare kernels. For any real
matrix, the quadratic forms
$x^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Ax$ and $y^{\mathsf T}AA^{\mathsf T}y$ are squared
Euclidean lengths, so the two products have the same kernels as $A$ and
$A^{\mathsf T}$. Rank-nullity then gives their ranks.

Row reduction gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
A
&=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1&2&12\\
-1&1&-2&-8\\
-1&-1&-8&-2\\
-5&-4&-37&-13
\end{pmatrix}\\
&\xrightarrow[\ R_3\leftarrow R_3+R_1\ ]{
\substack{R_2\leftarrow R_2+R_1\\R_4\leftarrow R_4+5R_1}}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1&2&12\\
0&0&0&4\\
0&-2&-6&10\\
0&-9&-27&47
\end{pmatrix}\\
&\xrightarrow{R_2\leftrightarrow R_3}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1&2&12\\
0&-2&-6&10\\
0&0&0&4\\
0&-9&-27&47
\end{pmatrix}\\
&\xrightarrow{R_2\leftarrow-\frac12R_2}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1&2&12\\
0&1&3&-5\\
0&0&0&4\\
0&-9&-27&47
\end{pmatrix}\\
&\xrightarrow{R_4\leftarrow R_4+9R_2}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1&2&12\\
0&1&3&-5\\
0&0&0&4\\
0&0&0&2
\end{pmatrix}\\
&\xrightarrow{\substack{R_3\leftarrow R_3-2R_4\\R_4\leftarrow\frac12R_4}}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1&2&12\\
0&1&3&-5\\
0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&1
\end{pmatrix}
\xrightarrow{R_3\leftrightarrow R_4}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1&2&12\\
0&1&3&-5\\
0&0&0&1\\
0&0&0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

There are pivots in columns $1$, $2$ and $4$, so

$$
\operatorname{rank}(A)=3.
$$

The exact zero eigenvalue agrees with this singularity, but it is the row
reduction that shows the nullity is exactly one; the rounded eigenvalues
alone would not.

For part (a), if $x\in\ker(A)$ then $A^{\mathsf T}Ax=0$. Conversely, if
$A^{\mathsf T}Ax=0$, then

$$
0=x^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Ax
=(Ax)^{\mathsf T}(Ax)
=\|Ax\|^2,
$$

so $Ax=0$. Hence

$$
\ker(A^{\mathsf T}A)=\ker(A).
$$

Both matrices have four columns. Since $\operatorname{nullity}(A)=4-3=1$,
rank-nullity gives

$$
{\operatorname{rank}(A^{\mathsf T}A)=4-1=3}.
$$

Similarly,

$$
\ker(AA^{\mathsf T})=\ker(A^{\mathsf T}),
$$

because

$$
y^{\mathsf T}AA^{\mathsf T}y
=(A^{\mathsf T}y)^{\mathsf T}(A^{\mathsf T}y)
=\|A^{\mathsf T}y\|^2.
$$

Transposition preserves rank, so $\operatorname{rank}(A^{\mathsf T})=3$ and
$\operatorname{nullity}(A^{\mathsf T})=1$. Therefore

$$
{\operatorname{rank}(AA^{\mathsf T})=4-1=3}.
$$

For part (c), write a rank-one matrix as

$$
B=uv^{\mathsf T},
\qquad u\ne0,\quad v\ne0.
$$

Then

$$
BA^{\mathsf T}
=uv^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}
=u(Av)^{\mathsf T}.
$$

This product has rank at most $1$. It has rank $0$ when $Av=0$. Such a
non-zero $v$ exists because $\operatorname{nullity}(A)=1$. It has rank $1$
when $Av\ne0$, which can be achieved by choosing $v\notin\ker(A)$.
Consequently, the possible values are

$$
{\operatorname{rank}(BA^{\mathsf T})\in\{0,1\}.}
$$

The kernel identities give the product ranks without multiplying the
matrices and the rank-one factorisation $B=uv^{\mathsf T}$ reduces part (c)
to whether $Av$ is zero.

</Example>

### Possible nullities of a linear map

Let

$$
T:M_{9\times7}(\mathbb R)\longrightarrow P_{25}(\mathbb R)
$$

be a linear map. Suppose the kernel of $T$ has dimension $k$. Then:

(a) the maximum possible value of $k$ is ___;

(b) the minimum possible value of $k$ is ___; and

(c) $T$ is surjective if and only if $k=$ ___.

#### Solution

The domain consists of $9\times7$ matrices, so one basis is given by the
matrices with a single $1$ and all other entries $0$. Hence

$$
\dim\bigl(M_{9\times7}(\mathbb R)\bigr)=9\cdot7=63.
$$

The space $P_{25}(\mathbb R)$ has basis
$\{1,x,\ldots,x^{25}\}$, so

$$
\dim\bigl(P_{25}(\mathbb R)\bigr)=26.
$$

Let $r=\operatorname{rank}(T)$. Rank-nullity gives

$$
r+k=\dim\bigl(M_{9\times7}(\mathbb R)\bigr)=63,
$$

so

$$
k=63-r.
$$

The rank is at least $0$ and cannot exceed the dimension of the codomain:

$$
0\le r\le26.
$$

Therefore

$$
37=63-26\le k\le63=63-0.
$$

The upper bound is attained by the zero map, whose kernel is the whole
63-dimensional domain. The lower bound is attainable because a
63-dimensional domain can map onto the 26-dimensional codomain by sending
26 domain basis vectors to a basis of $P_{25}(\mathbb R)$ and the remaining
37 basis vectors to zero. Thus

$$
{\text{(a) the maximum possible value of $k$ is }63}
$$

and

$$
{\text{(b) the minimum possible value of $k$ is }37}.
$$

Finally, $T$ is surjective exactly when its image is the whole codomain.
This is equivalent to

$$
\operatorname{rank}(T)=\dim\bigl(P_{25}(\mathbb R)\bigr)=26.
$$

Using $k=63-r$,

$$
T\text{ is surjective}
\Longleftrightarrow r=26
\Longleftrightarrow k=63-26=37.
$$

Hence

$$
{\text{(c) $T$ is surjective if and only if $k=37$.}}
$$

</Example>

For object 3, denote the matrix by $A_3$. Row reduction gives

}
$$

Different objects need different tests: row reduction counts pivots and
free variables, coefficient comparison gives polynomial dimensions and
eigenvalue statements require a square map.

</Example>

### Consequences of prescribed kernel and image vectors

Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces and let

$$
T:V\to W
$$

be a linear transformation. Let $v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4$ be vectors in $V$ and let $p,q$ be vectors in $W$.

It is given that $v_1,v_2$ are linearly independent and also $p,q$ are linearly independent. It is also given that

$$
T(v_1)=0,\qquad
T(v_2)=0,\qquad
T(v_3)=p,\qquad
T(v_4)=q.
$$

#### (a)

Select the statements which are definitely true. If it is not possible to be certain whether a statement is true or false, do not select it.

- $\ker(T)$ is a subspace of $V$.
- $\ker(T)$ is a subspace of $W$.
- $\operatorname{nullity}(T)\le2$.
- $\operatorname{nullity}(T)=2$.
- $\operatorname{nullity}(T)\ge2$.
- $-8v_2+5v_3$ is not an element of $\ker(T)$.

#### (b)

Select the statements which are definitely true. If it is not possible to be certain whether a statement is true or false, do not select it.

- $\operatorname{im}(T)$ is a subspace of $V$.
- $\operatorname{im}(T)$ is a subspace of $W$.
- $\operatorname{rank}(T)\le2$.
- $\operatorname{rank}(T)=2$.
- $\operatorname{rank}(T)\ge2$.
- $4p+3q$ is not an element of $\operatorname{im}(T)$.

#### (c)

Now suppose that you are told that the vector space $V$ is in fact $\mathbb R^5$. Select the correct statement from the following list, then give a brief explanation:

- $\operatorname{rank}(T)=3$ and $\operatorname{nullity}(T)\ne3$.
- $\operatorname{nullity}(T)=5$ and $\operatorname{rank}(T)=5$.
- Either $\operatorname{nullity}(T)=3$ or $\operatorname{rank}(T)=3$, but we do not have enough information to tell which.
- $\operatorname{nullity}(T)=3$ and $\operatorname{rank}(T)=3$.
- $\operatorname{nullity}(T)=3$ and $\operatorname{rank}(T)\ne3$.
- $\operatorname{nullity}(T)=2$ and $\operatorname{rank}(T)=2$.

#### (d)

By selecting a statement from each list, complete a carefully explained proof that $\{v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4\}$ is a linearly independent set.

#### Solution

Write

$$
K=\ker(T)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
I=\operatorname{im}(T).
$$

For (a), the kernel is a subspace of the domain $V$, so the first statement is definitely true and the second is false.

Since $T(v_1)=T(v_2)=0$, both $v_1$ and $v_2$ belong to $K$. Being linearly independent, they give $K$ at least two independent directions, so

$$
\operatorname{nullity}(T)=\dim K\ge2.
$$

There is no information ruling out further independent vectors in $K$, so neither $\operatorname{nullity}(T)\le2$ nor $\operatorname{nullity}(T)=2$ is forced.

Finally,

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(-8v_2+5v_3)
&=-8T(v_2)+5T(v_3)\\
&=-8(0)+5p\\
&=5p.
\end{aligned}
$$

The independence of $p,q$ implies $p\ne0$, so $5p\ne0$. Hence
$-8v_2+5v_3\notin\ker(T)$. The definitely true choices in (a) are

$$
{\ker(T)\text{ is a subspace of }V,\qquad
\operatorname{nullity}(T)\ge2,\qquad
-8v_2+5v_3\notin\ker(T).}
$$

For (b), the image is a subspace of the codomain $W$, not necessarily a subspace of $V$. Because

$$
T(v_3)=p,\qquad T(v_4)=q,
$$

both $p$ and $q$ belong to $I$. Their independence gives

$$
\operatorname{rank}(T)=\dim I\ge2.
$$

There may be further independent vectors in the image, so rank at most $2$ and rank equal to $2$ are not forced. Also,

$$
4p+3q
=4T(v_3)+3T(v_4)
=T(4v_3+3v_4),
$$

so $4p+3q\in\operatorname{im}(T)$. Therefore the definitely true choices in (b) are

$$
{
\operatorname{im}(T)\text{ is a subspace of }W,
\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(T)\ge2.
}
$$

For (c), the rank-nullity theorem applies to the domain $V=\mathbb R^5$:

$$
\operatorname{rank}(T)+\operatorname{nullity}(T)=5.
$$

The conclusions from (a) and (b) give

$$
\operatorname{nullity}(T)\ge2,
\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(T)\ge2.
$$

Thus

$$
2\le\operatorname{rank}(T)\le3,
\qquad
2\le\operatorname{nullity}(T)\le3.
$$

The only possible pairs are

$$
(\operatorname{rank}(T),\operatorname{nullity}(T))=(2,3)
\quad\text{or}\quad
(3,2).
$$

The given information does not determine whether an additional independent image direction occurs. Therefore the correct listed statement is

$$
{
\text{either }\operatorname{nullity}(T)=3\text{ or }\operatorname{rank}(T)=3,
\text{ but there is not enough information to tell which}.
}
$$

For (d), suppose that scalars $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3,\lambda_4$ satisfy

$$
\lambda_1v_1+\lambda_2v_2+\lambda_3v_3+\lambda_4v_4=0.
$$

Apply $T$ to both sides. Since $T$ is linear and $T(0)=0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
0
&=T\left(\lambda_1v_1+\lambda_2v_2+\lambda_3v_3+\lambda_4v_4\right)\\
&=\lambda_1T(v_1)+\lambda_2T(v_2)
+\lambda_3T(v_3)+\lambda_4T(v_4)\\
&=\lambda_3p+\lambda_4q.
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $p,q$ are linearly independent, the last equation implies

$$
\lambda_3=\lambda_4=0.
$$

Substituting these values into the original relation gives

$$
\lambda_1v_1+\lambda_2v_2=0.
$$

Since $v_1,v_2$ are linearly independent,

$$
\lambda_1=\lambda_2=0.
$$

Therefore

$$
\lambda_1=\lambda_2=\lambda_3=\lambda_4=0,
$$

which is precisely the homogeneous criterion for
$\{v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4\}$ to be linearly independent.

</Example>
