This page sets out the rules that addition and scalar multiplication must
obey for linear combinations to make sense. It then gives the standard
examples and the first facts that follow from the rules.

## The operations that define a vector space

<Recall of="field" />

Vectors in $\mathbb R^n$, matrices in $M_{m\times n}(F)$ and polynomials in
$P_n(F)$ can all be added and scaled. A vector space asks only that these
operations stay inside the set and obey the same rules.

<Definition slug="vector-space">

### Vector space

<Statement>

A *vector space* $V$ over a field $F$ is a non-empty set of vectors
equipped with an addition rule $V\times V\to V$ and a scalar-multiplication
rule $F\times V\to V$ satisfying:

1. **Closure under addition:** if $u,v\in V$, then $u+v\in V$.
2. **Associativity of addition:** $(u+v)+w=u+(v+w)$.
3. **Commutativity of addition:** $u+v=v+u$.
4. **Zero vector:** there is a $0\in V$ such that $0+u=u+0=u$.
5. **Additive inverses:** for every $u\in V$, there is a $-u\in V$ such that $u+(-u)=(-u)+u=0$.
6. **Closure under scalar multiplication:** if $u\in V$ and $\lambda\in F$, then $\lambda u\in V$.
7. **Associativity of scalar multiplication:** $\lambda(\mu u)=(\lambda\mu)u$.
8. **Multiplication by one:** $1u=u$.
9. **Scalar distributivity:** $(\lambda+\mu)u=\lambda u+\mu u$.
10. **Vector distributivity:** $\lambda(u+v)=\lambda u+\lambda v$.

</Statement>

The field supplies the scalar arithmetic; $V$ supplies the vectors and the
two operations. The axioms make an expression such as
$\lambda_1v_1+\cdots+\lambda_nv_n$ independent of how the additions are
bracketed or ordered.

</Definition>

The scalar field is part of the definition. $\mathbb C$ is a vector space
over $\mathbb C$ and also over $\mathbb R$ if only real scalars are allowed.
Over $\mathbb R$, no real multiple of $1$ equals $i$. This changes the basis
count: $\{1\}$ is a basis over $\mathbb C$, whereas $\{1,i\}$ is a basis over
$\mathbb R$.

## Familiar spaces and a first counterexample

<Recall of="complex-number" />

<Example slug="standard-vector-spaces" of="vector-space" difficulty="routine">

### Standard vector spaces

The most familiar example is $\mathbb R^n$ over $\mathbb R$. For
$$
u=\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\\vdots\\x_n\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v=\begin{pmatrix}y_1\\y_2\\\vdots\\y_n\end{pmatrix},
$$
addition is componentwise:
$$
u+v=
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1+y_1\\x_2+y_2\\\vdots\\x_n+y_n
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The zero vector has every entry equal to zero.

Other examples are:

- $\mathbb C^n$ over $\mathbb C$, or over $\mathbb R$ when scalar
  multiplication is restricted to real scalars;
- $M_{m\times n}(F)$, the set of $m\times n$ matrices with entries in $F$, over $F$;
- $P_n(F)$, the polynomials of degree at most $n$ with coefficients in $F$, over $F$;
- $P(F)$, the set of all polynomials with coefficients in $F$, over $F$.

A polynomial in $P_n(F)$ is determined by its coefficient list
$(a_0,\ldots,a_n)$, which gives $\dim P_n(F)=n+1$ once
[dimension](/algebra/vector-spaces/basis-and-dimension) is defined. An
unqualified $P_n$ means $P_n(\mathbb R)$ unless a lesson states otherwise.
This space notation differs from the calculus notation $P_n(x)$ for a
[Taylor polynomial](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-polynomials).

To show that these are vector spaces, verify the ten axioms. For matrices, the zero vector is the zero matrix and additive inverses are obtained by negating each entry. For $P_n(F)$, the zero vector is the zero polynomial. If
$$
p(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_kz^k\in P_n(F),
$$
then
$$
\lambda p(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\lambda a_k)z^k\in P_n(F),
$$
so scalar multiplication stays inside the polynomial space.

</Example>

One failed axiom is enough to show that a set is not a vector space under
the given operations.

<Example slug="non-vector-space-first-quadrant" of="vector-space" difficulty="routine">

### A set that is not a vector space

Consider
$$
S=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:x\geq0,\ y\geq0\}
$$
with the usual addition and scalar multiplication. The element $(1,1)$ belongs to $S$, but
$$
-1(1,1)=(-1,-1)\notin S.
$$
Thus $S$ is not closed under scalar multiplication and lacks the additive inverse of $(1,1)$.

</Example>

## Consequences of the axioms

The axioms imply a few facts that later pages use without further proof.

<Proposition slug="vector-space-additive-properties" of="vector-space">

### Properties of addition

<Statement>

In a vector space, the zero vector is unique, cancellation holds,
$$
u+v=u+w\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v=w,
$$
and each vector has a unique additive inverse.

</Statement>

</Proposition>

<Proof of="vector-space-additive-properties">

If $0_1$ and $0_2$ are zero vectors, then
$$
0_1=0_1+0_2=0_2.
$$
If $u+v=u+w$, add $-u$ to both sides and use associativity and the zero-vector property to obtain $v=w$. If $u+v=0=u+w$, cancellation gives $v=w$, so the additive inverse is unique.

</Proof>

<Proposition slug="vector-space-scalar-properties" of="vector-space">

### Properties of scalar multiplication

<Statement>

For every vector $u$ and scalars $\lambda,\mu$,
$$
0u=0,\qquad \lambda0=0,\qquad (-1)u=-u,
$$
$$
\lambda u=0\Longrightarrow \lambda=0\text{ or }u=0,
$$
and, if $u\neq0$,
$$
\lambda u=\mu u\Longrightarrow \lambda=\mu.
$$

</Statement>

</Proposition>

<Proof of="vector-space-scalar-properties">

Because $0+0=0$, distributivity gives
$$
0u=(0+0)u=0u+0u,
$$
and cancellation gives $0u=0$. Similarly,
$$
\lambda0=\lambda(0+0)=\lambda0+\lambda0,
$$
so $\lambda0=0$. Also,
$$
(-1)u+u=(({-1})+1)u=0u=0,
$$
so $(-1)u=-u$. If $\lambda u=0$ and $\lambda\neq0$, multiply by the field inverse $\lambda^{-1}$ to obtain $u=0$. Finally, if $\lambda u=\mu u$ and $u\neq0$, then
$$
(\lambda-\mu)u=0,
$$
so $\lambda-\mu=0$ and hence $\lambda=\mu$.

</Proof>

[Subspaces](/algebra/vector-spaces/subspaces) gives the test for whether a
subset inherits these operations. Later, the kernel and image of a [linear
transformation](/algebra/linear-transformations/linear-transformations), a
map that preserves both operations, give important examples.
