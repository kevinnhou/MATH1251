This page builds bases from given vectors and then uses a basis to turn each vector into a column of coordinates. Coordinates let calculations in any finite-dimensional space be done with columns in $F^n$.

## Constructing a basis from data

<Recall of="basis" />

<Recall of="dimension" />

A basis can be built from given vectors in two directions, both using pivot
columns. A spanning set is cut down to a maximal independent subset and an
independent set is extended by standard basis vectors until it spans.

If $S$ is a finite non-empty subset of a vector space, then $S$ contains a subset which is a basis for $\operatorname{span}(S)$. In particular, a non-zero vector space spanned by a finite set has a basis.

<Method slug="spanning-set-to-basis" of="basis,span,homogeneous-independence-test">

### Reduce a spanning set to a basis

<Statement>

For a finite set $S=\{u_1,\ldots,u_n\}\subseteq\mathbb R^m$:

1. Form the matrix $A=[\,u_1\ \cdots\ u_n\,]$.
2. Row reduce $A$ to echelon form.
3. Keep the original vectors whose columns correspond to pivot columns of
   the echelon form.

The remaining original vectors form a basis for $\operatorname{span}(S)$.

</Statement>

Row operations identify which columns are redundant, but the basis must use the corresponding columns of the original matrix. Removing the redundant vectors preserves the [span](/algebra/vector-spaces/spans).

</Method>

<Example slug="reduce-eight-vectors" of="spanning-set-to-basis" difficulty="routine">

### Eight vectors in $\mathbb R^5$

Suppose the row-reduced matrix has columns corresponding to eight original vectors:
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1&2&3&4&5&7&1&5\\
0&0&0&1&6&8&2&6\\
0&0&0&0&1&9&3&7\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&1&8\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The leading columns are $1,4,5,7$. Retaining the four original vectors in those positions gives a basis for the span of the eight vectors, so that span has dimension $4$.

</Example>

If $V$ is spanned by a finite set and $S$ is an independent subset of $V$, then there exists a basis for $V$ containing $S$. In other words, every independent subset can be extended to a basis.
To build it, keep adjoining a vector outside the current span until the set
spans $V$. Each addition preserves independence and the dimension of $V$
bounds the size of any independent set, so the process stops after finitely
many steps.

<Method slug="extend-independent-set-to-basis" of="basis,dimension,independent-adjoining">

### Extend an independent set to a basis

<Statement>

For an independent set $S=\{u_1,\ldots,u_n\}\subseteq\mathbb R^m$:

1. Form the matrix with columns
$$
[\,u_1\ \cdots\ u_n\ e_1\ \cdots\ e_m\,],
$$
where $e_1,\ldots,e_m$ are the standard basis vectors.
2. Row reduce this matrix to echelon form.
3. Keep the original columns corresponding to all pivot columns.

The resulting vectors form a basis for $\mathbb R^m$ containing $S$.

</Statement>

The standard basis supplies candidate vectors outside the current span. The
pivot columns keep the given independent vectors and add only the candidates
needed to reach dimension $m$. As before, take the columns from the original
matrix: row reduction changes the vectors but not the pivot positions.

</Method>

<Example slug="extend-two-vectors-in-r3" of="extend-independent-set-to-basis" difficulty="routine">

### Extending a set in $\mathbb R^3$

Let
$$
S=\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\4\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\5\end{pmatrix}
\right\}.
$$
Writing $u_1,u_2,u_3$ for these vectors, the first two are independent and
$$
u_3=2u_2-u_1,
$$
so $\{u_1,u_2\}$ is a basis for $\operatorname{span}(S)$. Following the
construction, adjoin the standard basis columns:
$$
\left[
u_1\ u_2\ e_1\ e_2\ e_3
\right]
=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1&1&0&0\\
2&2&0&1&0\\
3&4&0&0&1
\end{pmatrix}
\longrightarrow
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0&2&-1\\
0&1&0&-\frac32&1\\
0&0&1&-\frac12&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The leading columns are $1,2,3$. Keeping the corresponding original columns
gives the basis
$$
\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\4\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
\right\}
$$
for $\mathbb R^3$.

</Example>

## Coordinates record the unique coefficients

Once an ordered basis is fixed, the unique coefficients of a vector form a
column of scalars.

<Definition slug="coordinate-vector" of="basis,independence-unique-representation">

### Coordinate vector

<Statement>

Let $B=(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$ be an ordered basis for a vector space $V$. If
$$
u=x_1u_1+\cdots+x_nu_n,
$$
then the *coordinate vector* of $u$ with respect to $B$ is the column
$$
[u]_B=
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1\\
\vdots\\
x_n
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

</Statement>

The order of $B$ fixes the order of the entries. Changing the order of the basis changes the coordinate vector even though it does not change $u$.

</Definition>

<Method slug="compute-coordinate-vector" of="coordinate-vector">

### Compute a coordinate vector

<Statement>

To find $[u]_B$ for an ordered basis $B=(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$:

1. Solve
$$
u=x_1u_1+\cdots+x_nu_n
$$
for the coefficients.
2. Place the coefficients in the order determined by $B$:
$$
[u]_B=
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1\\
\vdots\\
x_n
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

</Statement>

Step 1 is a [span](/algebra/vector-spaces/spans) membership problem and its solution is unique because $B$ is independent.

</Method>

<Example slug="standard-polynomial-coordinates" of="coordinate-vector,compute-coordinate-vector" difficulty="routine">

### Coordinates in the standard polynomial basis

For the ordered basis
$$
B=(1,x,x^2,\ldots,x^n)
$$
of $P_n(F)$ over the chosen scalar field $F$, the polynomial
$$
p=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_nx^n,
\qquad a_0,\ldots,a_n\in F,
$$
has coordinate vector
$$
[p]_B=
\begin{pmatrix}
a_0\\
a_1\\
\vdots\\
a_n
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="polynomial-coordinate-calculation" of="compute-coordinate-vector" difficulty="routine">

### Coordinates in a non-standard polynomial basis

Let
$$
B=(1+x,\ x+x^2,\ 1+x^2).
$$
First check that these polynomials form a basis. If
$\lambda_1(1+x)+\lambda_2(x+x^2)+\lambda_3(1+x^2)=0$,
equating the constant, linear and quadratic coefficients gives
$$
\lambda_1+\lambda_3=0,\qquad
\lambda_1+\lambda_2=0,\qquad
\lambda_2+\lambda_3=0.
$$
These equations force all three coefficients to be zero. Thus $B$ is an
independent set of three vectors in the three-dimensional space
$P_2(\mathbb R)$,
so it is a basis. To find the coordinates of $x^2+x+1$, solve
$$
x^2+x+1
=\lambda_1(1+x)+\lambda_2(x+x^2)+\lambda_3(1+x^2).
$$
Equating coefficients gives
$$
\lambda_1+\lambda_3=1,\qquad
\lambda_1+\lambda_2=1,\qquad
\lambda_2+\lambda_3=1.
$$
Thus
$$
\lambda_1=\lambda_2=\lambda_3=\frac12,
$$
and
$$
[x^2+x+1]_B=
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac12\\[2pt]
\frac12\\[2pt]
\frac12
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

</Example>

## Orthonormal coordinates

When the basis vectors in $\mathbb R^m$ are orthonormal, dot products give
the coordinates directly, with no system to solve.

<Definition slug="orthonormal-set">

### Orthonormal set

<Statement>

A set $S=\{u_1,\ldots,u_n\}$ in $\mathbb R^m$ is *orthonormal* if
$$
u_i\cdot u_j=
\begin{cases}
1,&i=j,\\
0,&i\ne j.
\end{cases}
$$

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="orthonormal-set-independent" of="orthonormal-set,linear-independence">

### Orthonormal sets are independent

<Statement>

Every orthonormal set is linearly independent.

</Statement>

If $\lambda_1u_1+\cdots+\lambda_nu_n=0$, take the dot product with $u_k$. Orthonormality leaves $\lambda_k=0$. This holds for every $k$.

</Theorem>

<Definition slug="orthonormal-basis" of="basis,orthonormal-set">

### Orthonormal basis

<Statement>

An *orthonormal basis* of a subspace of $\mathbb R^m$ is a basis that is also
an orthonormal set.

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="orthonormal-coordinate-formula" of="orthonormal-basis,coordinate-vector">

### Coordinates in an orthonormal basis

<Statement>

If $B=(u_1,\ldots,u_n)$ is an ordered orthonormal basis of a subspace
$V\subseteq\mathbb R^m$ and $v\in V$, then
$$
[v]_B=
\begin{pmatrix}
u_1\cdot v\\
\vdots\\
u_n\cdot v
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

</Statement>

Each dot product extracts one coefficient because the other basis vectors are perpendicular to it.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="orthonormal-coordinate-formula">

Write $v=x_1u_1+\cdots+x_nu_n$. Taking the dot product with $u_k$ gives
$$
u_k\cdot v
=\sum_{j=1}^n x_j(u_k\cdot u_j)
=x_k.
$$
These are exactly the entries of $[v]_B$.

</Proof>

<Example slug="orthonormal-coordinates-in-r3" of="orthonormal-coordinate-formula,dimension-counting" difficulty="routine">

### An orthonormal basis of $\mathbb R^3$

The set
$$
B=\left(
\frac1{\sqrt3}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix},
\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix},
\frac1{\sqrt6}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}
\right)
$$
is orthonormal, hence independent. It has three vectors in $\mathbb R^3$, so it is a basis. For
$$
v=\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\\a_3\end{pmatrix},
$$
the orthonormal formula gives
$$
[v]_B=
\begin{pmatrix}
(a_1+a_2+a_3)/\sqrt3\\
(a_1-a_2)/\sqrt2\\
(a_1+a_2-2a_3)/\sqrt6
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

</Example>

## Coordinate arithmetic and matrix tests

<Theorem slug="coordinate-vector-operations" of="coordinate-vector,basis">

### Coordinates preserve vector-space operations

<Statement>

If $B$ is an ordered basis for $V$, $u,v\in V$ and $\lambda\in F$, then
$$
u=v\iff [u]_B=[v]_B,
\qquad
[u+v]_B=[u]_B+[v]_B,
\qquad
[\lambda u]_B=\lambda[u]_B.
$$

</Statement>

These identities follow by adding or scaling the coefficients in the unique representations.

</Theorem>

<Corollary slug="square-matrix-basis-criteria" of="basis,dimension-counting,homogeneous-independence-test">

### Square-matrix basis criteria

<Statement>

For a square matrix $A$ with columns $u_1,\ldots,u_n$, the following are equivalent:

- the columns are linearly independent;
- the columns span $\mathbb R^n$;
- the columns form a basis for $\mathbb R^n$.

</Statement>

For a square matrix, these basis criteria are equivalent to the familiar tests for invertibility.

</Corollary>

[Data fitting](/algebra/vector-spaces/data-fitting) uses coordinates in $P_n(\mathbb R)$ to express interpolation conditions.
Later, coordinates in the domain and codomain turn a
[linear transformation into a matrix](/algebra/linear-transformations/matrix-representations).
