The span of a set is everything its vectors produce by linear combination. This page shows that the span is the smallest subspace containing the set and turns membership of the span into a linear system.

## Combining chosen vectors

<Recall of="subspace" />

<Definition slug="linear-combination" of="vector-space">

### Linear combination

<Statement>

Let $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ be vectors in a vector space $V$ over $F$. A *linear combination* of these vectors is an expression
$$
\lambda_1v_1+\cdots+\lambda_nv_n,
\qquad \lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n\in F.
$$

</Statement>

The vectors are fixed while the scalars vary. For non-zero, non-parallel
$u,v\in\mathbb R^3$, the vectors
$$
\lambda_1u+\lambda_2v,\qquad \lambda_1,\lambda_2\in\mathbb R,
$$
form the plane through the origin determined by $u$ and $v$, with each
coefficient moving along one direction.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="linear-combination-closure" of="linear-combination,vector-space">

### Closure under linear combinations

<Statement>

Every linear combination of a finite set of vectors in a vector space $V$ is an element of $V$.

</Statement>

</Theorem>

<Proof of="linear-combination-closure">

For one vector, $\lambda_1v_1\in V$ by scalar closure. Suppose every combination of $k$ vectors lies in $V$. If
$$
w=\lambda_1v_1+\cdots+\lambda_kv_k\in V,
$$
then scalar closure gives $\lambda_{k+1}v_{k+1}\in V$ and addition closure gives
$$
w+\lambda_{k+1}v_{k+1}\in V.
$$
Induction proves the claim for every finite number of vectors.

</Proof>

## The smallest subspace containing the chosen vectors

<Definition slug="span" of="linear-combination">

### Span

<Statement>

For a subset $S$ (possibly infinite) in a vector space $V$ over $F$, the
*span* of $S$ is the set of all **finite** linear combinations of vectors in
$S$:
$$
\operatorname{span}(S)
=
\left\{
\lambda_1v_1+\cdots+\lambda_kv_k:
k\geq 0,\ v_1,\ldots,v_k\in S,\ \lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k\in F
\right\}.
$$
For a finite set $S=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$, this is the same as allowing one
coefficient for each listed vector. When $k=0$, the sum is the empty sum
$0$.

</Statement>

The span is the [subspace](/algebra/vector-spaces/subspaces) generated by the chosen vectors.
The empty-sum convention gives $\operatorname{span}(\varnothing)=\{0\}$,
which is needed for the zero vector space.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="span-is-subspace" of="span,subspace">

### The span is a subspace

<Statement>

If $S$ is a subset of a vector space $V$, then $\operatorname{span}(S)$ is a subspace of $V$. It is the smallest subspace of $V$ that contains $S$.

</Statement>

</Theorem>

<Proof of="span-is-subspace">

The empty combination gives $0\in\operatorname{span}(S)$. If $u$ and $w$
are finite linear combinations of vectors in $S$, concatenating their lists
of terms expresses $u+w$ as another finite linear combination. For
$\gamma\in F$, multiplying every coefficient in a finite combination for
$u$ by $\gamma$ expresses $\gamma u$ in the span. The [subspace test](/algebra/vector-spaces/subspaces) therefore applies.

If $W$ is any subspace containing $S$, then $W$ is closed under scalar multiplication and addition. It must therefore contain every linear combination of the vectors in $S$, so $\operatorname{span}(S)\subseteq W$. Hence the span is the smallest such subspace.

</Proof>

<Example slug="span-line-and-plane" of="span" difficulty="routine">

### Lines and planes through the origin

For $v=(1,0,0)\in\mathbb R^3$,
$$
\operatorname{span}\{v\}
=
\{(\lambda,0,0):\lambda\in\mathbb R\},
$$
which is the line $y=z=0$. The coefficient can be any real number, so the
whole line is obtained; restricting it to an interval would give a bounded
segment, which is not closed under scalar multiplication. Any plane through
the origin containing this line is a larger subspace containing $v$.

</Example>

## Spanning familiar spaces

The examples from here on are over $\mathbb R$, so $P_n$ means
$P_n(\mathbb R)$ and matrix entries are real.

<Definition slug="spanning-set" of="span">

### Spanning set

<Statement>

A subset $S$ in a vector space $V$ is a *spanning set* for $V$ when
$$
\operatorname{span}(S)=V.
$$

</Statement>

A spanning set may contain redundant vectors.

</Definition>

<Example slug="standard-spanning-sets" of="spanning-set" difficulty="routine">

### Standard spanning sets

The set
$$
\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
\right\}
$$
spans $\mathbb R^2$, since every $(a,b)^T$ is
$$
a\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
+b\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The set
$$
\{1,x,\ldots,x^n\}
$$
spans $P_n$, since each polynomial of degree at most $n$ is a linear combination of these powers. The four matrices
$$
E_{11}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
E_{12}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
E_{21}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
E_{22}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
$$
span $M_{2\times2}(\mathbb R)$, because
$$
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}
=aE_{11}+bE_{12}+cE_{21}+dE_{22}.
$$

</Example>

<Definition slug="column-space" of="span">

### Column space

<Statement>

If $S=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ is a set of vectors in $\mathbb R^m$ and $A=[\,v_1\ \cdots\ v_n\,]$, then the span of $S$ is the *column space* of $A$:
$$
\operatorname{col}(A)=\operatorname{span}(S)
=\{Ac:c\in\mathbb R^n\},
$$
where $c$ records the coefficients of the columns. Thus multiplying $A$ by
$c$ is exactly the linear combination represented by those coefficients.

</Statement>

</Definition>

## Membership as a linear system

Because $Ac$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$, asking whether a
vector lies in a span is asking whether a linear system has a solution.

<Method slug="span-membership" of="span" see="column-space">

### Test membership in a span

<Statement>

To decide whether $u\in\mathbb R^m$ lies in $\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$, form $A=[\,v_1\ \cdots\ v_n\,]$ and solve
$$
Ac=u.
$$
A solution for the scalar coefficients exists if and only if $u$ belongs to the span, equivalently to $\operatorname{col}(A)$.

</Statement>

Use row reduction on the augmented matrix $[A\mid u]$. A contradictory row means that $u$ is not in the span.

</Method>

<Example slug="span-membership-no-solution" of="span-membership" difficulty="routine">

### A vector outside a span

Let
$$
S=
\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\2\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}
\right\}.
$$
To test whether $(1,2,3)^T$ belongs to $\operatorname{span}(S)$, row-reduce
$$
\left[
\begin{array}{cc|c}
1&2&1\\
0&1&2\\
2&-1&3
\end{array}
\right]
\sim
\left[
\begin{array}{cc|c}
1&0&-3\\
0&1&2\\
0&0&11
\end{array}
\right].
$$
The last row is contradictory, so the system has no solution and
$$
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}\notin\operatorname{span}(S).
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="span-r3-membership" of="span-membership,spanning-set" difficulty="routine">

### Three vectors spanning $\mathbb R^3$

For
$$
A=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&1&2\\
2&0&1\\
3&2&-1
\end{bmatrix},
$$
row reduction gives
$$
A\sim
\begin{bmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Thus $A c=b$ has a solution for every $b\in\mathbb R^3$. The columns
$$
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}
$$
span $\mathbb R^3$.

</Example>

<Example slug="span-polynomial-membership" of="span-membership" difficulty="routine">

### Membership in a polynomial span

Let
$$
q_1=x^3+2x^2,\qquad q_2=x-2,
$$
and write
$$
p=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3.
$$
Then
$$
\alpha q_1+\beta q_2
=-2\beta+\beta x+2\alpha x^2+\alpha x^3.
$$
The constant and linear coefficients require $a_0=-2\beta$ and
$a_1=\beta$; the quadratic and cubic coefficients require $a_2=2\alpha$
and $a_3=\alpha$. Thus $p\in\operatorname{span}\{q_1,q_2\}$ exactly when
$$
a_0=-2a_1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
a_2=2a_3.
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="nonspanning-polynomial-set" of="span-membership,spanning-set" difficulty="routine">

### A set that does not span $P_2$

Consider
$$
q_1=x+2x^2,\qquad q_2=x-2,\qquad q_3=x^2+1.
$$
For
$$
p=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2,
$$
the equation
$$
\alpha q_1+\beta q_2+\gamma q_3=p
$$
gives
$$
-2\beta+\gamma=a_0,\qquad
\alpha+\beta=a_1,\qquad
2\alpha+\gamma=a_2.
$$
The first two equations give $\gamma=a_0+2\beta$ and
$\alpha=a_1-\beta$. Substituting into the third gives the condition
$$
a_2=a_0+2a_1.
$$
Conversely, when this condition holds, those choices of $\alpha$ and
$\gamma$ solve all three equations for any $\beta$.
The polynomial $p=x^2$ fails the condition, so these three polynomials do not span $P_2$.

</Example>

[Linear independence](/algebra/vector-spaces/linear-independence) asks when the coefficients in a span are unique.
