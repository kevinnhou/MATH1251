This page fits functions to data in two ways. A least-squares line approximates the data and polynomial interpolation fits it exactly by solving for coordinates in a polynomial basis.

## Approximation versus exact interpolation

<Recall of="coordinate-vector" />

<Recall of="basis" />

<Recall of="span" />

Data fitting starts with prescribed pairs $(t_i,y_i)$ and asks for a function whose values describe the data. The inputs $t_i$ may be times or another variable, the outputs $y_i$ may be measured quantities and the inputs need not be equally spaced.

An approximation uses a function that is close to the data, while interpolation requires an exact fit at every supplied input. Exact fitting is developed
below as [polynomial interpolation](#interpolation-in-a-standard-basis).
[Taylor polynomials](/calculus/taylor-series/taylor-polynomials) are a
different construction, matching derivative data at one centre.

## Least squares as a projection

Least squares is an [orthogonal projection](/algebra/linear-transformations/geometric-examples)
of the data vector onto a space of model values. Projection is developed
formally in the next chapter, so the normal equations below are derived
directly by minimising a function of the line's two coefficients.

<Method slug="least-squares-line-fit" see="polynomial-interpolation">

### Fit a straight line by least squares

<Statement>

For real data $(t_i,y_i)\in\mathbb R^2$, form
$$
M=
\begin{pmatrix}
t_1&1\\
t_2&1\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
t_n&1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
Y=
\begin{pmatrix}
y_1\\y_2\\\vdots\\y_n
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
If at least two input values are distinct, the least-squares straight-line
fit $y=mt+c$ has the unique coefficient vector
$$
\begin{pmatrix}m\\c\end{pmatrix}
=(M^{\mathsf T}M)^{-1}M^{\mathsf T}Y.
$$

</Statement>

For a candidate line $y=mt+c$, the vector of predicted values at the inputs
is
$$
M\begin{pmatrix}m\\c\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The columns of $M$ contain the sampled values of $t$ and $1$, so multiplying
them by $m$ and $c$ produces the line's predicted values. The line need not
pass through every data point.

The fitted value vector $M\binom{m}{c}$ lies in the [column space](/algebra/vector-spaces/spans)
$$
\operatorname{col}(M)=\operatorname{span}\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}t_1\\\vdots\\t_n\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}1\\\vdots\\1\end{pmatrix}
\right\}.
$$
Least squares chooses $m$ and $c$ to minimise the sum of squared vertical
errors
$$
E(m,c)=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-mt_i-c)^2
=\left\|Y-M\begin{pmatrix}m\\c\end{pmatrix}\right\|^2,
$$
where $\|\cdot\|$ is the usual Euclidean norm on $\mathbb R^n$. The problem
is therefore to find the point of $\operatorname{col}(M)$ closest to $Y$; the
formula in the statement is the coordinate calculation for that projection.

At a minimum, differentiating with respect to $m$ and $c$ gives
$$
\sum_{i=1}^n t_i(y_i-mt_i-c)=0,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-mt_i-c)=0.
$$
These are exactly the two entries of
$$
M^{\mathsf T}\left(Y-M\binom{m}{c}\right)=0,
\qquad
M^{\mathsf T}M\binom{m}{c}=M^{\mathsf T}Y.
$$
Thus the residual
$$
Y-M\binom{m}{c}
$$
is perpendicular to both columns of $M$, hence to their span. If $x_*$
solves the normal equations and $x$ is any other coefficient vector, this
perpendicularity gives
$$
\|Y-Mx\|^2=\|Y-Mx_*\|^2+\|M(x-x_*)\|^2.
$$
So $Mx_*$ is the closest model vector to $Y$.

When two input values are distinct, $at_i+b=0$ for every $i$ forces
$a=b=0$, so the columns of $M$ are independent. Hence $M^{\mathsf T}M$ is
invertible and the minimising coefficient vector is unique. Multiplying the
normal equations by $(M^{\mathsf T}M)^{-1}$ gives the coefficient formula in
the statement.

</Method>

<Example slug="least-squares-line-example" of="least-squares-line-fit" difficulty="routine">

### A least-squares line

For $(0,2)$, $(3,4)$ and $(4,4)$,
$$
M=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1\\
3&1\\
4&1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
Y=
\begin{pmatrix}
2\\4\\4
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Here
$$
M^{\mathsf T}M=
\begin{pmatrix}25&7\\7&3\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
M^{\mathsf T}Y=\binom{28}{10}.
$$
The determinant of $M^{\mathsf T}M$ is $26$ and solving the normal
equations gives
$$
\begin{pmatrix}m\\c\end{pmatrix}
=\frac1{26}
\begin{pmatrix}3&-7\\-7&25\end{pmatrix}
\binom{28}{10}
=\begin{pmatrix}7/13\\27/13\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus the best-fit line is
$$
y=\frac{7}{13}t+\frac{27}{13}.
$$
It does not pass through all three data points; it minimises their total
squared vertical error.

</Example>

## Interpolation in a standard basis

Exact fitting becomes a [coordinate](/algebra/vector-spaces/coordinates-and-bases) problem when the chosen functions form a [basis](/algebra/vector-spaces/basis-and-dimension) of a polynomial space.
The data are real, so $P_k$ means $P_k(\mathbb R)$ in this section.

<Definition slug="polynomial-interpolation" of="coordinate-vector,basis,span">

### Polynomial interpolation

<Statement>

Given $n$ distinct inputs $t_1,\ldots,t_n$ and prescribed outputs $y_1,\ldots,y_n$, *polynomial interpolation* finds the polynomial $f\in P_{n-1}$ satisfying
$$
f(t_i)=y_i,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.
$$
Using the standard basis
$$
B=(1,t,t^2,\ldots,t^{n-1}),
$$
write
$$
f(t)=a_0+a_1t+\cdots+a_{n-1}t^{n-1}.
$$
The coefficients form the coordinate vector
$$
[f]_B=
\begin{pmatrix}
a_0\\a_1\\\vdots\\a_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

</Statement>

The interpolation conditions become a linear system for this coordinate vector.

</Definition>

<Method slug="vandermonde-interpolation" of="polynomial-interpolation" see="lagrange-interpolation">

### Interpolate in the standard polynomial basis

<Statement>

To find the coordinate vector of the interpolating polynomial in $B=(1,t,\ldots,t^{n-1})$:

1. Form the Vandermonde matrix
$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&t_1&t_1^2&\cdots&t_1^{n-1}\\
1&t_2&t_2^2&\cdots&t_2^{n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
1&t_n&t_n^2&\cdots&t_n^{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}
$$
and the data vector $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)^T$.
2. Solve
$$
Ax=y.
$$
3. Read the solution
$$
x=
\begin{pmatrix}
a_0\\a_1\\\vdots\\a_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}
=[f]_B
$$
as the coordinates of the interpolating polynomial.

</Statement>

The $i$th row imposes the condition $f(t_i)=y_i$. Because the inputs are distinct, these conditions determine exactly one coordinate vector.

</Method>

<Theorem slug="distinct-inputs-unique-interpolation" of="vandermonde-interpolation,polynomial-interpolation">

### Distinct inputs give a unique interpolant

<Statement>

If $t_1,\ldots,t_n$ are distinct, the columns of the Vandermonde matrix are linearly independent and span $\mathbb R^n$. Hence $Ax=y$ has a unique solution for every data vector $y$, so there is exactly one polynomial in $P_{n-1}$ satisfying
$$
f(t_i)=y_i,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.
$$

</Statement>

</Theorem>

<Proof of="distinct-inputs-unique-interpolation">

Suppose a linear combination of the columns is zero. Its coefficients define
$$
p(t)=\lambda_1+\lambda_2t+\cdots+\lambda_nt^{n-1},
$$
and the zero combination says $p(t_i)=0$ for every $i$. By the factor
theorem, each distinct root contributes a factor $(t-t_i)$, so a non-zero
polynomial of degree at most $n-1$ cannot have all $n$ roots. Hence every
$\lambda_i$ is zero. The $n$ columns are independent in $\mathbb R^n$,
so they also span $\mathbb R^n$, giving a unique solution to $Ax=y$.

</Proof>

## Interpolation in a basis adapted to the data

The same interpolating polynomial has simpler coordinates in a basis whose
polynomials each pick out one data input.

<Definition slug="lagrange-polynomial" of="polynomial-interpolation">

### Lagrange polynomials

<Statement>

For distinct inputs $t_1,\ldots,t_n$, the $j$th Lagrange polynomial is
$$
\ell_j(t)=\prod_{\substack{k=1\\k\ne j}}^n
\frac{t-t_k}{t_j-t_k},
\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.
$$
It has degree at most $n-1$ and satisfies
$$
\ell_j(t_j)=1,
\qquad
\ell_j(t_k)=0\quad\text{when }k\ne j.
$$

</Statement>

Each factor $t-t_k$ makes $\ell_j$ zero at one other input, while dividing
by $t_j-t_k$ makes that factor equal to $1$ at $t_j$. Distinct inputs
ensure that no denominator is zero.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="lagrange-basis" of="lagrange-polynomial,basis">

### The Lagrange polynomials form a basis

<Statement>

The set
$$
L=(\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_n)
$$
is a basis for $P_{n-1}$.

</Statement>

</Theorem>

<Proof of="lagrange-basis">

If
$$
\lambda_1\ell_1(t)+\cdots+\lambda_n\ell_n(t)=0,
$$
evaluate at $t=t_k$. Every term vanishes except $\lambda_k\ell_k(t_k)=\lambda_k$, so $\lambda_k=0$. This holds for every $k$, so the Lagrange polynomials are linearly independent; with $n$ polynomials in $P_{n-1}$, they form a basis.

</Proof>

<Method slug="lagrange-interpolation" of="lagrange-basis,polynomial-interpolation" see="vandermonde-interpolation">

### Interpolate in the Lagrange basis

<Statement>

For data $(t_j,y_j)$, construct the Lagrange polynomials and set
$$
f(t)=\sum_{j=1}^n y_j\ell_j(t).
$$
Then $f(t_i)=y_i$ for every $i$, which gives the interpolating polynomial.

</Statement>

The data values are the coordinates in the Lagrange basis, while $a_0,\ldots,a_{n-1}$ are the coordinates of the same polynomial in the standard basis:
$$
[f]_L=
\begin{pmatrix}
y_1\\\vdots\\y_n
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
[f]_B=
\begin{pmatrix}
a_0\\\vdots\\a_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The coordinate vector changes with the basis; the polynomial $f$ does not.

</Method>

<Example slug="interpolation-root-example" of="lagrange-interpolation" difficulty="exam">

### Estimating a root from an interpolant

For
$$
g(x)=x-9^{-x},
$$
use the inputs $0,\frac12,1$. The data values are
$$
g(0)=-1,\qquad g\left(\frac12\right)=\frac16,\qquad g(1)=\frac89.
$$
The corresponding Lagrange polynomials are
$$
\ell_1(t)=2\left(t-\frac12\right)(t-1),\qquad
\ell_2(t)=-4t(t-1),\qquad
\ell_3(t)=2t\left(t-\frac12\right).
$$
Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
p(t)
&=-\ell_1(t)+\frac16\ell_2(t)+\frac89\ell_3(t)\\
&=-1+\frac{25}{9}t-\frac89t^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
Solving $p(t)=0$ gives
$$
8t^2-25t+9=0,
\qquad
t=\frac{25\pm\sqrt{337}}{16}.
$$
The root of the interpolating polynomial in the data range is
$$
t\approx 0.415.
$$
This root is chosen because the sampled values change sign between $0$
and $\frac12$; the other quadratic root lies outside the data interval.
It approximates the root of $g$ itself, which is about $0.408004$.
Interpolation is exact at the sampled values, not at a zero between sample
points.

</Example>

Interpolation is exact at the supplied inputs, but accuracy between the
inputs is a separate question and extrapolation beyond them can be poor. For
equally spaced inputs, high-degree interpolants can develop large
oscillations near the ends of the interval; this is Runge's phenomenon.
