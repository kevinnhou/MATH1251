This page applies the [matrix-power](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications) method to a discrete-time system whose state is a probability vector. A transition matrix moves probability between states, its powers give the distribution after any number of steps and its eigenvalue-$1$ mode describes a stationary distribution.

As in diagonalisation, $P$ denotes a change-of-basis matrix. The bold
$\mathbf p$ denotes a probability vector or stationary distribution, which is
a different object even when a formula uses both.

## Probability states and transitions

<Definition slug="transition-matrix" of="matrix-of-linear-map">

### Transition matrix

<Statement>

An $n\times n$ *transition matrix* for a Markov system has non-negative entries and each column sums to $1$:

$$
a_{ij}\geq0,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^n a_{ij}=1
\quad (j=1,\ldots,n).
$$

</Statement>

Column $j$ records the probabilities of moving from state $j$ to each possible next state. Some texts use the transposed convention, with these probabilities in rows.

</Definition>

<Definition slug="markov-system" of="transition-matrix">

### Markov system

<Statement>

A *Markov system* is the discrete dynamical system

$$
\mathbf{x}(k+1)=A\mathbf{x}(k),
\qquad
k=0,1,2,\ldots,
$$

where $A$ is a transition matrix and $\mathbf{x}(k)$ is the state vector at time $k$.

</Statement>

If $\mathbf{x}(0)$ is a probability vector, then $\mathbf{x}(k)$ records the state probabilities after $k$ transitions.

</Definition>

<Proposition slug="probability-vector-preservation" of="transition-matrix,markov-system">

### Probability vectors remain probability vectors

<Statement>

If $\mathbf{x}$ has non-negative entries summing to $1$ and $A$ is a transition matrix, then $A\mathbf{x}$ also has non-negative entries summing to $1$.

</Statement>

</Proposition>

<Proof of="probability-vector-preservation">

Non-negativity follows because each entry of $A\mathbf{x}$ is a sum of products of non-negative numbers. Let $\mathbf{1}$ be the all-ones vector. Since the columns of $A$ sum to $1$,

$$
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}A=\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf{x}
=\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}
=1.
$$

</Proof>

## Stationary distributions are eigenvalue-one modes

<Definition slug="stationary-distribution" of="markov-system,eigenvalue">

### Stationary distribution

<Statement>

A *stationary distribution* is a probability vector $\mathbf{p}$ satisfying

$$
A\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{p}.
$$

Thus a stationary distribution is an eigenvector for the eigenvalue $1$, scaled so that its entries sum to $1$.
Equivalently, it is a non-negative eigenvector for eigenvalue $1$ normalized
to have component sum $1$.

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="markov-unit-eigenvalue" of="transition-matrix,eigenvalue">

### The eigenvalue one

<Statement>

Every transition matrix has $1$ as an eigenvalue.

</Statement>

</Theorem>

<Proof of="markov-unit-eigenvalue">

The column-sum condition gives

$$
A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1},
$$

so $1$ is an eigenvalue of $A^{\mathsf T}$. Since

$$
\det(A^{\mathsf T}-\lambda I)
=\det\bigl((A-\lambda I)^{\mathsf T}\bigr)
=\det(A-\lambda I),
$$

$A$ and $A^{\mathsf T}$ have the same eigenvalues. Hence $1$ is an eigenvalue of $A$. The all-ones vector is generally an eigenvector of $A^{\mathsf T}$, not of $A$.

</Proof>

The eigenvalue $1$ is necessary for a stationary distribution, but this
computation alone does not produce a non-negative, normalised eigenvector or
show that the system converges to a unique limit. Those conclusions need
extra hypotheses and the theorem below gives one convenient set.

## Evolving a Markov system

<Method slug="markov-system-via-diagonalisation" of="markov-system,matrix-power-via-diagonalisation">

### Evolve a Markov system by diagonalisation

<Statement>

For

$$
\mathbf{x}(k+1)=A\mathbf{x}(k),
$$

repeated substitution gives

$$
\mathbf{x}(k)=A^k\mathbf{x}(0).
$$

If $A=PDP^{-1}$, compute this as

$$
\mathbf{x}(k)=PD^kP^{-1}\mathbf{x}(0).
$$

</Statement>

In an eigenbasis, the eigenvalue-$1$ component is unchanged, while every mode
with modulus less than $1$ decays, as in
[stability of systems](/algebra/complex-numbers/stability-of-systems). This is
the calculation from [matrix powers](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications)
with the state vector read as probabilities.

</Method>

## Long-term Markov behaviour

<Theorem slug="markov-long-term-limit" of="markov-system,markov-system-via-diagonalisation,stationary-distribution">

### A sufficient condition for long-term Markov behaviour

<Statement>

Suppose that a transition matrix $A$ satisfies:

1. $1$ is an eigenvalue with algebraic multiplicity $1$;
2. every other eigenvalue $\lambda_j$ has $|\lambda_j|<1$;
3. there is a basis of eigenvectors for $\mathbb R^n$.

Then $\mathbf{x}(k+1)=A\mathbf{x}(k)$ has a limit $\mathbf{p}$ in the
eigenspace for $\lambda=1$. It is the unique vector in that eigenspace
whose component sum equals the component sum of $\mathbf{x}(0)$; it may
be zero when that sum is zero. If $\mathbf{x}(0)$ is a probability vector,
then $\mathbf{p}$ is the stationary distribution and does not depend on
the initial probabilities.

</Statement>

</Theorem>

<Proof of="markov-long-term-limit">

Let $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ be an eigenbasis with $Av_1=v_1$ and
$Av_j=\lambda_jv_j$ for $j\ge2$. Expand the initial state as

$$
\mathbf{x}(0)=\alpha_1v_1+\cdots+\alpha_nv_n.
$$

Repeated application of $A$ gives

$$
\mathbf{x}(k)=A^k\mathbf{x}(0)
=\alpha_1v_1+\sum_{j=2}^n\alpha_j\lambda_j^kv_j.
$$

Since $|\lambda_j|<1$ for $j\ge2$, the sum tends to zero. Thus
$\mathbf{x}(k)\to\alpha_1v_1$. For $j\ge2$, the column-sum identity
$\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}A=\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}$ gives
$\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}v_j=\lambda_j\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}v_j$, hence
$\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}v_j=0$. Since the eigenvectors form a basis, $v_1$
must have non-zero component sum. The preserved initial sum therefore
determines $\alpha_1v_1$ uniquely. If the initial state is a probability
vector, every $\mathbf{x}(k)$ is one too, so its limit is non-negative and
sums to $1$.

</Proof>

These hypotheses are sufficient rather than necessary. The real eigenbasis
keeps the proof in diagonal form. Convergence can also be analysed with
complex eigenvectors or Jordan blocks, provided the other modes still have
modulus less than $1$.

<Example slug="stock-market-markov-system" of="transition-matrix,markov-system,probability-vector-preservation" difficulty="exam">

### A three-state stock market

Order the states as bull, bear and stagnant. The transition matrix is

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
0.65&0.60&0.35\\
0.10&0.05&0.40\\
0.25&0.35&0.25
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For example, the first column says that a bull market is followed by a bull market with probability $0.65$, a bear market with probability $0.10$ and a stagnant market with probability $0.25$. Each column sums to $1$ and every entry is non-negative, so this is a transition matrix.

If the market starts in the bear state, then

$$
\mathbf{x}(0)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0\\1\\0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{x}(k+1)=A\mathbf{x}(k).
$$

</Example>

<Example slug="stock-market-long-term-behaviour" of="markov-system-via-diagonalisation,markov-long-term-limit,stationary-distribution" difficulty="exam">

### The stock market in the long run

For this transition matrix, the eigenvalues and corresponding eigenvectors are

$$
\lambda_1=1,\quad
v_1=
\begin{pmatrix}229\\70\\109\end{pmatrix};
\qquad
\lambda_2=-0.2,\quad
v_2=
\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\1\end{pmatrix};
\qquad
\lambda_3=0.15,\quad
v_3=
\begin{pmatrix}-5\\3\\2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

These eigenvectors form a basis, $1$ has multiplicity $1$ and

$$
|-0.2|<1,
\qquad
|0.15|<1.
$$

For the initial bear state,

$$
\mathbf{x}(k)
=P
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&(-0.2)^k&0\\
0&0&(0.15)^k
\end{pmatrix}
P^{-1}
\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
P=\begin{pmatrix}v_1&v_2&v_3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The last two modes vanish as $k\to\infty$, leaving a multiple of $v_1$. Its entries sum to $229+70+109=408$, so the limiting probability vector is

$$
\mathbf{p}
=\frac1{408}
\begin{pmatrix}229\\70\\109\end{pmatrix}
\approx
\begin{pmatrix}
0.5613\\
0.1716\\
0.2672
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entries are non-negative and sum to $1$ and $A\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{p}$ because $Av_1=v_1$. Thus $\mathbf{p}$ is the stationary distribution: the long-run probabilities of bull, bear and stagnant states.

The other two eigenmodes are temporary departures from this distribution.
Both shrink and the negative eigenvalue makes its departure alternate in
sign. The initial bear state therefore affects the short run but is forgotten
in the long run.

</Example>
