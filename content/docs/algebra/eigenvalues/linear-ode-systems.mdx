This page solves coupled first-order equations $\mathbf{x}'=A\mathbf{x}$ by
the continuous analogue of [matrix powers](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications).
An eigenvector gives a direction whose time dependence is a single
exponential and its eigenvalue is the rate, read for growth or decay by
[stability of systems](/algebra/complex-numbers/stability-of-systems).

The page links the two strands. The Calculus ODE pages develop scalar
methods, but the exponential modes needed here are derived below, so the page
can be read first and compared with the scalar methods later.

## First-order systems

With a single equation, the system is a [first-order linear equation](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/first-order-equations), which that page solves with an integrating factor.

<Recall of="first-order-linear-equation" />

<Definition slug="first-order-linear-system" of="matrix-of-linear-map">

### First-order linear system

<Statement>

A *first-order linear system* with constant coefficient matrix $A$ has the form

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t)=A\mathbf{x}(t),
$$

where $\mathbf{x}(t)=(x_1(t),\ldots,x_n(t))^{\mathsf T}$ and $A$ is an $n\times n$ matrix.
Specifying $\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_0$ makes this an initial-value
problem.

</Statement>

Writing the equations as one matrix equation records all couplings in $A$. For example,

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_1'&=x_1+3x_2,\\
x_2'&=3x_1+x_2
\end{aligned}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\mathbf{x}'=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&3\\
3&1
\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}.
$$

</Definition>

## Eigenvectors as exponential modes

An eigenvector is a direction that $A$ does not mix with other directions,
and its eigenvalue is the rate attached to that direction. An [eigenbasis](/algebra/eigenvalues/diagonalisation) writes every initial vector as a combination of such modes.

<Recall of="diagonalisation" />

<Recall of="eigenvalue" />

<Theorem slug="exponential-mode-of-system" of="first-order-linear-system,eigenvalue,eigenvector">

### Exponential modes

<Statement>

For a constant matrix $A$ and a non-zero vector $\mathbf u$, the function

$$
\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{u}e^{\lambda t}
$$

is a solution of $\mathbf{x}'=A\mathbf{x}$ if and only if $\lambda$ is an
eigenvalue of $A$ and $\mathbf{u}$ is a corresponding eigenvector. The zero
solution is handled separately by taking $\mathbf u=\mathbf0$.

</Statement>

When $A$ is real, the calculation may take place in $\mathbb C^n$ to use
complex eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Combining a complex solution with its
conjugate then gives real solutions, as in the oscillatory example below.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="exponential-mode-of-system">

Differentiating the proposed function gives

$$
\mathbf{x}'(t)=\lambda\mathbf{u}e^{\lambda t}.
$$

The equation $\mathbf{x}'=A\mathbf{x}$ therefore becomes

$$
\lambda\mathbf{u}e^{\lambda t}
=A\mathbf{u}e^{\lambda t}.
$$

Since $e^{\lambda t}\neq0$, this is equivalent to $A\mathbf{u}=\lambda\mathbf{u}$. The reverse implication is the same calculation read backwards.

</Proof>

## Superposing independent modes

<Proposition slug="linear-combination-of-system-solutions" of="first-order-linear-system">

### Linear combinations of solutions

<Statement>

If $\mathbf{x}_1(t)$ and $\mathbf{x}_2(t)$ solve $\mathbf{x}'=A\mathbf{x}$, then every linear combination

$$
\alpha\mathbf{x}_1(t)+\beta\mathbf{x}_2(t)
$$

also solves the system.

</Statement>

Differentiation and multiplication by $A$ are both linear:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}(\alpha\mathbf{x}_1+\beta\mathbf{x}_2)
=\alpha A\mathbf{x}_1+\beta A\mathbf{x}_2
=A(\alpha\mathbf{x}_1+\beta\mathbf{x}_2).
$$

</Proposition>

<Method slug="system-solution-via-eigenvectors" of="first-order-linear-system,exponential-mode-of-system,diagonalisation">

### Solve a system by eigenmodes

<Statement>

When $A$ has an eigenbasis $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$:

1. find the eigenvalues and corresponding eigenvectors;
2. write the initial vector as $\mathbf{x}_0=c_1v_1+\cdots+c_nv_n$;
3. replace each eigenmode $v_j$ by $e^{\lambda_jt}v_j$;
4. combine the modes:
   $$
   \mathbf{x}(t)
   =c_1e^{\lambda_1t}v_1+\cdots+c_ne^{\lambda_nt}v_n.
   $$

</Statement>

This is the [matrix-power](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications) calculation with the discrete factor $\lambda_j^n$ replaced by the continuous factor $e^{\lambda_jt}$. A matrix without an eigenbasis instead produces terms such as $te^{\lambda t}$, as in the repeated-root case for scalar equations.

</Method>

<Example slug="decaying-two-by-two-system" of="system-solution-via-eigenvectors" difficulty="routine">

### A system with a limiting state

Consider

$$
\mathbf{x}'=
\begin{pmatrix}
-3&3\\
3&-3
\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x},
\qquad
\mathbf{x}(0)=
\begin{pmatrix}4\\0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The eigenvalues are $0$ and $-6$, with eigenvectors

$$
v_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Since

$$
\begin{pmatrix}4\\0\end{pmatrix}
=2\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
 +2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix},
$$

the solution is

$$
\mathbf{x}(t)
=2\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
 +2e^{-6t}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
2+2e^{-6t}\\
2-2e^{-6t}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The $e^{-6t}$ mode decays to zero, so $\mathbf{x}(t)$ tends to the
equilibrium $(2,2)^{\mathsf T}$. The solution does not tend to zero, because
the $\lambda=0$ mode stays constant.

</Example>

<Example slug="oscillating-two-by-two-system" of="system-solution-via-eigenvectors" difficulty="exam">

### Complex eigenvalues and a real solution

For

$$
\mathbf{x}'=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-2\\
2&1
\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x},
\qquad
\mathbf{x}(0)=
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},
$$

the eigenvalues are $1+2i$ and $1-2i$. Corresponding eigenvectors may be chosen as

$$
v_+=\begin{pmatrix}i\\1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v_-=\begin{pmatrix}-i\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At $t=0$, the coefficients must satisfy

$$
c_+\begin{pmatrix}i\\1\end{pmatrix}
+c_-\begin{pmatrix}-i\\1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Solving these two scalar equations gives $c_+=-i/2$ and $c_-=i/2$.
Therefore

$$
\mathbf{x}(t)
=
-\frac{i}{2}e^{(1+2i)t}\begin{pmatrix}i\\1\end{pmatrix}
 +\frac{i}{2}e^{(1-2i)t}\begin{pmatrix}-i\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Using Euler's formula $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ with
$\theta=2t$ and simplifying yields the real solution

$$
\mathbf{x}(t)
=e^t
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos(2t)\\
\sin(2t)
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Direct differentiation checks that $\mathbf{x}'=A\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x}(0)=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$.

The factor $e^t$ makes the amplitude grow, while the sine and cosine rotate
the state with angular frequency $2$. The trajectory therefore spirals
outward from the origin: the imaginary parts of the eigenvalues produce the
oscillation and their positive real part produces the instability.

</Example>

## Higher-order equations as systems

[Constant-coefficient scalar equations](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/second-order-constant-coefficient-equations) are solved with a characteristic equation and its
roots. Each such equation can also be rewritten as a first-order system.

<Example slug="higher-order-ode-as-system" of="first-order-linear-system" difficulty="routine">

### Converting a higher-order equation

The third-order equation

$$
y'''+ay''+by'+cy=0
$$

becomes a first-order system after setting

$$
x_1=y,\qquad x_2=y',\qquad x_3=y''.
$$

Then

$$
\frac{d}{dt}
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1\\x_2\\x_3
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
-c&-b&-a
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
x_1\\x_2\\x_3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The first two rows record $x_1'=x_2$ and $x_2'=x_3$, while the third row is the original equation solved for $y'''$.

</Example>

## A coupled economic model

<Example slug="reserve-bank-linear-system" of="first-order-linear-system,system-solution-via-eigenvectors" difficulty="exam">

### A two-variable economic model

Let $y_1$ be the inflation level and $y_2$ the interest-rate level. Consider
the model

$$
\frac{dy_1}{dt}=\frac12y_1-\frac15y_2,
\qquad
\frac{dy_2}{dt}=\frac35y_1-\frac25y_2,
$$

with

$$
\mathbf{y}(0)=
\begin{pmatrix}4/100\\6/100\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1/2&-1/5\\
3/5&-2/5
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The characteristic polynomial is

$$
\det(A-\lambda I)
=\lambda^2-\frac1{10}\lambda-\frac2{25},
$$

so

$$
\lambda_1=\frac{1+\sqrt{33}}{20},
\qquad
\lambda_2=\frac{1-\sqrt{33}}{20}.
$$

Eigenvectors with second component $1$ are

$$
v_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
(9+\sqrt{33})/12\\1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
(9-\sqrt{33})/12\\1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Writing the initial vector as $\mathbf{y}(0)=c_1v_1+c_2v_2$ gives

$$
c_1=\frac{33-\sqrt{33}}{1100},
\qquad
c_2=\frac{33+\sqrt{33}}{1100}.
$$

Hence

$$
\mathbf{y}(t)
=\frac{33-\sqrt{33}}{1100}
e^{(1+\sqrt{33})t/20}v_1
 +\frac{33+\sqrt{33}}{1100}
e^{(1-\sqrt{33})t/20}v_2.
$$

The first exponent is positive and the second is negative, so one eigenmode grows and the other decays.

The growing mode is the model's unstable direction: any non-zero component
of the initial state along $v_1$ eventually dominates. The decaying mode
along $v_2$ is a short-run adjustment.

</Example>

<Recall of="continuous-root-stability" />

For a mode $e^{\lambda t}$, $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)$ controls growth or
decay, as in the [continuous stability criterion](/algebra/complex-numbers/stability-of-systems)
for scalar characteristic roots. The time factor is the same exponential that
appears in [second-order constant-coefficient
equations](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/second-order-constant-coefficient-equations).
