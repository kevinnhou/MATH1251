This page uses [diagonalisation](/algebra/eigenvalues/diagonalisation) to compute matrix powers and to read the long-term behaviour of each mode. In an eigenbasis, repeated multiplication by $A$ becomes repeated scalar multiplication, so the first question is how many independent modes a repeated eigenvalue supplies.

The same separation reappears in [linear ODE systems](/algebra/eigenvalues/linear-ode-systems), with $e^{\lambda t}$ in place of $\lambda^n$ and in [Markov systems](/algebra/eigenvalues/markov-systems), with a probability interpretation.

## Multiplicities

<Recall of="diagonalisation" />

<Definition slug="algebraic-multiplicity" of="characteristic-polynomial">

### Algebraic multiplicity

<Statement>

The *algebraic multiplicity* of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is its multiplicity as a root of the characteristic polynomial.

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Definition slug="geometric-multiplicity" of="eigenspace">

### Geometric multiplicity

<Statement>

The *geometric multiplicity* of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is the dimension of its eigenspace $E_\lambda$.

</Statement>

The geometric multiplicity counts the independent eigenvectors available for that eigenvalue and hence how many it can contribute to an eigenbasis.

</Definition>

These two counts explain the [failed diagonalisation
example](/algebra/eigenvalues/diagonalisation#failed-diagonalisation-example):
an eigenvalue can repeat in the characteristic polynomial while its
eigenspace supplies too few independent eigenvectors.

<Theorem slug="geometric-multiplicity-bound" of="algebraic-multiplicity,geometric-multiplicity">

### Comparing the multiplicities

<Statement>

For every eigenvalue,

$$
\text{geometric multiplicity}
\leq
\text{algebraic multiplicity}.
$$

</Statement>

</Theorem>

<Proof of="geometric-multiplicity-bound">

Let $g=\dim E_\lambda$ and begin a basis of the whole space with a basis
of $E_\lambda$. In these coordinates, the first $g$ columns of $A$ have
$\lambda I_g$ in their first $g$ entries and zeros below. Thus
$A-tI$ is block upper triangular, with $\lambda-t$ on each of those
$g$ diagonal positions. Its determinant has the factor
$(\lambda-t)^g$, so the algebraic multiplicity is at least $g$.

</Proof>

<Example slug="repeated-eigenvalue-multiplicities" of="algebraic-multiplicity,geometric-multiplicity" difficulty="routine">

### Three repeated-eigenvalue cases

Consider the three matrices

$$
A_1=
\begin{pmatrix}
3&2&0\\
0&3&2\\
0&0&3
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A_2=
\begin{pmatrix}
3&0&0\\
0&3&2\\
0&0&3
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A_3=3I.
$$

Each has characteristic polynomial $(3-\lambda)^3$, so the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda=3$ is $3$.

For $A_1$,

$$
A_1-3I=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&2&0\\
0&0&2\\
0&0&0
\end{pmatrix},
$$

so $x_2=x_3=0$ and

$$
E_3=\operatorname{span}\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
\right\}.
$$

The geometric multiplicity is $1$.

For $A_2$,

$$
A_2-3I=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&0\\
0&0&2\\
0&0&0
\end{pmatrix},
$$

so $x_3=0$ and

$$
E_3=\operatorname{span}\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}
\right\}.
$$

The geometric multiplicity is $2$. Finally, $A_3-3I=0$, so the eigenspace is $\mathbb R^3$ and the geometric multiplicity is $3$. Only the last case has three independent eigenvectors and hence an eigenbasis.

</Example>

## Matrix powers

<Proposition slug="diagonal-matrix-powers" of="diagonalisation">

### Powers of a diagonal matrix

<Statement>

If

$$
D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m),
$$

then, for every non-negative integer $k$,

$$
D^k=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k,\ldots,\lambda_m^k).
$$

</Statement>

The matrix stays diagonal because each coordinate direction is acted on independently.

</Proposition>

<Proof of="diagonal-matrix-powers">

For $k=0$, both sides equal $I$. If the formula holds for $k$, then

$$
D^{k+1}=D^kD
=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m^k\lambda_m)
=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^{k+1},\ldots,\lambda_m^{k+1}).
$$

Induction proves the result.

</Proof>

<Theorem slug="diagonalised-matrix-powers" of="diagonalisation,diagonal-matrix-powers">

### Powers after diagonalisation

<Statement>

If

$$
A=PDP^{-1},
$$

then, for every non-negative integer $k$,

$$
A^k=PD^kP^{-1}.
$$

</Statement>

</Theorem>

<Proof of="diagonalised-matrix-powers">

For $k=0$, both sides are $I$. If $A^k=PD^kP^{-1}$, then

$$
A^{k+1}
=A^kA
=PD^kP^{-1}PDP^{-1}
=PD^{k+1}P^{-1}.
$$

</Proof>

<Method slug="matrix-power-via-diagonalisation" of="diagonalisation,diagonalised-matrix-powers">

### Compute a matrix power by diagonalisation

<Statement>

To compute $A^k x_0$ for a non-negative integer $k$ when $A$ is diagonalisable:

1. choose an eigenbasis and form $P$ from its eigenvectors;
2. form the diagonal matrix $D$ from the corresponding eigenvalues;
3. write $A=PDP^{-1}$;
4. replace $A^k$ by $PD^kP^{-1}$, so the powers are taken only on the diagonal;
5. apply the result to $x_0$.

</Statement>

Equivalently, if $x_0=c_1v_1+\cdots+c_nv_n$ in the eigenbasis, then

$$
A^kx_0
=c_1\lambda_1^kv_1+\cdots+c_n\lambda_n^kv_n.
$$

This form shows each mode evolving on its own.

</Method>

## Long-term behaviour

<Theorem slug="spectral-mode-behaviour" of="matrix-power-via-diagonalisation">

### Long-term behaviour of the modes

<Statement>

Suppose $A=PDP^{-1}$ is diagonalisable, so it has an eigenbasis. A mode
contribution $c\lambda^kv$ with $c\ne0$ decays to zero when $|\lambda|<1$,
has constant magnitude when $|\lambda|=1$ and grows when $|\lambda|>1$.
Without an eigenbasis, a Jordan block can produce additional factors such as
$k\lambda^{k-1}$, so the diagonalisation hypothesis is what makes this scalar
classification complete (especially at the unit circle).

</Statement>

This is the modulus test for the modes of a scalar recurrence from [stability of systems](/algebra/complex-numbers/stability-of-systems). [Diagonalisation](/algebra/eigenvalues/diagonalisation) reduces a matrix recurrence to such scalar modes.

</Theorem>

<Example slug="power-of-ones-matrix" of="matrix-power-via-diagonalisation,spectral-mode-behaviour" difficulty="routine">

### Powers of the all-ones matrix

For

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix},
$$

use the eigenvectors computed for the [all-ones matrix](/algebra/eigenvalues/eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors):

$$
P=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&-1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&0\\
0&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
P^{-1}
=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&-1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Therefore, for $n\geq1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^n
&=PD^nP^{-1}\\
&=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&-1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
2^n&0\\
0&0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&-1
\end{pmatrix}\\
&=2^{n-1}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For $x_0=(a,b)^{\mathsf T}$,

$$
A^nx_0
=2^{n-1}(a+b)
\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The component in the $0$-eigenspace disappears after one step, while the component in the $2$-eigenspace grows like $2^n$.

</Example>

<Example slug="three-by-three-power" of="matrix-power-via-diagonalisation" difficulty="exam">

### A three-by-three power

Let

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
3&1&1\\
0&2&0\\
0&0&2
\end{pmatrix},
$$

and take the eigenvectors

$$
v_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v_2=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v_3=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},
$$

for eigenvalues $3,2,2$, respectively. Then

$$
P=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1&-1\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D=
\begin{pmatrix}
3&0&0\\
0&2&0\\
0&0&2
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
P^{-1}=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1&1\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The multiplication check gives $A=PDP^{-1}$ and hence

$$
A^n=P
\begin{pmatrix}
3^n&0&0\\
0&2^n&0\\
0&0&2^n
\end{pmatrix}
P^{-1}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
3^n&3^n-2^n&3^n-2^n\\
0&2^n&0\\
0&0&2^n
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The eigenvalue $2$ is repeated but has two independent eigenvectors, $v_2$
and $v_3$. Together with $v_1$ they form an eigenbasis, so the diagonal power
calculation is valid.

</Example>
