This page defines eigenvalues, eigenvectors and eigenspaces and gives the determinant test for finding them. A [matrix representation](/algebra/linear-transformations/matrix-representations) depends on the chosen [basis](/algebra/vector-spaces/basis-and-dimension) and the most useful basis vectors are those that the map sends to scalar multiples of themselves.

Enough such vectors form an [eigenbasis](/algebra/eigenvalues/diagonalisation). That basis separates [matrix powers](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications), [linear ODE systems](/algebra/eigenvalues/linear-ode-systems) and [Markov systems](/algebra/eigenvalues/markov-systems) into scalar modes.

<Definition slug="eigenvalue" of="linear-map">

### Eigenvalue

<Statement>

Let $T:V\to V$ be a linear map over a field $F$. A scalar
$\lambda\in F$ is an *eigenvalue* of $T$ if there is a non-zero vector
$v\in V$ such that

$$
T(v)=\lambda v.
$$

</Statement>

The equation says that the line through $v$ is mapped into itself, with
every vector on that line scaled by $\lambda$. A negative real $\lambda$
reverses direction and $\lambda=0$ collapses the line to the origin. For
a matrix $A$, the same condition is

$$
Av=\lambda v.
$$

</Definition>

<Definition slug="eigenvector" of="eigenvalue">

### Eigenvector

<Statement>

If $T(v)=\lambda v$ for a non-zero vector $v$, then $v$ is an *eigenvector* of $T$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda$.

</Statement>

The zero vector is excluded because $T(0)=\lambda 0$ holds for every scalar, so it says nothing about a direction. Any non-zero scalar multiple of an eigenvector is another eigenvector for the same eigenvalue.

</Definition>

<Example of="eigenvalue,eigenvector" difficulty="routine">

### An eigenvector of differentiation

Let $V=C^\infty(\mathbb R,\mathbb R)$, the vector space of smooth real-valued
functions and let $T:V\to V$ be differentiation, $T(f)=f'$. For any real
$\lambda$, the function

$$
f(x)=e^{\lambda x}
$$

satisfies

$$
T(f)=f'=\lambda e^{\lambda x}=\lambda f.
$$

Thus $e^{\lambda x}$ is an eigenvector of differentiation for the eigenvalue $\lambda$. For a matrix, the same test reads $Av=\lambda v$, which is the form used throughout [diagonalisation](/algebra/eigenvalues/diagonalisation).

</Example>

<Definition slug="eigenspace" of="eigenvalue,eigenvector,kernel-of-linear-map">

### Eigenspace

<Statement>

For $A\in M_n(F)$ and an eigenvalue $\lambda\in F$, the *eigenspace*
corresponding to $\lambda$ is

$$
E_\lambda=\ker_F(A-\lambda I).
$$

Its non-zero vectors are precisely the eigenvectors of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda$.

</Statement>

To find the eigenspace, solve the homogeneous system

$$
(A-\lambda I)v=0.
$$

The [kernel](/algebra/linear-transformations/kernel-image-rank) description makes the eigenspace a subspace, so it contains the zero vector even though the zero vector is not an eigenvector.

</Definition>

## The characteristic polynomial

<Definition slug="characteristic-polynomial" of="matrix-of-linear-map" see="characteristic-equation">

### Characteristic polynomial

<Statement>

For an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ over $F$, the *characteristic polynomial* is

$$
p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I).
$$

The equation $p_A(\lambda)=0$ is the associated characteristic equation.

</Statement>

The characteristic polynomial turns the search for a non-zero solution of
$(A-\lambda I)v=0$ into root-finding over the chosen field. A polynomial with
no roots in $F$ may gain roots when the scalars are extended, for example
from $\mathbb R$ to $\mathbb C$.

Some texts define the polynomial as $\det(\lambda I-A)$ instead. For an
$n\times n$ matrix this is $(-1)^n p_A(\lambda)$, so both conventions give
the same eigenvalues.

Two names clash with Calculus. The [characteristic equation of a scalar
ODE](/calculus/ordinary-differential-equations/second-order-constant-coefficient-equations)
is a different object with the same name. In [constrained
extrema](/calculus/functions-of-several-variables/constrained-extrema), the
letter $\lambda$ denotes a Lagrange multiplier rather than an eigenvalue.

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="eigenvalue-root-criterion" of="eigenvalue,eigenvector,eigenspace,characteristic-polynomial">

### The eigenvalue criterion

<Statement>

A scalar $\lambda\in F$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if and only if

$$
p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)=0.
$$

For such a $\lambda$, the eigenvectors are the non-zero vectors in $\ker(A-\lambda I)$.

</Statement>

This determinant test is how eigenpairs are computed. [Diagonalisation](/algebra/eigenvalues/diagonalisation) then uses the full list of roots to build an eigenbasis.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="eigenvalue-root-criterion">

The equation $Av=\lambda v$ is equivalent to $(A-\lambda I)v=0$. A non-zero solution exists exactly when $A-\lambda I$ is singular and a square matrix is singular exactly when its determinant is zero. The description of the eigenvectors then follows from the definition of the kernel.

</Proof>

<Theorem slug="matrix-eigenvalue-count" of="characteristic-polynomial" see="fundamental-theorem-of-algebra">

### Counting eigenvalues

<Statement>

The characteristic polynomial of an $n\times n$ matrix is degree $n$. Over $\mathbb C$, it has $n$ roots counted with multiplicity and these roots are the eigenvalues of the matrix.

</Statement>

The roots exist by the
[fundamental theorem of algebra](/algebra/complex-numbers/complex-polynomials).
Their moduli and real parts later govern growth in [stability of systems](/algebra/complex-numbers/stability-of-systems).

</Theorem>

## Computing eigenpairs

<Proposition slug="triangular-matrix-eigenvalues" of="characteristic-polynomial">

### Eigenvalues of a triangular matrix

<Statement>

For a triangular matrix, the eigenvalues are its diagonal entries, counted with their multiplicities.

</Statement>

The matrix $A-\lambda I$ is still triangular, so its determinant is the product of its diagonal entries and no full expansion is needed.

</Proposition>

<Example slug="eigenvalues-of-ones-matrix" of="eigenvalue-root-criterion,eigenspace" difficulty="routine">

### The matrix with all entries equal to one

Let

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The characteristic polynomial is

$$
p_A(\lambda)
=\det\begin{pmatrix}1-\lambda&1\\1&1-\lambda\end{pmatrix}
=(1-\lambda)^2-1
=\lambda(\lambda-2).
$$

Thus the eigenvalues are $0$ and $2$. For $\lambda=0$,

$$
A v=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v=\alpha\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix},
$$

so

$$
E_0=\operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\right\}.
$$

For $\lambda=2$,

$$
(A-2I)v=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v=\alpha\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},
$$

so

$$
E_2=\operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\right\}.
$$

The two eigenspaces are perpendicular lines: $A$ doubles the component
parallel to $(1,1)^{\mathsf T}$ and removes the component parallel to
$(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}$. The roots $0$ and $2$ alone do not show this. The
eigenspaces are what describe the action of the matrix and supply an
eigenbasis.

</Example>
