This page asks when a matrix has enough independent eigenvectors to form an eigenbasis. In that basis the [matrix representation](/algebra/linear-transformations/matrix-representations) is diagonal, so repeated multiplication or coupled evolution splits into scalar modes, as in [matrix powers](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications), [linear ODE systems](/algebra/eigenvalues/linear-ode-systems) and [Markov systems](/algebra/eigenvalues/markov-systems).

Diagonalisation always takes place over a chosen scalar field: the
change-of-basis matrix and the diagonal entries must belong to that field.
A real matrix may therefore be diagonalisable over $\mathbb C$ but not over
$\mathbb R$.

<Theorem slug="distinct-eigenvalues-independent" of="eigenvalue,eigenvector,linear-independence">

### Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues

<Statement>

Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. In particular, an $n\times n$ matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors.

</Statement>

</Theorem>

<Proof of="distinct-eigenvalues-independent">

Let $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ be eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ and suppose

$$
c_1v_1+\cdots+c_nv_n=0.
$$

To isolate one coefficient, fix $k$ and apply

$$
B_k=\prod_{j\neq k}(A-\lambda_jI)
$$

to both sides. The factors commute because each is a polynomial in $A$.
Every term except the $k$th is killed by one factor, while

$$
B_kv_k=\prod_{j\neq k}(\lambda_k-\lambda_j)v_k.
$$

Thus the transformed relation is

$$
0=c_k\left(\prod_{j\neq k}(\lambda_k-\lambda_j)\right)v_k.
$$

The scalar product is non-zero and $v_k\ne0$, so $c_k=0$. Since $k$ was
arbitrary, all coefficients vanish.

</Proof>

<Definition slug="eigenbasis" of="eigenvector,basis">

### Eigenbasis

<Statement>

An *eigenbasis* for a vector space is a basis whose vectors are eigenvectors of the linear map under consideration.

</Statement>

In an eigenbasis, each coordinate direction is scaled independently. Every vector therefore splits into eigenmodes, which [matrix powers](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications) and [linear ODE systems](/algebra/eigenvalues/linear-ode-systems) evolve separately.

</Definition>

<Definition slug="diagonalisation" of="eigenbasis,matrix-of-linear-map">

### Diagonalisation

<Statement>

An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ over a field $F$ is *diagonalisable over $F$*
if there are an invertible matrix $P\in M_n(F)$ and a diagonal matrix
$D\in M_n(F)$ such that

$$
P^{-1}AP=D,
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
A=PDP^{-1}.
$$

The columns of $P$ are the eigenvectors in the chosen eigenbasis and the corresponding eigenvalues appear on the diagonal of $D$.

</Statement>

</Definition>

<Theorem slug="diagonalisation-criterion" of="diagonalisation,eigenbasis,linear-independence">

### When diagonalisation is possible

<Statement>

An $n\times n$ matrix over $F$ is diagonalisable over $F$ if and only if it
has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors in $F^n$.

</Statement>

This test decides whether [matrix powers](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications) can be computed in diagonal form.

</Theorem>

<Proof of="diagonalisation-criterion">

If $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ are independent eigenvectors, let

$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}v_1&\cdots&v_n\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n).
$$

The matrix $P$ is invertible and applying $A$ to each column gives $AP=PD$. Hence $P^{-1}AP=D$.

Conversely, if $P^{-1}AP=D$, then $AP=PD$. The columns of $P$ are therefore eigenvectors corresponding to the diagonal entries of $D$. Since $P$ is invertible, its columns are linearly independent.

</Proof>

## Similarity and change of basis

Two square matrices $A$ and $B$ are *similar* if an invertible matrix $P$
satisfies

$$
B=P^{-1}AP.
$$

Similar matrices represent the same linear operator in different bases, as
in a [matrix representation](/algebra/linear-transformations/matrix-representations),
so their entries can differ. They share eigenvalues, determinant, rank,
nullity and invertibility. Diagonalisation $A=PDP^{-1}$ is the case in which
the matrix in the new basis is diagonal.

## Worked examples

<Example slug="complex-eigenbasis-example" of="eigenvalue,eigenvector,diagonalisation" difficulty="routine">

### A real matrix with complex eigenvalues

For

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&-1\\
1&2
\end{pmatrix},
$$

the characteristic polynomial is

$$
p_A(\lambda)=(2-\lambda)^2+1
=\lambda^2-4\lambda+5.
$$

Its eigenvalues are $\lambda=2+i$ and $\lambda=2-i$. For $\lambda=2+i$,

$$
A-(2+i)I=
\begin{pmatrix}
-i&-1\\
1&-i
\end{pmatrix},
$$

and solving $(A-(2+i)I)v=0$ gives

$$
v=\alpha\begin{pmatrix}i\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The conjugate eigenvalue has eigenvector $\begin{pmatrix}-i\\1\end{pmatrix}$. These two eigenvectors are independent over $\mathbb C$, so the matrix is diagonalisable over $\mathbb C$ although it has no real eigenvalues. Discarding the [complex roots](/algebra/complex-numbers/stability-of-systems) of a real matrix would miss this diagonalisation.

</Example>

A repeated eigenvalue needs more care. Its [algebraic
multiplicity](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications) counts how often it
repeats as a root of the characteristic polynomial. Its geometric
multiplicity is the dimension of its eigenspace, which is never larger.

A repeated root is not itself an obstruction to diagonalisation over $F$. The
obstruction is an eigenspace that is too small, leaving fewer than $n$
independent eigenvectors in $F^n$. The [three repeated-eigenvalue
cases](/algebra/eigenvalues/spectral-applications#repeated-eigenvalue-multiplicities)
compare the possibilities.

<Example slug="failed-diagonalisation-example" of="eigenspace,diagonalisation" difficulty="routine">

### A repeated eigenvalue without enough modes

Let

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
3&-1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then

$$
p_A(\lambda)
=(3-\lambda)(1-\lambda)+1
=(\lambda-2)^2.
$$

The only eigenvalue is $2$. Solving

$$
(A-2I)v=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-1\\
1&-1
\end{pmatrix}v=0
$$

gives

$$
E_2=\operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\right\}.
$$

The algebraic multiplicity is $2$, but the geometric multiplicity is
$\dim(E_2)=1$. There is only one independent eigenvector, so no eigenbasis of
$\mathbb R^2$ exists and $A$ is not diagonalisable over either $\mathbb R$ or
$\mathbb C$.

</Example>

<Example slug="triangular-diagonalisation-example" of="triangular-matrix-eigenvalues,distinct-eigenvalues-independent,diagonalisation" difficulty="routine">

### A triangular matrix with an eigenbasis

For

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&2&3\\
0&4&5\\
0&0&6
\end{pmatrix},
$$

the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries $1$, $4$ and $6$. They are
distinct, so the corresponding eigenvectors are independent. Solving
$(A-\lambda I)v=0$ gives $v_1=(1,0,0)^{\mathsf T}$ for $\lambda=1$.
For $\lambda=4$, the last two equations give $z=0$ and the first gives
$3x=2y$, so choose $v_2=(2,3,0)^{\mathsf T}$. For $\lambda=6$, the
second equation gives $2y=5z$; taking $z=10$ gives $y=25$ and the first
equation then gives $x=16$. Thus one choice is

$$
v_1=
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v_2=
\begin{pmatrix}2\\3\\0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v_3=
\begin{pmatrix}16\\25\\10\end{pmatrix}.
$$

With

$$
P=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&2&16\\
0&3&25\\
0&0&10
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&4&0\\
0&0&6
\end{pmatrix},
$$

the column checks $Av_j=\lambda_jv_j$ give $AP=PD$ and hence

$$
A=PDP^{-1}.
$$

</Example>

## Symmetric matrices

Real symmetric matrices are especially well behaved: their eigenvalues are
real and their eigenvectors can be chosen mutually perpendicular. This is the
spectral theorem, stated here without proof. The orthonormal form makes the
change of basis easy to invert, because the inverse of $Q$ is its transpose.

<Proposition slug="real-symmetric-diagonalisation" of="diagonalisation,eigenbasis" see="orthonormal-basis">

### Real symmetric matrices

<Statement>

If $A$ is a real symmetric matrix, then its eigenvalues are real, its eigenvectors can be chosen as an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb R^n$ and

$$
Q^{\mathsf T}AQ=D
$$

for an orthogonal matrix $Q$ and a diagonal matrix $D$.

</Statement>

</Proposition>

<Example slug="orthonormal-eigenbasis-example" of="real-symmetric-diagonalisation,diagonalisation" difficulty="routine">

### Orthonormal eigenvectors

For

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1\\
1&1
\end{pmatrix},
$$

the eigenvectors

$$
u_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
u_2=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\end{pmatrix}
$$

are perpendicular and have length $\sqrt2$. Normalising them gives

$$
\widehat u_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\widehat u_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}-1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus $Q=(\widehat u_1\ \widehat u_2)$ is orthogonal and

$$
Q^{\mathsf T}AQ=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&0\\
0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

</Example>

## Invertibility and the zero eigenvalue

<Theorem slug="invertibility-eigenvalue-criterion" of="eigenvalue,characteristic-polynomial">

### Invertibility and eigenvalues

<Statement>

An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is invertible if and only if $0$ is not an eigenvalue of $A$.

</Statement>

The number $0$ is an eigenvalue exactly when $A$ has a non-zero vector in its [kernel](/algebra/linear-transformations/kernel-image-rank), which is exactly when $A$ is not invertible.

</Theorem>
